Acids and alkalis · Process

Neutralisation

Start here

Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide are mixed in just the right amounts. Universal indicator turns green, which is pH 7. Then all the water is boiled off in a dish.

If you had to guess, what is left in the dish: nothing at all, or something solid?

A salt is left. The acid and alkali reacted to make a salt and water.

Right. Sodium chloride crystals are left. The salt was dissolved in the water.

Key words

  • The reaction between an acid and a base. With an alkali, it makes a salt and water.

  • The compound made when a metal takes the place of the hydrogen in an acid.

  • A medicine that neutralises extra acid in the stomach.

  • Rain made more acidic by sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels.

Key knowledge

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base.

An alkali is a base that dissolves in water.

The acid and the alkali cancel each other out. The pH moves towards 7.

Nothing is destroyed. The atoms are rearranged into a salt and water.

The mass after the reaction is the same as the mass before.

An acid and an alkali always make a salt and water.

The salt stays dissolved in the water.

Evaporate the water and the salt is left as crystals.

The first part of the salt's name comes from the metal in the alkali.

The second part comes from the acid.

Hydrochloric acid makes chlorides. Sulfuric acid makes sulfates. Nitric acid makes nitrates.

Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide

Nitric acid + sodium hydroxide
Hydrochloric acid + potassium hydroxide

Add alkali to an acid one drop at a time, with universal indicator in it.

At first the pH rises very slowly. The acid uses up each drop of alkali.

When the acid runs out, the pH jumps quickly past 7.

After that, extra alkali is left over. The solution is alkaline.

The pH is exactly 7 only if the amounts of acid and alkali match.

  • Measure some dilute acid into a beaker.
  • Add the alkali a little at a time. Stir after each addition.
  • Test a drop with universal indicator paper each time.
  • Stop when the pH is 7.
  • Heat the solution in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form.
  • Leave it to cool. Crystals of the salt form.

Your stomach makes hydrochloric acid. It has a pH of about 2.

  • It kills most bacteria in the food you eat.
  • It gives the right pH for the enzymes that digest protein.

A layer of mucus stops the acid harming the stomach wall.

Indigestion is pain or discomfort in the stomach after eating.

It can happen when the stomach makes too much acid.

Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest.

It happens when stomach acid rises into the oesophagus (food pipe).

Antacid tablets contain substances that neutralise acid, such as magnesium hydroxide.

They neutralise the extra acid, so the pain eases.

Hydrochloric acid + magnesium hydroxide makes magnesium chloride + water.

  • Farmers spread lime on soil that is too acidic for their crops.
  • Factories neutralise waste acid before it goes into rivers.
  • Lime is added to lakes that have become too acidic.

Too much lime would make the soil or water alkaline. That also harms living things.

So the pH is tested, and lime is added a little at a time.

Clean rain is weakly acidic, about pH 5.6. Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it.

Burning fossil fuels such as coal releases sulfur dioxide.

Car engines release nitrogen oxides.

These gases dissolve in water in the clouds and make acids.

The rain that falls is more acidic than clean rain. This is acid rain.

  • Power stations spray their waste gases with powdered limestone and water.
  • This neutralises and removes the sulfur dioxide.
  • Catalytic converters in cars reduce the nitrogen oxides released.
  • Burning less fossil fuel makes less of both gases.
  • Lime is added to acidic lakes and soil to neutralise the acid.

Chemists use neutralisation to find how much acid is in a solution.

They add alkali from a burette until one drop changes the indicator's colour.

The volume of alkali used shows how much acid there was.

This method is called titration.

Limestone reacts with the sulfur dioxide in a power station's waste gases.

This makes calcium sulfate.

Calcium sulfate is used to make plasterboard for the walls of houses.
