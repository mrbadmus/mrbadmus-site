Gas pressure

What is actually pushing on the inside of a balloon?

Start here

The marshmallow in the vacuum jar

You already know gas particles are far apart and moving fast. Here is what that lets them do. A marshmallow sits in a sealed jar. Air is pumped out of the jar and the marshmallow swells up. Air is let back in and it shrivels.

Nothing was added to the marshmallow. So why did it grow?

Your turn

Commit first. What causes the pressure inside a blown-up balloon?

The particles hitting the wall. Gas particles are so far apart that they hardly ever touch each other at all.

Gas particles move fast in every direction. Each time one hits a wall it gives that wall a tiny push. There are so many hits each second that they add up to one steady push. That push is gas pressure. So the marshmallow has gas pushing out from inside. It has air pushing in from outside. Pump the outside air away and the inside push has nothing left to balance it. So the marshmallow swells.

!

Think again

“Gas pressure is the particles pushing against each other.”

The wrong idea, out loud: 'the particles are squashed up against each other, and that squashing is what pushes.' Predict before you run it — spread the same particles out into a bigger box. What happens to the pressure?

Make your prediction first — then the lab runs.

Every wall hit adds one to the counter. Watch the counter and the pressure gauge move together.

The pressure falls. The particles never touched each other in either box, so crowding cannot be the cause — and the counter and the gauge move together, which tells you what is. Pressure is wall hits per second, and nothing else.

Words to know

Say the meaning out loud before you tap the card.

Your turn

Now change something else instead. A sealed can of air is heated and the pressure inside rises. Why?

They move faster — so they hit the walls more often, and harder.
!

Think again

“Heating a gas makes the particles themselves get bigger, which is why the pressure rises.”

Name it: 'heating makes the particles themselves get bigger.' Now test it. If every particle really had grown, what would happen to the mass of gas in the sealed can?

The mass would have to go up — more stuff means more mass — but a sealed can cannot gain a single particle, and a balance proves it does not. So the particles did not grow. Only their speed changed.

Investigate

Here is the full instrument. Predict first: what happens to the pressure if you squash the gas into half the space?

Make your prediction first — then the lab runs.

Each wall hit is one push. More hits per second means more pressure. Change one control at a time.

Squashing the gas into half the space doubles how often the particles hit the walls, so the pressure doubles. Now try the temperature and the particle count the same way — predict first, one control at a time.

Key note

Gas pressure is billions of particles hitting the walls every second. More particles, less space, or higher temperature all mean more collisions — and more pressure. The particles never change size.

Going further

Heating does two jobs at once. Most answers only mention one of them. Faster particles reach the walls more often. They also arrive harder, because they are moving faster when they land. Both of those raise the pressure. And not one extra particle was added to do it.
