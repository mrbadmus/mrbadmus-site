Gas pressure

Start here

An aerosol can has stopped spraying. When you shake it, it sounds empty.

If you had to guess, is that can really empty inside?

It is still full of gas. That is why it must never go on a fire.

It is still full of gas. That is why it must never go on a fire.

Key words

  • How much force pushes on each square metre of a surface.

  • When a moving particle hits something and bounces off.

  • A measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

Key knowledge

Gas particles move fast, in random directions.

They keep colliding with the walls of their container.

Each collision pushes on the wall. Together, these pushes are the gas pressure.

Gas pressure pushes on every wall, the top and sides as well as the bottom.

Particles also bump into each other. Those bumps do not push on the walls.

So they do not add to the pressure.

Changing the pressure

RowWhat the particles doPressure
Heat the gas in the same container
Make the container smaller
Add more gas
Cool the gas
Let half the gas out

At the same temperature, the particles move no faster. The walls are closer, so they get there sooner.

As a tyre is pumped up, more particles hit its walls each second.

A tyre warms up as a car is driven along.

When the tyre cools down, its pressure falls back.

A little water is boiled in a can, so steam fills it.

The can is turned upside down into cold water.

The steam condenses into a few drops of water.

Now very few particles inside are hitting the walls.

The air outside now pushes much harder than the gas inside. It crushes the can.

  • Wrong: Gas pressure is particles pushing against each other.

    Right: Gas pressure comes from particles hitting the walls.

  • Wrong: Heating a gas makes its particles swell.

    Right: They move faster. Their size never changes.

  • Wrong: The air in a pumped-up tyre is packed solid, its particles touching.

    Right: They are closer than outside, but still far apart. It is still a gas.

Key fact

Gas pressure is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of their container. More collisions each second, or harder ones, mean a higher pressure.
