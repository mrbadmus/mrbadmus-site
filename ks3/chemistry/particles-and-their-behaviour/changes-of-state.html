When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate more and more.

At the melting point they break free of their fixed positions. The solid melts.

When a liquid is heated, its particles move faster.

At the boiling point they escape from each other and spread far apart.

When a gas cools, its particles slow down and come together. It condenses.

When a liquid cools, its particles slow down and settle into fixed positions. It freezes.

Melting, evaporating, boiling and sublimation take in energy.

Freezing and condensing give energy out.

Freezing water gives out the same energy that melting the ice took in.

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

That is the energy they have because they are moving.

While ice melts, the temperature stays at 0 °C, though heating goes on.

The energy is overcoming the forces that hold the particles in place.

It is not making them move faster.

The same happens at 100 °C, while the water boils.

Boiling separates the particles completely. Melting only frees them from fixed positions.

For water, boiling takes about seven times as much energy as melting.

Below its melting point, a substance is a solid.

Between its melting point and boiling point, it is a liquid.

Above its boiling point, it is a gas.

A substance freezes at the same temperature as it melts.

The particles in a liquid move at different speeds.

Some fast particles at the surface can escape. This is evaporation.

It happens at any temperature, but only from the surface.

The fastest particles leave, so the liquid left behind cools down.

Boiling happens all through the liquid, at one temperature, its boiling point.

The bubbles in boiling water are water as a gas.

They are not air. A pan would soon run out of air.

The tiny early bubbles, long before boiling, are dissolved air.

The white cloud above a kettle is not steam.

It is tiny drops of water. The steam has condensed in the cooler air.

Right at the spout there is a clear gap. That is the steam.

In every change of state, the mass stays the same.

No particles are made or destroyed. They are only rearranged.

Changes of state can be reversed. Cool the water and you get the ice back.

Melting needs only one substance, and energy from heating.

Dissolving needs two substances, such as sugar and water. It works in cold water.

When sugar dissolves, its particles spread out between the water particles.

No sugar is lost. 5 g of sugar in 100 g of water makes 105 g of solution.

Evaporate the water and you get the sugar back.

Examples

Melt some stearic acid in a test tube, standing in hot water.

Let it cool. Take its temperature every 30 seconds.

Plot temperature against time.

The line goes flat while the stearic acid freezes.

That temperature, about 69 °C, is its melting point.

Hazard: hot liquid. Risk: burns. Control: let it cool before touching.

  • Wrong: Some mass is lost when ice melts.

    Right: The mass stays the same. No particles are lost.

  • Wrong: Melting and dissolving are the same thing.

    Right: They are easy to mix up, but different. Melting turns a solid to liquid by heating. Dissolving spreads a solid through a liquid, like sugar in tea.

  • Wrong: The bubbles in boiling water are air.

    Right: They are water as a gas.

  • Wrong: Water only turns to gas at 100 °C.

    Right: It evaporates from its surface at any temperature.

Key fact

Changes of state are physical changes. They can be reversed, and the mass stays the same. While a pure substance melts or boils, its temperature does not change.
