Particles and their behaviour · Model

The particle model

Start here

Mix 50 ml of water with 50 ml of alcohol. The total is about 97 ml.

If you had to guess, where did the missing 3 ml go?

Nothing is lost. The small particles of one liquid fit into gaps between the other's.

Key words

  • A tiny piece of matter, far too small to see.

  • Anything that has mass and takes up space.

  • A simple picture that helps explain something we cannot see.

Key knowledge

The particle model says three things about all matter.

  • It is made of tiny particles, far too small to see.
  • There is empty space between the particles.
  • The particles are always moving.

The particles can be atoms or molecules. You meet these in the next unit.

Alcohol particles are bigger than water particles.

If the gaps were already full, 50 ml and 50 ml would make 100 ml.

Evidence for the model

RowWhat it shows
Water and alcohol mix to less than 100 ml
A gas can be squashed into a smaller space
A smell spreads across a still room

The gaps between particles hold nothing at all.

Air in the gaps would itself be particles, with gaps of its own.

So each gap is empty space, far too small to see.

Squashing a gas makes these empty spaces smaller. The particles stay the same.

Cut a sugar cube so each piece is half as wide as the last.

After about 24 of these halvings, the piece is one sugar particle.

Near the end, the piece is only a few particles across.

A sugar particle can be broken up, but only by a chemical change.

What comes out is new substances. It is no longer sugar.

So one particle is the smallest possible piece of sugar.

  • Wrong: The gaps between particles are full of air.

    Right: Air is made of particles too. The gaps hold nothing.

  • Wrong: A sharper knife could cut sugar smaller forever.

    Right: One particle is the smallest piece of sugar.

  • Wrong: Matter is all one piece, with no gaps in it.

    Right: Matter is made of separate particles with empty space between them.

Key fact

All matter is made of particles, far too small to see. There is empty space between the particles, and they are always moving.

Around 400 BC, Democritus argued that cutting must stop somewhere.

He called the last piece atomos, which means uncuttable.

He had no evidence. Measurements came over two thousand years later.

A sugar particle is about a millionth of a millimetre across.

A human hair is about a hundred thousand times wider.
