Energy changes in reactions · Investigation

Measuring a temperature change

Start here

Four groups mix the same acid and alkali, in the same amounts, in one room. They report temperature rises of 5 °C, 7 °C, 2 °C and 7 °C.

If you had to guess, what mainly caused the different answers?

Right. Each group lost a different amount of heat. Their cups and timing differed.

Thermometers can be a little off, but not by five degrees. The groups lost different amounts of heat.

Key words

  • The final temperature minus the start temperature.

  • A material that heat passes through slowly, such as polystyrene.

  • An error that makes every reading wrong in the same direction.

  • An error that makes readings scatter above and below the true value.

Key knowledge

Measure the temperature of the reactants before you mix them.

This is the start temperature.

Then record the highest temperature reached. For a fall, record the lowest.

This is the final temperature.

temperature change = final temperature − start temperature

A positive answer is a rise, so the reaction is exothermic.

A negative answer is a fall, so the reaction is endothermic.

An acid and an alkali are mixed. Find the temperature change.

  • Convert: both readings are in °C, so there is nothing to convert.
  • Formula: temperature change = final temperature − start temperature
  • Insert: temperature change = 27 °C − 20 °C
  • Fine-tune: 27 − 20 = 7
  • Answer: temperature change = +7 °C

The temperature rose, so the reaction is exothermic.

Use the second row of the table. Find the temperature change.

  • Convert: both readings are in °C, so there is nothing to convert.
  • Formula: temperature change = final temperature − start temperature
  • Insert: temperature change = 12 °C − 20 °C
  • Fine-tune: 12 − 20 = −8
  • Answer: temperature change = −8 °C

The temperature fell, so the reaction is endothermic.

Always take the start away from the final. The sign then shows the direction.

  • Measure 25 cm³ of acid into a polystyrene cup.
  • Stand the cup in a beaker so it cannot tip over.
  • Measure the temperature of the acid. This is the start temperature.
  • Add 25 cm³ of alkali and fit the lid.
  • Stir, and watch the thermometer.
  • Record the highest temperature reached.

A warm mixture loses energy to the room. A cold mixture gains energy from it.

Either way, the measured change is smaller than the true change.

  • Polystyrene is a good insulator, so less energy passes through the sides.
  • The lid cuts down the energy passing through the top.
  • Read the highest or lowest temperature as soon as it is reached.
  • Stir, so the whole mixture is at the same temperature.

After that reading, the temperature drifts back towards room temperature.

Making it a fair test

Control variables

Random errors make readings scatter above and below the true value.

Repeating and taking a mean reduces their effect.

A systematic error makes every reading wrong in the same direction.

Heat loss is a systematic error. It makes every measured rise too small.

Repeating does not remove it. Better insulation and a lid reduce it.

So readings can agree closely and still all be too small.

  • Wrong: Repeating and taking a mean always gives the true value.

    Right: A mean reduces random error. It cannot remove a systematic error like heat loss.

  • Wrong: Readings that agree closely must be correct.

    Right: Readings can agree closely and all be too small in the same way.

  • Wrong: Temperature change = start temperature − final temperature.

    Right: Temperature change = final temperature − start temperature.

  • Wrong: A change of −8 °C means you made a mistake.

    Right: A negative change means the temperature fell. The reaction is endothermic.

Key fact

Temperature change = final temperature − start temperature. A rise shows an exothermic reaction, and a fall shows an endothermic reaction.

To compare fuels, burn each one under a can of water.

Use the same mass of water, and the same distance from the flame.

Burn the same mass of each fuel.

Much of the heat escapes into the air, so every result is too small.

The results can still show which fuel gives out the most energy.

Polystyrene foam is mostly trapped air. Air is a poor conductor of heat.

A glass beaker takes in some of the energy itself as it warms up.

A light polystyrene cup takes in very little.
