Magnetism and electromagnetism · Contrast

Magnets and poles

Start here

Two bar magnets snap together. You turn one round, and now they push apart.

If you had to guess, what changed when one magnet was turned round?

Its strength is the same. A different end now faces the other magnet.

Yes. Each end is a pole, and the two ends of a magnet behave oppositely.

Key words

  • One end of a magnet, where its force is strongest. Every magnet has a north and a south pole.

  • To push apart. Like poles repel.

  • To pull together. Unlike poles attract.

  • A material a magnet attracts: iron, steel, nickel or cobalt.

Key knowledge

Every magnet has two poles, one at each end.

Hang a magnet up and one end turns to point north.

That end is the north-seeking pole, written N.

The other end is the south-seeking pole, written S.

Poles always come in pairs.

Cut a magnet in half and each piece has an N and an S.

Like poles repel. N and N push apart. S and S push apart.

Unlike poles attract. N and S pull together.

The forces on the two magnets are equal in size and opposite in direction.

Magnets push and pull without touching, so this is a non-contact force.

The force between two magnets gets weaker quickly as the gap grows.

Halve the gap, and the force grows to far more than double.

That is why two magnets snap together over the last centimetre.

A magnet only attracts a few materials.

Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic materials.

Most metals are not magnetic. Aluminium, copper, brass and gold are not.

Wood, plastic and glass are not magnetic either.

A magnet attracts a magnetic material, whichever pole you use.

Near a magnet, a piece of steel becomes a magnet for a while.

The end of the steel nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole.

So the steel is pulled in, whichever pole of the magnet you use.

Attraction cannot tell a magnet from plain steel.

Repulsion is the only proof. Only a magnet can be pushed away by a magnet.

  • Wrong: All metals are magnetic.

    Right: Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are. Aluminium and copper are not.

  • Wrong: It stuck to the magnet, so it must be a magnet.

    Right: Plain steel sticks too. Only repulsion proves it is a magnet.

  • Wrong: Turning a magnet round makes it stronger or weaker.

    Right: It changes which pole faces the other magnet. Its strength stays the same.

  • Wrong: A magnet does nothing to plain steel.

    Right: A magnet attracts plain steel, whichever pole you use.

  • Wrong: Cut a magnet in half to get a north piece and a south piece.

    Right: Each piece is a new magnet with its own N and S.

Key fact

Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Like poles repel and unlike poles attract. A magnet also attracts iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. So only repulsion proves an object is a magnet.

Rungs 3 and 4 you mark yourself.

Rung 1

You hold one pole of a bar magnet near one end of an unlabelled steel bar. It is pulled in. You turn the magnet round and hold its other pole near the same end. It is pulled in again. What do you now know about the bar?

Rung 2

Two identical bar magnets repel each other across a 4 cm gap. They are then moved to a 2 cm gap, still the same way round. What happens to the force pushing them apart?

Rung 3

A paper clip hangs from the north pole of a magnet. It hangs just as well from the south pole. Explain why, using the idea of poles.

Rung 4

A scrapyard sorts crushed drinks cans. Steel and aluminium cans pass on the same belt. A magnet lifts one kind out. Say which kind is lifted. Explain how you would check that a can left behind is really aluminium.

Recycling plants use magnets to sort cans.

Steel cans are lifted out, because steel is a magnetic material.

Aluminium cans are left behind, because aluminium is not.

To check a can left behind, hold a magnet near it both ways round.

Any pull at all means it is a magnetic material.

Near a magnet, iron and steel both become magnets.

Take the magnet away, and soft iron loses its magnetism straight away.

Steel can keep some, and become a magnet of its own.
