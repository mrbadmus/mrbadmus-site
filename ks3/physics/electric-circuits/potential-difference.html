Potential difference

Start here

A torch bulb says 2.5 V. A car headlamp says 12 V. A mains lamp in your house says 230 V.

If you had to guess, what is that number telling you?

That number is in volts. It is the p.d. the bulb needs, not an amount it uses up.

Right. Much less and it glows dimly. Much more and it burns out.

Key words

  • The energy each unit of charge transfers between two points. The everyday word is voltage.

  • The unit of potential difference, written V.

  • The meter that measures potential difference. It goes across a part, in parallel.

  • The potential difference a part is designed to work at, such as 2.5 V.

Key knowledge

A cell gives the charge energy and pushes it round the circuit.

As the charge goes through a lamp, it transfers energy to the lamp.

Potential difference is the energy each unit of charge transfers between two points.

You can picture it as a push that makes the current flow.

It is shortened to p.d. The everyday word for it is voltage.

It is measured in volts, written V.

You measure potential difference with a voltmeter.

A p.d. is a difference between two points, so the voltmeter needs two leads.

It goes across a part, in parallel, with one lead on each side.

The ammeter goes in the loop, in series, so the current flows through it.

A lamp and a resistor are in series with a 6.0 V battery.

The battery's potential difference is shared between them.

The lamp gets 2.0 V and the resistor gets 4.0 V.

The shares add up to the battery's p.d. 2.0 V + 4.0 V = 6.0 V.

The part that resists the current more gets the bigger share.

battery p.d. = p.d. across part 1 + p.d. across part 2

A 4.5 V battery drives a lamp and a resistor in series. The lamp has 1.8 V across it. Find the p.d. across the resistor.

  • Convert: nothing. Both values are in volts.
  • Formula: p.d. across resistor = battery p.d. − p.d. across lamp
  • Insert: p.d. across resistor = 4.5 V − 1.8 V
  • Fine-tune: 4.5 − 1.8 = 2.7
  • Answer: p.d. across resistor = 2.7 V

Check: 1.8 V + 2.7 V = 4.5 V, the battery's p.d.

A 12 V battery drives two resistors in series. The first has 4500 mV across it. Find the p.d. across the second.

  • Convert: 4500 mV ÷ 1000 = 4.5 V
  • Formula: p.d. across resistor 2 = battery p.d. − p.d. across resistor 1
  • Insert: p.d. across resistor 2 = 12 V − 4.5 V
  • Fine-tune: 12 − 4.5 = 7.5
  • Answer: p.d. across resistor 2 = 7.5 V

There are 1000 millivolts (mV) in 1 volt.

In parallel, each branch is joined straight across the battery.

So each branch gets the battery's full potential difference.

Both voltmeters read 6.0 V, the same as the battery.

The number on a bulb is its rating.

It is the potential difference the bulb is designed to work at.

A 2.5 V bulb glows properly with 2.5 V across it.

With much less, it glows dimly. With much more, it burns out.

A cell's rating is the p.d. it gives, such as 1.5 V.

Cells joined in series add up: four 1.5 V cells give 6.0 V.

  • Wrong: Voltage flows round the circuit and gets used up.

    Right: Charge flows. Potential difference is measured between two points.

  • Wrong: A voltmeter goes in the loop, like an ammeter.

    Right: A voltmeter goes across a part, in parallel with it.

  • Wrong: A part with the battery's full p.d. across it must be faulty.

    Right: A part alone in a loop, or on a parallel branch, gets the full p.d.

  • Wrong: The number on a bulb is how much electricity it uses.

    Right: It is the potential difference the bulb is designed to work at.

  • Wrong: Take 4500 mV straight away from 12 V.

    Right: Change millivolts to volts first: 4500 mV = 4.5 V.

Key fact

Potential difference, or voltage, is the energy each unit of charge transfers between two points. It is measured in volts with a voltmeter connected across a part, in parallel.

Charge is measured in coulombs.

One volt means each coulomb of charge transfers one joule of energy.

So a 12 V battery gives each coulomb 12 joules.

Two resistors in series share the battery's p.d.

Change one resistor, and the shares change.

A volume slider and a fuel gauge both work this way.
