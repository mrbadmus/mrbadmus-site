Electric circuits · Investigation

Building and measuring a circuit

Start here

Two pairs build the same circuit from the same diagram. One pair's lamp lights. The other pair's lamp stays dark.

If you had to guess, what is most likely wrong with the pair whose lamp stays dark?

Most likely. One clip that does not grip breaks the loop, so no current flows.

A meter reading cannot turn a lamp off. A dark lamp most often means a gap in the loop.

Key words

  • In the loop, so the whole current passes through it. An ammeter goes in series.

  • Across a component, with one lead on each side. A voltmeter goes in parallel.

  • A reading that does not fit the pattern of the others. Take it again.

Key knowledge

  • Use the standard symbol for each component.
  • Draw the wires as straight lines, using a ruler.
  • Join the wires with square corners, and leave no gaps.
  • Draw a voltmeter on its own branch, across the component.

The ammeter goes in the loop, in series. The whole current passes through it.

The voltmeter goes across the lamp, in parallel.

A p.d. is a difference between two points, so it needs a lead on each side.

In a single loop, the current is the same everywhere.

So the ammeter reads the same before or after the lamp.

Here the lamp's resistance is 3.00 V ÷ 0.30 A = 10 Ω.

Why

  • Follow the diagram. Build the main loop first, with the switch open.
  • Put the ammeter into the loop.
  • Add the voltmeter last, with one lead on each side of the lamp.
  • Join the red terminal of each meter towards the + end of the battery.
  • Check that every clip grips bare metal.
  • Close the switch. Read both meters at the same time.
  • Open the switch between readings, so the wires do not get hot.

An ammeter has almost no resistance.

Across the lamp, it acts like a wire bridging the lamp.

The current goes through the ammeter instead of the lamp.

A huge current flows and the reading goes off the scale.

Switch off at once. This can damage the meter.

A voltmeter has a very high resistance.

In the loop, it lets almost no current flow, so the lamp stays dark.

The ammeter reads 0.00 A.

The voltmeter reads 3.00 V, the whole battery p.d. That looks right, but the circuit is not working.

Only the ammeter and the dark lamp give the fault away.

Finding a fault

RowMost likely causeCheck first
Lamp dark, both meters read zero
Lamp dark, voltmeter reads the battery p.d.
Lamp goes out, ammeter off the scale
Lamp dim, both readings low
One reading does not fit

Take each reading two or three times.

A reading that does not fit the pattern is an anomalous result.

Take that reading again. Never just ignore it or smooth it over.

Record your readings in a table, with the units in the headings.

For example, you could test how the length of a wire affects its resistance.

  • Independent variable: the length of wire. This is what you change.
  • Dependent variable: the resistance. Measure the current and the p.d. to work it out.
  • Control variables: the same wire material, thickness and battery.
  • Work out R = V ÷ I for each length, then plot a graph.

  • Wrong: The ammeter reads zero, so it must be broken.

    Right: A zero usually means a gap in the loop. Check every clip first.

  • Wrong: It does not matter which way round a meter's leads go.

    Right: Red goes towards the + end. A needle meter wired backwards can be damaged.

  • Wrong: Changing the order of parts in one loop changes the meter readings.

    Right: In one loop, the current is the same everywhere, whatever the order.

  • Wrong: The voltmeter reads the battery's p.d., so the circuit must be working.

    Right: Check the ammeter and the lamp too. A voltmeter in the loop reads that as well.

  • Wrong: Put the ammeter across the lamp.

    Right: The ammeter goes in the loop, in series.

  • Wrong: Put the voltmeter in the loop.

    Right: The voltmeter goes across the component, in parallel.

Key fact

An ammeter goes in the loop, in series, because it has almost no resistance. A voltmeter goes across a component, in parallel, because its resistance is very high. A meter in the wrong place makes a different circuit.

A multimeter is an ammeter and a voltmeter in one box.

Turn the dial to amps and it has almost no resistance.

Turn the dial to volts and it has a very high resistance.

On the ohms setting, it works out R = V ÷ I for you.

An ammeter has a tiny resistance, so it lowers the current a little.

A voltmeter takes a tiny current, so it changes the p.d. a little.

In a school circuit, both effects are too small to matter.

Got a circuit that will not work, and a symptom to describe?

Wear eye protection. Use only the low-voltage supply your teacher gives you, never mains. Never connect an ammeter straight across a cell. If anything gets hot or smells hot, open the switch and tell your teacher.
