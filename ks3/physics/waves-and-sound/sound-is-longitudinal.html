Waves and sound · Contrast

Sound is longitudinal

Start here

A slinky lies stretched out on the floor. You push one end forward and pull it back. A squashed-up patch of coils runs along it.

If you had to guess, as the squashed-up patch passes, does each coil shuffle along and back, or stay where it is?

Yes. Each coil moves a little way along and back, then stops where it began.

A coil has to move to get squashed. It shuffles along and back, then stops.

Key words

  • A wave where the material moves back and forth along the direction of travel.

  • A place where the particles are squashed closer together.

  • A place where the particles are spread further apart.

  • A wave where the material moves at right angles to the direction of travel.

Key knowledge

In a transverse wave, the material moves at right angles to the direction of travel.

Shake a slinky up and down and a transverse wave runs along it.

In a longitudinal wave, the material moves back and forth along the direction of travel.

Push and pull the end of the slinky and a longitudinal wave runs along it.

In both kinds, the wave carries energy and the material stays where it was.

A loudspeaker cone moves forward and squashes the air in front of it.

This squashed region is a compression.

The cone moves back and leaves a region where the air is spread out.

This spread-out region is a rarefaction.

Compressions and rarefactions travel away from the loudspeaker. That is the sound wave.

Each layer of air moves back and forth, then ends where it started.

Examples

A sound wave has a wavelength.

It is the distance from one compression to the next compression.

A sound wave also has an amplitude.

It is how far each layer of air moves from its rest position.

Sound is often drawn as a wavy line.

The line is a graph. It shows how squashed the air is at each place.

The peaks are compressions. The dips are rarefactions.

The air itself moves back and forth, along the direction of travel.

  • Wrong: Sound is transverse, because it is drawn as a wavy line.

    Right: Sound is longitudinal. The wavy line is only a graph.

  • Wrong: In a compression, the air travels to your ear.

    Right: Only the compression travels. Each layer of air moves back and forth.

  • Wrong: A longitudinal wave has no amplitude, because it has no crests.

    Right: It has an amplitude: how far each layer of air moves from its rest position.

  • Wrong: A compression is made by heating the air.

    Right: A compression is made by squashing the air. The particles are pushed closer together.

  • Wrong: The wavelength of sound runs from a compression to a rarefaction.

    Right: The wavelength runs from one compression to the next compression.

Key fact

Sound is longitudinal: the air moves back and forth along the direction of travel. This makes compressions, where the air is squashed, and rarefactions, where it is spread out.

An earthquake sends both kinds of wave through the Earth.

P waves are longitudinal. S waves are transverse.

S waves cannot travel through liquids.

This helped scientists work out that the Earth's outer core is liquid.

Water waves are not purely transverse.

Each patch of water moves round a small circle.

So it moves a little along, as well as up and down.
