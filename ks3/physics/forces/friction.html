Friction

Start here

You push a heavy crate harder and harder. Then it starts to slide.

If you had to guess, once the crate is sliding, is keeping it going easier than starting it, or just as hard?

A little easier. Friction is biggest just before the crate starts to slide.

A little easier. Friction is biggest just before the crate starts to slide.

Key words

  • A contact force between two surfaces. It acts against sliding.

  • A substance, such as oil, put between two surfaces to reduce friction.

  • A kind of friction from the air. It acts against an object moving through air.

  • A spring balance with a scale in newtons. It measures force.

Key knowledge

Friction acts against the sliding, or against a slide that is about to start.

Friction acts even when nothing slides.

On a tilted desk, part of a book's weight pulls it down the slope.

Friction acts up the slope and holds the book still.

Before sliding starts, it is called static friction.

Friction drops a little once the surfaces slide.

If the push equals the friction, the forces are balanced.

Then a moving crate keeps going at a steady speed.

Push harder than the friction, and the crate speeds up.

No resultant force is needed to keep an object moving.

Here the push is only there to balance the friction.

Friction and air resistance are what slow it down.

Once the push stops, friction is the backward resultant.

Less friction means it slows more gently and goes further.

Rougher surfaces give more friction.

Pressing the surfaces together harder gives more friction.

On a level surface, a heavier block presses down harder.

While it slides, double the weight and the friction about doubles.

Treble the weight, and the friction about trebles.

A smooth surface still has some friction.

A lubricant, such as oil or a film of water, keeps the surfaces apart. This reduces friction.

Friction transfers energy by heating.

Rub your hands together and they get warm.

Brakes get hot as they slow a car down.

In a machine, this energy is wasted.

Water resistance acts on things moving through water, as air resistance does in air.

Both are kinds of friction, and both act against the motion.

At GCSE, both are called drag.

Pull a block across a surface with a newtonmeter.

Read the force while the block slides at a steady speed.

At a steady speed, the pull on the newtonmeter equals the friction.

  • Change one thing, the surface. This is the independent variable.
  • Measure the force. This is the dependent variable.
  • Keep the block and its load the same. These are control variables.

Repeat each reading and work out the mean.

  • Wrong: A smooth surface has no friction.

    Right: Even polished wood has some friction.

  • Wrong: Friction only acts once something moves.

    Right: Friction also stops a still object from starting to slide.

  • Wrong: At a steady speed, friction has been beaten, so it is zero.

    Right: At a steady speed, friction is as big as the forward force.

Key fact

Friction is a contact force that acts against sliding. Rougher surfaces and harder pressing give more friction, and lubricants reduce it. Friction transfers energy by heating.

Under a microscope, even a smooth surface is bumpy.

Only the tops of the bumps touch the other surface.

Pressing harder squashes the bumps. More of them touch, so friction grows.

Spread the same block over a bigger area, and each bump is pressed less.

So for hard, dry surfaces, the area makes little difference to friction.

When you walk, your shoe pushes backwards on the ground.

Friction pushes your shoe forwards.

On ice there is very little friction, so walking is hard.
