Drawing and adding forces

Start here

A sledge sits on ice. One rope pulls it right with 40 N. Another pulls it left with 25 N.

If you had to guess, how hard is the sledge pulled overall?

15 N to the right. The 25 N pull cancels 25 N of the 40 N pull.

15 N to the right. The 25 N pull cancels 25 N of the 40 N pull.

Key words

  • An arrow that shows a force. Its length shows the size of the force.

  • The single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together.

  • 1000 newtons. Its symbol is kN.

Key knowledge

Draw each force as an arrow.

The arrow starts on the object the force acts on.

It points the way the force acts.

Its length shows the size, so a bigger force gets a longer arrow.

Label each arrow with its size in newtons.

Choose a scale, such as 1 cm for every 10 N.

Then a 40 N force is an arrow 4 cm long.

A 25 N force is an arrow 2.5 cm long.

Use the same scale for every arrow on one diagram.

Forces that point the same way add up.

Two people push a car forwards, one with 200 N and one with 150 N.

The resultant force is 200 N + 150 N = 350 N forwards.

Forces that point opposite ways subtract.

Take the smaller force away from the bigger one.

The resultant points the same way as the bigger force.

One tug pulls a barge right with 1.2 kN. Another pulls it left with 400 N. Find the resultant force.

  • Convert: 1.2 kN × 1000 = 1200 N
  • Formula: resultant = bigger force − smaller force
  • Insert: resultant = 1200 N − 400 N
  • Fine-tune: 1200 − 400 = 800
  • Answer: 800 N to the right

Always give the size, the unit and the direction.

A resultant of 0 N means the forces are balanced.

With balanced forces, a still object stays still.

With balanced forces, a moving object keeps a steady speed in a straight line.

A resultant on a still object makes it start to move.

A resultant in the direction of motion makes the object speed up.

A resultant against the motion makes it slow down.

It keeps moving the same way while it slows.

A sideways resultant changes the direction of motion.

  • Wrong: The bigger force wins, so the resultant is the bigger force.

    Right: The smaller force cancels part of the bigger one. Only the difference is left.

  • Wrong: Equal and opposite forces on one object stop existing.

    Right: Both forces still act. Only their resultant is zero.

  • Wrong: Draw every arrow the same length so the diagram looks tidy.

    Right: A longer arrow means a bigger force. Draw the arrows to scale.

  • Wrong: Kilonewtons and newtons can go straight into one sum.

    Right: Change kilonewtons into newtons first. Multiply by a thousand.

Key fact

Draw a force as an arrow. Its length shows the size, and it points the way the force acts. Forces in the same direction add. Forces in opposite directions subtract. The resultant force is what is left, with a size in newtons and a direction.

Rung 3

A trolley is pulled forwards with 60 N. Friction drags it backwards with 20 N. Work out the resultant force, with its size and direction. Explain why the friction does not just disappear. Say what the resultant does to a trolley that starts at rest.

Rung 4

A parachutist is falling. Gravity pulls down with 700 N. Air resistance pushes up with 700 N. Then the parachutist pulls a cord and the parachute opens. Now the air pushes up with 760 N. Work out the resultant force in each case. Say what happens to the parachutist's motion each time.

Two equal and opposite forces along one line give no resultant.

Push up on one side of a steering wheel and down on the other.

These forces are not along one line, so the wheel turns.

So where each arrow starts is part of the diagram too.

A diagram showing every force on one object is called a free-body diagram.

It leaves out the forces that object puts on other things.
