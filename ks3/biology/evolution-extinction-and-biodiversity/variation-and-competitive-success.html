Evolution, extinction and biodiversity · System

Variation and competitive success

Start here

In a long drought, food and water run short.

If you had to guess, which mouse needs less food and water to stay alive?

The small one. A big body needs more food and water to keep going.

Yes. A small body needs less food and water, so it copes better.

Key words

  • When organisms need the same limited resource, so not all of them get enough.

  • Things an organism needs, such as food, water, light, space and mates.

  • The differences between individuals of the same species, or between species.

  • Having features that suit an organism to where it lives.

Key knowledge

Every organism needs resources. There is never enough for all of them.

So organisms compete for them.

  • Animals compete for food, water, space (territory) and mates.
  • Plants compete for light, water, minerals from the soil and space.

Members of the same species compete too, because they need the same things.

Most animals and plants produce far more young than can survive.

A frog lays thousands of eggs. Only a few grow into adult frogs.

Some get enough resources to survive and breed.

The rest die young or never breed.

Individuals of the same species vary. They differ in size, speed, coat thickness and fur colour.

Some variations help an individual get resources or escape predators.

These individuals compete more successfully. They are better adapted.

So they are more likely to survive and reproduce.

A mouse with very pale fur is hard for an owl to see on snow.

On dark soil, the same pale fur stands out. Owls catch it more easily.

The mouse has not changed. The conditions have.

Usually, a variation is only better in certain conditions.

Change the conditions, and a different individual may do best.

Different species can need the same resources.

Variation between species means one may compete more successfully.

Grey squirrels were brought to Britain from North America.

They are bigger than red squirrels and digest acorns better.

Grey squirrels get more of the food.

Greys also carry a virus that kills red squirrels.

Red squirrels have died out in many woods.

Natural selection is often summed up as survival of the fittest.

Fittest means best suited to the conditions. It does not mean strongest.

What counts is surviving long enough to breed and pass on genes.

Nobody can know which variation will matter in future.

A new disease might kill every mouse except a few that happen to resist it.

With lots of variation, some individuals are likely to survive a change.

They breed and pass on their genes, so the population recovers.

A population of only a few individuals has very little variation.

One change could wipe it out.

  • Wrong: Survival of the fittest means the strongest survive.

    Right: Fittest means best suited to the conditions. Often that is not the strongest.

  • Wrong: Some individuals are just better than others.

    Right: Whether a feature helps depends on the conditions. Change them and another may do best.

  • Wrong: Organisms only compete with other species.

    Right: Members of one species compete too, because they need the same things.

  • Wrong: Plants do not compete.

    Right: Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space.

Key fact

Individuals of a species vary, and resources are limited. So some individuals compete more successfully, and are more likely to survive and reproduce. Which variation helps depends on the conditions.

Peter and Rosemary Grant measured finches on a Galápagos island for forty years.

In the 1977 drought, only large, tough seeds were left.

Finches with deeper, stronger beaks survived better.

The next generation had deeper beaks on average.

After heavy rains in 1983, small soft seeds came back.

Then smaller beaks did better, and the average beak got smaller again.
