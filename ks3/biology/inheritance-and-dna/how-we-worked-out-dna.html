Inheritance and DNA · Investigation

How we worked out DNA's structure

Start here

In 1953, a short paper described DNA as a double helix. It had only two names on it.

If you had to guess, was the shape of DNA worked out by one brilliant person, or by several teams?

Several teams. X-ray images, chemical measurements and model building all had to fit.

Yes. X-ray images from London, chemical data from New York and models from Cambridge.

Key words

  • Two strands twisted round each other in a spiral. It is the shape of DNA.

  • Firing X-rays at a substance and studying the pattern they scatter into.

  • In DNA, A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G.

  • A way of showing how something works, tested against evidence.

Key knowledge

A DNA molecule is far too thin to see with a light microscope.

In the early 1950s, scientists used other evidence to work out its shape.

In 1953, the double helix model was published.

It needed work from four scientists in two labs, plus a chemist in New York.

Who did what

RowWhereWhat they did
Rosalind Franklin
Maurice Wilkins
James Watson and Francis Crick
Erwin Chargaff

X-rays scatter off the atoms in DNA and make a pattern of spots.

Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling took the clearest image, Photo 51.

Its pattern of spots is the sign of a helix.

The spacing of the spots gave the width of the molecule.

Her measurements helped show there were two strands.

Her measurements also showed the backbones are on the outside. The bases are inside.

Chargaff measured the amounts of the four bases in DNA.

In every species, the amount of A equals the amount of T.

The amount of C equals the amount of G.

This fits if A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G.

Each pair is one big base and one small base. So every rung is the same width.

Watson and Crick did no experiments on DNA themselves.

They built models out of metal and card.

They tested each model against the evidence from London and New York.

One model fitted it all: two strands, bases inside, A with T, C with G.

Other models did not fit. Linus Pauling's model had three strands.

In January 1953, Wilkins showed Photo 51 to Watson.

Franklin had not yet published it.

Crick also saw a report that included Franklin's measurements.

Their 1953 paper only said they were "stimulated by" the King's results.

Franklin's own paper was printed in the same issue of the journal.

In 1962, Watson, Crick and Wilkins won the Nobel Prize.

Franklin had died in 1958. The prize is not given after death.

Today, scientists must credit the people whose data they use.

  • Wrong: Watson and Crick discovered DNA.

    Right: DNA was found in 1869. Watson and Crick worked out its shape in 1953.

  • Wrong: A great discovery is one person's flash of insight.

    Right: The double helix needed evidence from several scientists in different labs.

  • Wrong: Photo 51 is a photograph of a DNA molecule.

    Right: It is a pattern of scattered X-rays. The shape was worked out from it.

  • Wrong: Watson and Crick took the X-ray images.

    Right: Franklin and Gosling took Photo 51 at King's College London.

Key fact

DNA is a double helix of two twisted strands. A pairs with T, and C pairs with G. Franklin and Wilkins provided the X-ray evidence. Watson and Crick used it to build the model in 1953.
