Getting metals out of rocks

Almost every metal we use came out of the ground joined to oxygen. Which method frees it is decided by one thing: where the metal sits relative to carbon.

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

A lump of green-blue stone from a Cornish mine, and a length of copper pipe.

The stone is malachite. Roughly half of it, by mass, is copper. None of that copper looks like copper, behaves like copper, or conducts like copper — and no amount of melting the stone will pour any out.

The copper is in there. Why can it not simply be melted out?

    •

Because the copper is chemically joined to other elements — in this stone, to oxygen and carbon. Melting changes a solid into a liquid and joins nothing and separates nothing. Getting the copper out means taking the oxygen off it, and that is a reaction, not a temperature.

A rock with enough of a metal compound in it to be worth digging up is an ore. Most ores are, or can be roasted into, the metal's oxide.

So extraction is nearly always the same job: take the oxygen away. Removing oxygen from a compound is called reduction, and there are only a few ways to do it. Which one works is decided by the reactivity series.

Key fact

Heating a metal oxide with carbon takes the oxygen away and leaves the metal — but only for metals below carbon in the reactivity series. Metals above carbon have to be split out with electricity.

!

Think again

“Any oxide will give up its oxygen to carbon if the furnace is hot enough.”

Hotter fixes a great many things in chemistry. Commit before you read on.

    •

Aluminium oxide is the counter-example, and it is not a rare or awkward one — it is most of the world's aluminium. Aluminium is above carbon in the series, so it holds oxygen more tightly than carbon does. Carbon has nothing to pull with, and a hotter furnace does not change which of the two wants the oxygen more.

Heat gets a possible reaction going faster. It cannot make an impossible one possible. That is why aluminium was a rarity for most of history and became ordinary within about thirty years of cheap electricity.

Key note

An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal compound to be worth extracting, and most ores are metal oxides. Removing the oxygen is reduction. A metal below carbon in the reactivity series can be reduced by heating its oxide with carbon, which takes the oxygen and leaves the metal — this is how iron, zinc, copper and lead are obtained. A metal above carbon holds oxygen too tightly for carbon to remove, and is obtained by passing electricity through its molten compound. The few metals below carbon that are least reactive of all, such as gold, are found in the ground as the metal itself.

Going further

A blast furnace does the copper-oxide test tube at the scale of a building. Iron ore, coke — which is nearly pure carbon — and limestone go in at the top, hot air is blown in at the bottom, and molten iron is tapped off after several hours. In the furnace most of the oxygen is actually taken by carbon monoxide, a gas formed from the coke on the way up, rather than by solid carbon touching solid ore. The overall result is the same reaction, and the detail is worth knowing because it explains why the furnace has to be tall.

Recycling aluminium takes roughly a twentieth of the electricity that extracting it from ore does, because the hard part — separating aluminium from oxygen — was done the first time and does not have to be paid for again. Melting a can is a change of state. That ratio is the reason a used drinks can is worth money, and the reason the recycling of aluminium was worth organising long before most other recycling was.
