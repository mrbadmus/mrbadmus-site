Keeping energy in: insulation

An ice cube in a blanket.

Two identical ice cubes on the same bench in the same room. One sits in the open air. The other is wrapped in a thick woollen blanket — the sort you would call warm.

Commit before the trial runs.

    •

The wrapped one lasts far longer. If a blanket were a source of warmth, wrapping ice in it would be the fastest way to melt it — and it is the slowest. A blanket does not make warmth. It slows a flow. That is the whole lesson, and the trial below lets you prove it with numbers.

You now know three routes energy can take: conduction, convection and radiation. Insulation is nothing more than blocking those routes. There is no fourth idea in this lesson — there is a set of measurements you take to find out which blockings work best, and one very stubborn misconception that the measurements destroy.

Think again

“A woolly hat warms your head.”

Commit to the correction, then read why the wording matters more than it looks.

    •

A hat has no energy supply. It cannot warm anything. What warms your head is your own body, running at about 100 W from your chemical store, and the hat simply slows the rate at which that energy escapes. Take the hat off a dead thermos flask and nothing happens — because there was never any warmth in the hat to begin with.

The ice trial is the proof, and it is worth being precise about why. If insulation warmed things, wrapped ice would melt faster. It melts slower. The only description that fits both trials is that insulation slows the flow, whichever way the flow happens to be going — hot water stays hot longer and ice stays frozen longer, from the same property. That is why the same foam box carries chips home and carries a transplant organ to hospital.

“Thicker insulation makes a house warmer.”

It makes it slower to cool, which is not the same claim. Insulation adds no energy to anything — an unheated house wrapped in a metre of loft wool still settles at the outside temperature, it just takes much longer to get there. What thicker insulation buys is a smaller rate of loss, so a smaller heating bill for the same temperature inside.

Key fact

An insulator slows the rate at which energy is transferred; it never stops it. Trapped air does most of the work, which is why a duvet, loft insulation, a wetsuit and a bird fluffing its feathers are all the same trick.

Key note

Insulation blocks conduction, convection and radiation. Trapped air does most of the work. It slows flow in both directions, which is why the same box keeps chips hot and ice frozen — and why "the hat warms me" is the wrong way round.

Going further

A vacuum flask is the only object most people own that defeats all three routes at once, and it is worth taking apart. Two glass walls with the air pumped out between them: no particles, so no conduction and no convection across the gap. Both facing surfaces silvered: radiation is reflected straight back rather than absorbed. The inner vessel supported on a few tiny points rather than resting on the outer: the remaining conduction path is made as thin as possible. James Dewar built the first one in 1892 and never patented it. It is not a warm object and not a cold one — it is an object that is extremely bad at letting energy past, which is why the same flask holds tea at 80 °C and liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.

Ask about this lesson

The trial is a teaching model. Each wrapping is given a fixed cooling factor against the bare control, so that repeat runs give the same answer; a real trial gives a spread of results and needs repeats before any wrapping can be ranked. The beakers are treated as identical in volume, starting temperature and surface area, which is exactly the control the planning cards ask you to impose and exactly what is hardest to achieve on a bench. Room temperature is held constant throughout.
