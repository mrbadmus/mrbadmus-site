Energy transfers · Process

Radiation

There is nothing at all between the Sun and the Earth for 150 million kilometres. No air, no particles, nothing to pass anything along. So how does the Sun's energy get here?

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

Conduction and convection both fail here.

Conduction needs particles touching. Convection needs a fluid that can move. Between here and the Sun there is neither — space is empty. And yet you can feel sunlight on your face, and it arrives eight minutes after it leaves.

Commit to how it crosses.

    •

By radiation — a wave that needs no material at all and travels perfectly well through nothing. It is the same family of thing as visible light, just at a wavelength your eyes cannot see, and it is the only one of the three routes that works across a vacuum. Everything in the room you are sitting in is emitting it right now, including you.

Two things about radiation cause almost all the trouble. It is the only route that does not need matter, which makes it feel exotic — and it shares a name with something dangerous, which makes it feel frightening. Neither impression is right, and both are worth dismantling in that order.

"Radiation" just means energy spreading out from a source. Sunlight is radiation. Radio is radiation. The warmth from a fire is radiation. What makes a few kinds dangerous is not the word — it is having enough energy per wave to break molecules apart, which only the far end of the family can do.

The kind in this lesson is infrared, and it sits just past red at the harmless end. It cannot break anything. When a newspaper writes "radiation" and means the dangerous sort, it is using a broad word narrowly — and that is why a perfectly safe process ends up sounding alarming.

Think again

“Heat rises, so heating always travels upwards.”

Half of this is true and the conclusion does not follow. Commit.

    •

What rises is warm air, because it is less dense than the cold air around it and floats on it. That is convection, and it genuinely does go upwards. But it is one route out of three, and the other two ignore gravity entirely.

Radiation travels in straight lines in every direction at once — up, down, sideways. Stand beside a bonfire and one side of you is warm; lie under a patio heater and the warmth comes down. Conduction is equally indifferent: hold a metal rod pointing downwards into a flame and the far end still gets hot.

"Heat rises" is a fact about air, stated as if it were a law about energy. Say "warm air rises" and the confusion disappears.

“Only hot things give out infrared radiation.”

Everything above absolute zero emits it, including you, this page and a block of ice. What changes with temperature is how much: a hotter surface emits far more, and at shorter wavelengths. A thermal camera pointed at a snowy field still sees a picture, because the snow is radiating too — it is simply radiating less than everything around it.

Key fact

Infrared radiation is an electromagnetic wave. It needs no particles at all, crosses a vacuum, and is emitted by every object — more from hotter surfaces, and more from matt black ones than from shiny silver ones.

Key note

Radiation needs no material, goes in all directions, and every object emits it. Warm air rises — energy does not. Infrared is at the harmless end of the radiation family and shares only a name with the dangerous kinds.

Going further

In 1800 William Herschel split sunlight with a prism and put a thermometer in each colour to see which was warmest. The reading rose as he moved from violet to red — and then, on a whim, he moved the thermometer past red, into the dark space where no colour fell at all. It rose higher still. He had found something arriving from the Sun that no eye can see, and he called it invisible rays. Everything in this lesson descends from a thermometer left in an apparently empty patch of a table. Infrared cameras, thermal imaging, remote controls, the discovery that the universe is full of faint microwave glow from its own beginning — all of it starts there.

Ask about this lesson

The bench is a teaching model. Each scenario switches whole routes on and off cleanly, which no real apparatus does — a vacuum flask still conducts a little through its seal, and no arrangement blocks convection completely without also changing the conduction. The detector is treated as reading only the route left open. Infrared is drawn as straight rays for clarity; it is emitted in every direction from every part of a warm surface at once.
