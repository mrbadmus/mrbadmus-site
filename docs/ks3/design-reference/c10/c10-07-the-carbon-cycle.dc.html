The Earth and its atmosphere · Process

The carbon cycle

Some of the carbon in you was in a dinosaur, and some of it was in the air last summer. It has been going round for four billion years — and it balanced, until recently.

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

An oak tree weighs about twenty tonnes. Dig up the soil it grew in and the soil weighs almost exactly what it did when the acorn was planted.

The tree did not eat the soil. It was watered, but water alone does not make wood. Twenty tonnes of solid material came from somewhere, and nothing visible was ever added.

Where did the mass of the tree come from?

    •

Out of the air. Most of the dry mass of a tree is carbon, and every atom of it arrived as carbon dioxide — a gas making up four hundredths of one per cent of the atmosphere, pulled in through pores in the leaves and built into wood using energy from sunlight. The tree is, quite literally, made of air. Burn it and all of that carbon goes straight back.

Carbon is not created or destroyed — it moves. It cycles between the air, living things, the oceans and the rocks, and every step is a chemical reaction you have already met.

Some of those steps take carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere. Others put it back. For most of history the two sides matched, and the amount in the air stayed roughly constant.

Key fact

Photosynthesis takes carbon dioxide out of the air; respiration, decomposition and combustion put it back. The same carbon atoms go round indefinitely — nothing is used up.

Think again

“Plants photosynthesise and animals respire. That is how the cycle balances.”

It is the diagram in most textbooks. Commit before you read on.

    •

Plants respire too — continuously, day and night, exactly as you do. They have to: respiration is how any living cell releases the energy it needs to stay alive, and a plant cell is no exception. In daylight a plant photosynthesises faster than it respires, so the net flow is inward; at night only respiration is running, and a forest at 3 a.m. is releasing carbon dioxide.

The tidy split into “plants take in, animals give out” is a description of the net daytime flow, not of what each organism does. Every living thing respires. Only some of them also photosynthesise.

Key note

Carbon moves between the air, living things, the oceans and the rocks. Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere; respiration, decomposition, volcanic activity and combustion return it. These flows were roughly in balance until fossil fuels — carbon buried for hundreds of millions of years — began to be burned in large quantities.

Going further

Coal exists because of a gap in evolution. Trees developed woody trunks around 360 million years ago, and for tens of millions of years afterwards very little could digest the substance they were made of. Dead trees fell and simply did not rot; they piled up, were buried, and were compressed into coal seams. Once fungi and bacteria evolved the enzymes to break wood down, that stopped happening. Almost all the coal on Earth comes from one window in history that will not open again.

The oceans are the quiet part of this story. They hold roughly fifty times as much carbon as the atmosphere and absorb a large share of what humans release — which slows the warming considerably. The cost is that dissolved carbon dioxide makes seawater slightly more acidic, and shells and coral skeletons are calcium carbonate, which acid attacks. That is the reaction from the acids unit, running at the scale of an ocean.
