Word equations

You can describe a reaction in a paragraph and still not have said what reacted. What has to be in the sentence for it to count?

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

Twenty-two words, and not one of them says what the reaction was.

“I held the magnesium ribbon in the flame with tongs and it burned with a really bright white light and left a white powder behind.” Every word is true. A chemist reading it still does not know what reacted with what.

What is missing from that sentence?

    •

The other reactant, and the name of the product. The sentence describes what it looked like; it never says that the magnesium joined with the oxygen in the air, or that the white powder is magnesium oxide. A word equation says all of it in six words and cannot avoid saying it.

magnesium + oxygenmagnesium oxide

A word equation puts the substances you start with — the reactants — on the left, and the substances you end with — the products — on the right. The arrow is read as “makes” or “turns into”. It is not an equals sign, and the two sides are not interchangeable: a reaction runs one way, and so does the arrow.

The plus sign is read as “and”. It never means add up.

Think again

“The arrow is like an equals sign, so you could write the equation the other way round.”

The two sides do have the same atoms in them, which is exactly why this is tempting. Commit before you read on.

    •

The two sides balance in atoms and they are not the same thing. Written the other way round, “magnesium oxide makes magnesium and oxygen” claims that white powder spontaneously turns back into burning metal and air, which it does not — it takes a furnace, and it is a different reaction with a different equation.

So the arrow carries information an equals sign does not: which way it went. Two other habits follow from the same mistake. You cannot cancel a substance that appears on both sides the way you cancel in algebra — it is doing something on each side. And “heat”, “energy” and “a flame” are not substances, so they never appear in the equation; if the conditions matter, they are written above the arrow.

Key note

A word equation puts the reactants on the left, the products on the right, and reads the arrow as “makes”. Plus means “and”, never add. Only substances appear — not heat, not energy, not a flame, and not anything that was merely present. The arrow runs one way, because so does the reaction.

Going further

Word equations run out of road quickly, and it is worth knowing exactly where. They can say which substances react and which are made. They cannot say how many particles of each — and that turns out to be most of chemistry. Two hydrogen particles react with one oxygen particle; "hydrogen + oxygen makes water" cannot tell you that, so it cannot tell you how much oxygen to supply or how much water to expect.

They also depend on the name being agreed. "Iron oxide" names three different compounds, and rust is a hydrated one, so a word equation for rusting is honest only if everyone reading it knows which iron oxide is meant. Symbols fix both problems at once, and that is the next lesson but one: formulae name the substance exactly, and the numbers in front of them say how many.
