## Pre-audit deep think

Written BEFORE opening any page, any built HTML, or any authored source file.
Everything below is prediction, not observation.

### 1. The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

1. **Pressure and force are the same word.** Children arrive using them
   interchangeably ("more pressure" = "pushing harder"). The whole unit stands
   on the fact that the SAME force gives a different pressure on a different
   area. Any sentence that uses "pressure" where it means "force" is an S1 in
   this unit specifically, because the unit exists to separate them.
2. **"Suction pulls."** The single most durable misconception in the topic.
   Straws suck drinks up; vacuum cleaners suck dirt in; a plunger sucks; a
   vacuum "pulls". There is no such force. The atmosphere PUSHES. I expect to
   find "sucks" somewhere on the atmospheric-pressure page and I will hunt for
   it by literal string.
3. **A vacuum actively pulls.** Corollary of 2, and it is the reason the
   crushed-can demonstration is so often explained backwards. Nothing is inside
   to pull; the outside air pushes in and there is now nothing pushing back.
4. **Air weighs nothing / air is "nothing".** The prerequisite for atmospheric
   pressure. If the unit does not establish that air has mass and therefore
   weight, its atmospheric page is built on sand.
5. **Pressure in a liquid depends on how MUCH liquid there is, or on the shape
   or width of the container.** The classic. A wide lake at 1 m depth and a
   thin tube at 1 m depth have the same pressure. Expect a page to say "the
   weight of all the water above pushing down", which is a half-truth that
   installs exactly this error.
6. **Pressure in a liquid acts downwards only.** Follows from 5. The honest
   statement is that at a point in a fluid, pressure acts equally in all
   directions (which is why a hole in the SIDE of a bottle squirts sideways).
7. **Things float because they are light / sink because they are heavy.** A
   ship is heavy and floats. Density is the fix — but density alone, with no
   force account, is only half the repair.
8. **Upthrust = the object's weight, always.** True only for a floating object
   at rest. A sinking object also has upthrust; it is simply smaller than the
   weight. Watch every upthrust diagram for equal arrows drawn on a sinker.
9. **Objects "lose weight" in water.** Apparent weight vs weight. The
   newtonmeter reads less; the weight has not changed.
10. **Air pressure pushes down only** (it pushes in all directions), and
    **air pressure increases with height** (some children think the sky
    "presses more from up there").
11. **Deep-sea animals must be crushed.** They are not — inside and outside
    pressures balance. Related: "pressure crushes things" as a blanket rule.
12. **Weight in kilograms.** The unit needs weight in newtons for every
    p = F/A calculation. A page that puts "a 50 kg person" straight into F is
    an S1.

### 2. Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- **"Pressure is force spread out."** Sounds helpful, and it makes a child
  believe the total force gets smaller on a bigger area. The force is the
  same; only the sharing changes.
- **"There is more water above, so more pressure."** Installs both the
  amount-of-water error and the downwards-only error in one clause. The honest
  KS3 version is depth (plus how heavy the liquid is), and pressure acting
  every way at once.
- **"Floating means density less than water."** A rule with no forces in it.
  A child who learns only this cannot answer "name the two forces on a
  floating duck and compare them" — which is the actual KS3/GCSE question.
- **"Upthrust is the water pushing up."** True but mechanism-free. The honest
  bridge is: pressure is greater deeper, so the push UP on the bottom face
  beats the push DOWN on the top face, and the difference is upthrust. That
  one sentence is what makes P5 a unit rather than four facts, and I will
  check specifically whether lesson 04 links back to lesson 02 for it.
- **"The vacuum sucks the can in" / "the steam pulls the sides".** See 2/3.
- **"Hydraulics multiply force"** stated with no mention that the distance
  divides — free energy, a conservation violation, and P?-energy will
  contradict it.
- **A "10 metres of atmosphere" scale claim.** Named in my brief because the
  chemistry audit found exactly this (C1-13). ~10 m of WATER is one
  atmosphere; the air column is ~8 km if you compressed it to sea-level
  density, and physically has no top. If P5's atmospheric page carries the
  same slip it is the same S1 in a second subject.

### 3. Where AQA / physics language differs from everyday language

| word | everyday | physics |
|---|---|---|
| pressure | stress, urgency; a tyre "number" | force per unit area, in Pa |
| force | strength, effort | a push or pull, in N |
| weight | mass in kg | a force, in N, = mass x g |
| suction | a real pull | not a thing; a pressure difference |
| vacuum | a cleaner | a space with (almost) nothing in it |
| fluid | a liquid | liquid OR gas |
| dense | thick, heavy | mass per unit volume |
| atmosphere | the sky | the air; also a unit, 1 atm |
| upthrust | (unknown) | the upward force from a fluid |
| displace | move out of the way | the volume of fluid pushed aside |
| head | part of you | height of liquid (should NOT appear at KS3) |
| gauge / absolute | — | KS4+; flag if present |

Also: pressure acts **at right angles to** (normal to) a surface — "normal"
is a word with a lethal everyday meaning and must not appear unglossed.

### 4. What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- Arrows inside a liquid drawn **downwards only**, or only on the base.
- Arrow **lengths not scaled with depth** — the one visual job the diagram has.
- The pressure-in-liquids drawing with a **wide and a narrow vessel showing
  different readings at the same depth**.
- **Upthrust and weight drawn equal on a sinking object** (or upthrust absent
  altogether on a sinker).
- A floating object drawn **not sitting at the right depth** for its density
  (a wooden block drawn 90% out of the water).
- **Hydraulic diagrams with no stroke/distance shown** — force out looks free.
- The **crushed can** drawn with arrows inside pointing out, or arrows labelled
  "suction", instead of atmosphere arrows outside pointing in.
- A **barometer** at KS3 (and if mercury, a safety and relevance problem).
- **Scale**: an air column and a water column drawn the same height.
- A drawing-pin diagram that **does not show the same force at both ends** —
  the whole point.

### 5. The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"You suck the drink up the straw."**

It is said in every primary classroom and most secondary ones, it is said by
adults who know better, and it installs suction as a force that then explains
straws, plungers, syringes, vacuum cleaners, crushed cans and (wrongly)
upthrust for the rest of the child's education. The honest sentence is: *you
lower the pressure in your mouth, and the atmosphere pushing on the surface of
the drink pushes it up the straw.* If this unit's atmospheric lesson does not
say that explicitly, that is a curriculum gap, not just a wording slip.

Runner-up, and the one I expect to find on the floating page:
> **"It floats because it is lighter than water."**

### 6. Where a simulation could teach the WRONG model while working perfectly

- A **p = F/A calculator** that computes correctly but accepts cm^2 and prints
  "Pa" — the classic unit trap, working perfectly, teaching the error.
- A **depth slider** whose number is right but whose arrows are all downward,
  or whose caption says "more water above".
- A liquid-pressure bench where changing **container width changes the
  reading** (wrong physics), or where it correctly does not but the caption
  never says so — the null result is the teaching, and a sim that does not
  point at it wastes it.
- A **float/sink bench decided by a density lookup**, with no upthrust and
  weight arrows: behaves perfectly, teaches the shortcut, leaves the child
  unable to answer a forces question.
- A float sim drawing **upthrust longer than weight on a resting floater**
  (that is an accelerating object, not a floating one).
- An **altitude sim** that falls linearly with height when the real fall is
  roughly exponential — tolerable at KS3 only if the caption does not claim
  linearity, and only if it does not put "space" at a wrong height.
- A **hydraulic bench** showing the output force with no distance readout.
- A **can-crush animation** whose arrows appear only after the vacuum forms
  (implying the vacuum caused them) rather than being there all along.

### 7. Magnitudes I will check against reality (this is the unit where scale goes wrong)

- 1 atm ~ 100 kPa = 100 000 Pa (101 325 Pa exactly); ~1 bar; ~15 psi.
- ~10 m of WATER ~ 1 atm (1000 x 10 x 10 = 100 000 Pa). ~10 m of atmosphere is
  ~120 Pa and is 1000x wrong.
- Uniform-density atmosphere would be ~8 km deep; real scale height ~8.5 km;
  half of sea-level pressure by ~5.5 km; Everest ~33 kPa (about a third).
- Deepest ocean ~11 km ~ 1100 atm.
- Density of water 1000 kg/m^3 = 1 g/cm^3; air ~1.2 kg/m^3 (about 800x less).
- g at KS3 = 10 N/kg (AQA GCSE uses 9.8 — check which the unit uses and
  whether it is used consistently, including in P11 and the forces unit).
- 1 m^2 = 10 000 cm^2 (NOT 100) and 1 cm^2 = 0.0001 m^2. The classic error.
- Person 70 kg = 700 N; two feet ~0.03 m^2 -> ~23 kPa. Stiletto 600 N on
  1 cm^2 -> 6 MPa. Elephant 5000 kg over four feet ~0.4 m^2 -> ~125 kPa. The
  "a woman in heels presses harder than an elephant" claim is TRUE; check the
  arithmetic if it appears.
- Upthrust on 1 m^3 fully submerged in water = ~10 000 N.
- Car tyre ~200 kPa is a GAUGE pressure (~2 bar above atmospheric) — a page
  that compares it to 100 kPa atmospheric without saying so is imprecise.

### 8. Pitch traps to flag

KS4, not KS3: p = h rho g as an equation to use; Archimedes' principle stated
formally with "weight of fluid displaced" as a calculation; gauge vs absolute;
Pascal's law with algebra; moments; anything requiring rearrangement of two
equations in one item. Pitched too low: "heavy things sink" with no density
and no forces; a p = F/A page that never makes the student do one.
