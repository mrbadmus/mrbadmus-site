## Pre-audit deep think

Written BEFORE opening any page, any built HTML, or any `ks3_data/p2` source file.

P2 is the unit where a KS3 physics course either establishes the difference between
**a quantity of energy and a rate of transferring it**, or fails to — and every later
electricity, work-and-power and efficiency lesson inherits the result. It is also the
unit most exposed to unit-of-measurement sloppiness, because it is the only place in
KS3 where five different energy units meet on the same pages: **J, kJ, kcal ("Calorie"),
kWh** — and, hiding among them, **W and kW, which are not energy units at all.**

### 1. The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

**About energy generally**
- **Energy is a fuel-like substance that gets used up.** The whole unit is titled
  around "using" energy at home, and every everyday sentence in a child's life
  ("don't waste electricity", "the battery is dead", "we've used all the gas")
  reinforces it. The AQA/KS3 model is stores and pathways: energy is *transferred*
  and *dissipated*, never consumed. A unit about household consumption has to hold
  conservation in one hand and the word "use" in the other. I expect at least one
  sentence that lets go.
- **"Types of energy."** Pre-2014 language — "electrical energy", "heat energy",
  "light energy", "sound energy" — is still what most primary schools teach and most
  parents say. AQA marks *stores* (chemical, kinetic, gravitational, thermal, elastic,
  nuclear, magnetic, electrostatic) and *pathways* (electrical working, mechanical
  working, heating, radiation). **"Electrical energy" is not a store; electricity is a
  pathway.** In a unit about electrical appliances, this is the single hardest
  discipline to hold and the easiest place on the whole course to slip. I will check
  every appliance sentence.
- **Energy and power as synonyms.** Everyday English uses "power" for electricity
  supply ("the power went off"), for strength, and for energy. The physics meaning —
  *energy transferred per second* — is a different word wearing the same clothes.

**About food (lesson 01)**
- **"Calories" are a substance in food**, or a measure of fatness, rather than a unit
  of energy.
- **The capital C.** A food-label "Calorie" is a **kilocalorie** = 1000 calories
  ≈ **4184 J ≈ 4.2 kJ**. Children (and adverts) say "a banana has 100 calories" when
  it has 100 kcal = 100,000 calories. If the page uses "Calorie" without ever naming
  it as kcal, or slides between "calorie" and "Calorie" on the same page, that is an
  S1 in a lesson whose entire subject is the unit.
- **kJ vs kcal on a UK label.** UK/EU labels are legally required to show **both** kJ
  and kcal, kJ first. A page showing only kcal is not showing a real UK label. The
  conversion is ×4.184 (children can use ×4.2). Watch for a page that quotes both and
  whose numbers don't satisfy the ratio — that is arithmetic a student can check.
- **Burning food = the number on the label.** The classroom peanut/crisp-under-a-
  boiling-tube practical typically recovers only **10–30%** of the label value,
  because most energy goes to the air, the tube, the mount and unburnt residue. If a
  page presents a burnt-food calorimetry result as agreeing with the label, that is
  invented data. If it presents a low result *and explains why*, that is excellent
  teaching and I should say so.
- **⚠️ NUT ALLERGY.** Burning a peanut is the archetypal version of this practical and
  is a live safeguarding issue in a UK school (aerosolised protein; a severely allergic
  child in the room). If lesson 01 runs a peanut, the safety wording is a **flag for
  Mide, never a rewrite**. Cheese puffs / tortilla chips are the usual substitute.
- **E = mcΔT arriving unannounced.** The energy-in-food practical is normally worked
  with c = 4.2 J/g/°C. That is arguably KS4, and it is a units minefield:
  4.2 **J/g/°C** with mass in grams versus 4200 **J/kg/°C** with mass in kg. A page
  that quotes 4.2 and then puts kilograms in is out by 1000.

**About power (lesson 02)**
- **"Watts per hour."** Extremely common, entirely wrong, and it survives into adult
  life. A watt is *already* per second. "Watts per hour" is energy per second per hour.
- **Power rating = energy used.** A 2 kW kettle used for 2 minutes transfers less than
  a 60 W bulb left on all night. Whether the page makes a child *feel* that reversal is
  the pedagogic test of the lesson.
- **A bigger kettle wastes energy.** It doesn't; it boils faster for roughly the same
  energy (slightly less, since there is less time to lose heat). If the page implies
  otherwise it is teaching a folk belief.
- **Power ratings are constant.** They are maxima or typicals. A fridge's plate says
  ~100–200 W but its compressor cycles; a "800 W" microwave is rated by *output*
  power and draws ~1100–1300 W from the wall. That last one is a genuine trap and a
  lovely stretch if it is handled.
- **Typography.** The unit symbol is capital **W** (person's name), lowercase word
  "watt". Kilowatt is **kW** — not KW, not Kw, not kw. Same discipline for **J**, **kJ**,
  **kWh** (not KWh, not kwh). A unit about units cannot be sloppy about unit case.
- **Plausible 2026 UK values** I will check against: LED bulb 5–10 W; old incandescent
  60 W; laptop 30–65 W; TV 50–120 W; phone charger 5–20 W; fridge-freezer ~100–200 W
  running, ~0.7–1.2 kWh/day; microwave ~1.2 kW input; toaster ~1 kW; washing machine
  ~0.5–2.2 kW; oven 2–3 kW; kettle 2–3 kW; electric shower 8.5–10.5 kW. Standby loads
  are now legally ≤0.5 W in the UK/EU — the "phone charger left plugged in costs a
  fortune" claim is a *dead* claim and asserting it is a science error.

**About E = P × t (lesson 03)**
- **The seconds trap.** E (J) = P (W) × t (**s**). Students write 3000 × 3 for "a 3 kW
  kettle for 3 minutes" and call it joules. The hours→minutes→seconds conversion is the
  single most-skipped step in this topic. I will check whether *every* worked example on
  the page performs it explicitly, and whether any practice item quietly needs it when
  the worked example never did.
- **The two consistent systems.** J = W × s **and** kWh = kW × h. Both are right; mixing
  them is not. A page that does not clearly separate them will produce students who
  multiply kW by seconds.
- **Units dropped mid-line.** The classic bad worked example: "E = P × t = 3000 × 180
  = 540000 J". The units vanish at substitution and reappear at the answer by
  assertion. Good practice carries them: "= 3000 W × 180 s = 540 000 J". FIFA
  (Formula → Insert → Fix → Answer) is this site's own house method for KS4 — does KS3
  use a compatible route?
- **Fading.** Full worked example → partially worked → independent. The named failure
  mode I am hunting: the *first* independent practice item demands a rearrangement or a
  time conversion that the worked example never showed.
- **Rearrangement.** P = E/t and t = E/P are KS3-legitimate but need their own scaffold.
- **Magnitude.** 540,000 J is a correct answer and a meaningless one to a twelve-year-old.
  Does anything anchor it? (1 J ≈ lifting an apple 1 m; 1 kJ ≈ a small biscuit;
  1 kWh = 3.6 MJ ≈ a 2 kW kettle for half an hour.) A right number that means nothing
  is a finding under this protocol's "scale and magnitude" line.
- **1 kWh = 3.6 MJ = 3 600 000 J.** If the page states a conversion I will check it.

**About the bill (lesson 04)**
- **kWh is ENERGY; kW is POWER.** The classic error, and the one this lesson exists to
  prevent: "kilowatts per hour", "the meter measures how many kilowatts we use",
  "we used 300 kW last month". A single stray "kW" where "kWh" belongs on this page is
  an S1, because this is the page a student would go back to.
- **"Unit" is overloaded.** A bill's "unit" *is* one kWh — but "unit" also means
  "unit of measurement" in every other lesson. That collision needs naming.
- **Standing charge.** A UK bill = (units × unit rate) + (standing charge × days),
  then VAT at 5% on domestic energy. A page that models a bill as units × price only
  is simplifying — acceptable if said, misleading if not.
- **Gas is not metered in kWh.** A UK gas meter reads **m³** (or old imperial ft³) and
  the bill converts via volume × volume-correction × calorific value ÷ 3.6. If the page
  says the gas meter reads kWh, that is wrong about the instrument.
- **Money will date.** Any pence-per-kWh figure printed without a date will be wrong
  within a year — the Ofgem cap moves quarterly. For late 2026 I would expect roughly
  **24–28 p/kWh electricity** and **6–8 p/kWh gas**, with standing charges near
  **50–70 p/day electricity** and **30–35 p/day gas**. Anything anchored at 2022–23
  crisis prices (34 p/kWh, 10 p/kWh gas) or at pre-2021 prices (14 p/kWh) is already
  wrong on a live page. **A money figure with no date attached is a finding in itself,
  regardless of its value** — and the fix is a dated framing, not a new number.
- **Meter reading arithmetic.** Units used = this reading − last reading. Watch for a
  worked bill whose numbers do not actually multiply out; bills are arithmetic a parent
  can check, and a wrong total on this page is embarrassing in a way most errors aren't.

**About resources (lesson 05)**
- **Renewable ≠ clean.** The conflation named in my brief and the most important
  science point in the lesson. **Biomass/wood is renewable and emits CO₂ at the point of
  use. Nuclear is non-renewable (uranium is a finite ore) and is low-carbon.** A
  two-column "renewable = good/clean, non-renewable = bad/dirty" table is the exact
  wrong drawing for this lesson and is what most KS3 resources ship.
- **Nuclear filed as renewable.** Common and wrong.
- **"Renewable" defined circularly** — "it renews itself". The honest KS3 definition:
  *replenished as fast as we use it, on a human timescale.*
- **"Fossil fuels will run out in 40 years."** That figure has been "40 years" since the
  1970s because reserves are a moving economic estimate, not a physical constant. Any
  reserve number without a date and a "at current rates of use" qualifier is a finding.
- **Where the energy came from.** Almost every renewable traces to the Sun (wind and
  waves from uneven heating, hydro from the water cycle, biomass from photosynthesis,
  fossil fuels from ancient photosynthesis). The exceptions are **tidal** (Moon's and
  Earth's rotational energy), **geothermal** (radioactive decay + primordial heat) and
  **nuclear**. A page that says "all our energy comes from the Sun" is wrong by three
  counterexamples; a page that makes the Sun link for the majority is doing something
  genuinely good.
- **Efficiency and capacity factor confused.** A wind turbine's ~40% is usually a
  *capacity factor* (a fraction of nameplate over a year), not an efficiency; the
  Betz limit (59.3%) is the efficiency ceiling. Solar PV ~20–23% efficiency. Gas CCGT
  ~55–60%; coal ~35–40%. Any efficiency number without a source or a date is a finding
  per my brief.
- **The UK generation mix moves fast** — any percentage split will be stale within two
  years and must be dated on the page.
- **"Energy resource" vs "fuel".** Wind is a resource, not a fuel. Watch the words.

### 2. Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- **"Electricity is the energy."** Saying "the plug delivers electrical energy into the
  kettle" installs electricity-as-substance. The honest pathway sentence is: energy is
  transferred *electrically* from the chemical store of the fuel at the power station to
  the thermal store of the water.
- **"The appliance uses X watts an hour."** Installs power as a per-hour quantity, which
  is precisely backwards, and makes kWh incomprehensible two lessons later.
- **"Wasted energy disappears."** A Sankey arrow bending downward off the page teaches
  that the waste branch leaves the universe. It goes to the *thermal store of the
  surroundings*, spread out and no longer useful — dissipated, not destroyed.
- **"Efficient means cheap."** Efficiency is a ratio of useful to total transfer; cost is
  a tariff. Related and not the same.
- **"Food gives you energy" as a magic phrase** — without the chemical-store-plus-oxygen
  respiration route, food energy floats free of any physics.
- **"Turn things off to save energy"** — as an environmental slogan detached from any
  quantity. If the page moralises without arithmetic it has swapped physics for
  citizenship. (The arithmetic version — "the bulb costs 1 p a night; the shower costs
  40 p in ten minutes" — is *far* more persuasive and is the actual lesson.)

### 4. What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- **Sankey diagrams** drawn with arrow widths that are not proportional, or that do not
  conserve (in ≠ sum of out). A Sankey is a *quantitative* drawing; a schematic Sankey
  with numbers on it is a lie of exactly the SYS-6 kind chemistry found.
- **Waste arrows pointing down and off the page**, unlabelled — teaches disappearance.
- **Food labels drawn wrong**: kcal only, or kJ and kcal that don't satisfy ×4.2, or a
  "per 100 g" column silently compared with a "per serving" column.
- **Bill mock-ups** with no standing charge, no meter readings, no date, no supplier —
  looking like a bill without behaving like one. Or with numbers that don't add up.
- **Bar charts of appliance power** with a linear axis where the shower (10 kW) squashes
  the LED bulb (7 W) into invisibility, so the child sees "bulbs use nothing", which is
  true and *not* the point when it is on all night.
- **Pie charts of the energy mix** without a date.
- **"Energy in food" calorimeter drawings** with the flame nowhere near the tube, no
  draught shield, and no stirrer — drawing an apparatus that would produce even worse
  data than the real one, without saying so.

### 5. The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"A kilowatt-hour is how many kilowatts you use in an hour."**

It is said in classrooms, written on worksheets, and printed on comparison websites as
"kilowatts per hour". It is wrong twice: kilowatts are not used up, and the hour is
multiplied, not divided. The correct sentence a child can hold is: **"A kilowatt-hour is
the amount of energy a one-kilowatt appliance transfers in one hour — it is an amount,
not a rate."** Whether lesson 04 says something equivalent, in those terms, is the single
highest-value check in this unit, and I will make it first on that page.

Runner-up, for lesson 01: **"A banana has about 100 calories"** — off by a factor of a
thousand, said by everyone, and never corrected. And for lesson 02: **"A 60 W bulb uses
60 watts of energy every hour."**

## energy-in-food

**Persona 1 walkthrough.** The hook is the best kind: a real object with two numbers on
it that visibly disagree, and a promise that neither is wrong. I committed the wrong
option A ("Calories are what your body uses; joules are the total") — the reveal appears
and does the arithmetic in front of me (*"229 × 4.18 gives 957 — which rounds to the 958
on the packet"*), which is far better than asserting it. Buttons stay enabled; nothing
marks A as wrong, which is the ruled commit behaviour and did not bother me.

Opened the calorimeter gate on the wrong food (pasta), then drove the bench hard: all
four foods, mass at 0.10 g and at 0.50 g, burn / pause / fresh sample / record, burns run
to completion, records taken mid-burn and at the end. The burn is genuinely good — the
sample visibly shrinks, the water warms, and at full burn the sample **does not fully
disappear** ("SAMPLE LEFT 0.03 g"), which is a modelled version of the real practical's
incomplete combustion. The table records the mass actually *consumed*, not the mass I
selected, which is honest and is the sort of thing most sims fake.

Two friction points, both logged as findings. First, I recorded four runs, read the four
per-gram values, then read "Going further" and found its stated error range did not
contain my own results (P2-01). Second — and this is the one I would have got away with
as a lazy student — I discovered that pressing **Record this run** three times on a
single burn produces three identical rows, completes the stop, and reveals the closing
panel about systematic error versus scatter, having done one measurement (P2-03).

CFIFA is excellent and I want to say so before the findings. **C = Convert** is a
first-class step, the pair is deliberately faded (worked example 1 has nothing to
convert; worked example 2 is in kilograms), and the Answer note on the second one names
the failure mode out loud: *"Insert 0.045 instead of 45 and the answer comes out 0.675 kJ
— a thousand times too small."* Units are carried on the Insert line
(`E = 15.0 kJ/g × 45 g`) and the bare-arithmetic Fine-tune line is immediately annotated
*"Kilojoules per gram times grams gives kilojoules."* This is the single best treatment of
the units-in-substitution problem I have seen at KS3.

Ladder: rung 1 answered wrong (0.57 kJ) — per-distractor feedback is exact
("That is 17 ÷ 30. More cheese means more energy, so this has to be a multiplication").
Rung 2 wrong — correct feedback for the option pressed. Rungs 3 and 4 typed, Complete
enabled, criteria shown. Nothing here was boring. One sentence in the think-again
(49 words, the gym sentence) made me re-read it; it is the longest main-body sentence in
the unit and it is doing real work, so I have not filed it.

**Persona 2 (examiner).** The unit-of-measurement science — my single most important
check on this lesson — is *right and taught explicitly*. The capital C is confronted by
name: *"The physicist's calorie warms one gram of water by one degree; the label's
Calorie — properly a kilocalorie — warms a kilogram."* 1 kcal = 4.18 kJ is used
consistently in the key facts, the vocabulary card and the reveal; 229 × 4.18 = 957 ≈ 958
checks; 250 kcal ≈ 1050 kJ checks. Fat ≈ 2× carbohydrate/protein per gram is correct
(37 vs 17 kJ/g), and all four instrument foods carry correct energy densities (crisps
22.0, peanut 24.5, dry pasta 15.0, cheddar 17.0 kJ/g — all within range of real label
values). Rung 1's cheddar at 17 kJ/g agrees with the bench's cheese exactly. The
distinction between burning in air and respiring is drawn, and drawn well. Systematic
versus random error is taught properly and is the right science-working-practice for the
slot. `ks4_becomes` (specific heat capacity, calorimetry as a required practical, the
contained-vs-metabolisable distinction) is accurate and, unlike C1's, **renders as prose**
— SYS-10 is absent here.

The examiner-level problems are: one causal claim in the wrong direction (P2-02), an
error-range claim its own instrument contradicts (P2-01), and the absence of any safety
wording at all on a lesson that burns food, including a peanut, in a school laboratory
(P2-07).

FINDING P2-01 · energy-in-food · "Going further" first sentence, against the calorimeter's own recorded values and against rung 3 · S2
  What's wrong: The stretch states *"Your school calorimeter will read low — usually
  20 to 60 per cent below the label"*. The instrument on the same page does not produce
  values in that range. Driven to completion at the default 0.30 g, the four foods record
  8.8, 10.6, 4.7 and 4.6 kJ/g against authored packet values of 22.0, 24.5, 15.0 and
  17.0 — that is **60%, 57%, 69% and 73% below**. Two of the four foods fall outside the
  stated range and the cheese is 13 points outside it. The lesson's own rung 3 is outside
  it too: *"Your calorimeter gives 9 kJ per gram for a peanut. The packet says 24"* is
  62.5% below. The drawer's own docstring states the design intent explicitly —
  *"0.30 to 0.46, so the bench reads 30–46% of the packet figure"*, i.e. 54–70% below —
  so the code and the prose were written to different numbers. A student who does exactly
  what the page asks (record three runs, then read the stretch) is told their honest
  result is outside the normal range.
  Evidence: driven table, four full burns at 0.30 g — `1 Crisps 0.30 g 31.7 °C 8.8 kJ/g ·
  2 Peanut 0.30 g 38.0 °C 10.6 kJ/g · 3 Dry pasta 0.30 g 16.9 °C 4.7 kJ/g · 4 Cheese

  `ks3_art/p2.py` `r_calorimeter` docstring, "0.30 to 0.46".
  Proposed solution: Change the stretch to the range the instrument actually produces and
  that real school calorimetry gives: *"Your school calorimeter will read low — usually
  it recovers only a third to a half of the label figure"*. That is true of the bench, of
  rung 3's numbers, and of the practical. Nothing else on the page moves.
  Who fixes: Code (standing authority), with Mide's nod on the replacement range
  Effort: small

FINDING P2-02 · energy-in-food · "Think again", quote 1, third paragraph (final clause) · S1
  What's wrong: *"Your body does it in dozens of small controlled steps at 37 °C, not in
  one flame — and it cannot get at all of it, which is part of why your measured value
  and the label disagree."* The causal claim in that final clause is wrong, and it points
  the wrong way. The classroom calorimeter measures the energy released by **combustion**;
  what a body can absorb has no bearing on how much of a flame's energy reaches 20 g of
  water in a boiling tube. The measured value is low for one reason only — energy escaping
  to the room, the glass and the unburnt residue — which is exactly what the page's own
  "Going further" says two blocks later: *"every source of that error… they are all in the
  same direction… None of these could ever make the reading too high."* Worse, the
  direction is backwards: UK label energies are computed with Atwater factors and are
  therefore already closer to *metabolisable* energy than a bomb-calorimeter value is, so
  digestive losses would make the label **lower** than a perfect combustion measurement,
  not higher. The page teaches the correct one-directional-heat-loss account and then
  offers a second, incompatible cause for the same gap.
  Evidence: rendered think-again text as quoted, against the rendered "Going further" text
  on the same page; and against the closing bench caption *"every one is low for the same
  reasons"* (`p2-calorimeter-mid-burn.png` shows the bench this caption belongs to).
  Proposed solution: End the sentence at the true and interesting part: *"Your body does
  it in dozens of small controlled steps at 37 °C, not in one flame — and it cannot get at

**Persona 1 walkthrough.** The hook is a straight provocation — *"Renewable and dirty.

committed wrong ("Nothing — wood must be cleaner than it looks") and the reveal is the

The first-pass sorter deliberately asks only "will it run out?" and tells you not to think

every one of the eight **wrongly** on purpose and the notes corrected me every time, in

walked away with eight wrong answers and a green tick (P2-12, filed under `p2-02`).

The two-axis grid is the lesson's flagship and it is a genuinely original piece of
teaching. Three axes, three different orderings, and the closing line — *"Three axes,
three different rankings, and no resource wins all three. That is the honest picture, and
it is why the lesson refuses to name a best one"* — is the right register for a
fourteen-year-old.

Friction log, and it is severe:
- **Half the grid is unreadable.** On "Carbon while generating" — the default axis, the one
  the lesson's own caption argues from — Wind, Solar, Hydroelectric and Tidal all sit at
  essentially the same height and their labels are printed on top of one another as an
  illegible smear. Four of the eight resources. I could not tell which was which at 1000 px
  or at 390 px. (P2-28.)
- **I could not tell which column meant renewable.** The vertical axis is labelled at both
  ends ("A LOT" / "ALMOST NONE"). The horizontal axis has one label, "WILL IT RUN OUT?",
  centred under a dividing line, and no label on either side of it. The lede promises
  *"Renewable across the bottom"*. I worked it out by recognising coal and gas; a student
  who did not already know the answer cannot. (P2-29.)

**Persona 2 (examiner).** This is the strongest science writing in the unit and it hits my
single most important predicted misconception dead centre. Renewable ≠ clean is not merely
stated, it is *instrumented*: the grid places nuclear (non-renewable, near-zero carbon) and
wood (renewable, high carbon) in the two cells the misconception says cannot exist, and the
think-again names them. The definition is honest and non-circular — *"the store refills on a
human timescale"* — and the key fact separates the axes explicitly. The second think-again
kills "electricity is a clean energy resource" by identifying electricity as a pathway, which
