## Pre-audit deep think

Written in full BEFORE opening any page, any authored source file, or any
built HTML. Everything below is prediction, not observation.

### 1. The misconceptions a real KS3 class walks in with

**M1 — "Magnets attract metal."** The single most common, and it is a *false
generalisation a child can test in ten seconds and appear to confirm* (fridge,
paperclip, tin can, screwdriver — all steel). The counter-evidence is exactly
the metals a Year 7 handles least: aluminium foil, a copper pipe, a 1p/2p coin
(copper-plated steel — *magnetic*, and a lovely trap), a £1 coin (nickel-brass
— not attracted, though it contains nickel), gold, silver, lead. Any page that
writes "magnets attract metals" without immediately naming the exceptions has
installed the misconception in one sentence. Even "magnets attract *some*
metals" is weak; the list — **iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel because steel is
mostly iron** — is the thing to say.

**M2 — the "magnetic" ambiguity.** English uses one word for three different
claims: (a) *this object is a magnet* (it has poles, it repels); (b) *this
object is attracted to a magnet* (a paperclip); (c) *this material can be
magnetised* (iron, steel). A child who hears "iron is magnetic" and "this bar
is magnetic" in the same lesson cannot tell which claim is being made, and the
confusion is the root of M3 and of the repulsion test. Predict: the page will
use "magnetic material" for (c) and "magnetic" loosely for (a) and (b), without
ever flagging that the word is doing double duty. This is genuinely the hardest
piece of language in the unit and I expect it to be unaddressed.

**M3 — "One pole attracts and the other repels."** Children encode
attraction/repulsion as a property of a *pole*, not of a *pair*. Symptom
sentences: "the north pole attracts", "the south end pushes away". The cure is
always to speak in pairs: N–S attract, N–N and S–S repel.

**M4 — attraction proves it is a magnet.** It does not: an unmagnetised iron
nail is attracted to a magnet, and a magnet is attracted to an unmagnetised
nail. **Repulsion is the only sure test that BOTH objects are magnets.** This
is the examiner's favourite point in the whole topic and my prediction is that
it is either missing or asserted once without the reasoning. If the unit
includes an "is this a magnet?" activity that credits attraction, that is an S1.

**M5 — "cut a magnet in half and you get a north magnet and a south magnet."**
Very sticky. Each half is a complete dipole. Related: "you can have a single
pole" (you cannot — monopoles are not a KS3 idea but the *impossibility* is
worth the sentence, because it is what makes magnetism different from charge).

**M6 — poles are charges.** "Opposite charges attract" and "opposite poles
attract" are the same sentence shape, so children merge them: a north pole
becomes "positive", a magnet becomes "charged", and rubbing a balloon becomes
magnetism. If P10 sits anywhere near a static-electricity lesson in the
skeleton, the two must be explicitly separated. Predict: not separated.

**M7 — Earth's magnetism, the reversal.** *The magnetic pole in the Arctic is
a magnetic SOUTH pole.* That is why a compass's north-seeking pole is attracted
to it — unlike poles attract. Almost every British textbook and most teachers
fudge this, usually with "the Earth acts like a giant bar magnet with its north
pole in the Arctic", which is flatly wrong and is self-inconsistent with the
compass behaviour taught on the same page. **This is my nomination for the
single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms on this topic.**
The three possible outcomes for the page, in order of quality: (i) states the
reversal correctly and explains why it follows from unlike-poles-attract;
(ii) dodges it by never naming the pole type, drawing an unlabelled internal
magnet — honest-ish but a wasted opportunity; (iii) states the comfortable
falsehood. (iii) is an S1. And whichever it does, it must be CONSISTENT with
what its own compass instrument shows.

**M8 — geographic vs magnetic north.** True north (the rotation axis) and
magnetic north are different places; the angle between them is *declination*
and it varies with where you stand and drifts with time (the north magnetic dip
pole has been sprinting toward Siberia for two decades; UK declination passed
through ~0° recently). At KS3 the distinction should be *made*, not
quantified. Predict: unmade, or made only in a stretch box.

**M9 — "there is a bar magnet inside the Earth."** The bar magnet is a *model*.
The real source is the moving liquid iron of the outer core (a dynamo). Two
independent reasons the literal reading is wrong and both are honest at KS3:
the core is far too hot to stay permanently magnetised, and the field reverses
every few hundred thousand years, which a lump of iron would not do. A page
that says "there is a giant magnet inside the Earth" without "we draw it as if"
plants a wrong model that KS4 then has to remove.

**M10 — the compass "points north".** It aligns with the *horizontal component
of the local field*. In the UK the field dips ~66–68° below horizontal, which
is why a dip circle reads two-thirds of the way to vertical here and why a
free-swinging needle has to be balanced. A compass near a speaker, a phone or a
steel table lies. Predict: "points north" stated flatly.

**M11 — the field of a current-carrying wire.** The classic wrong drawing is a
*radial* field (lines shooting out sideways, like an electric field from a
charge) or lines *along* the wire. The truth is concentric circles in the plane
perpendicular to the wire, direction given by the right-hand grip rule for
conventional current, and *closer to the wire = stronger*, which the concentric
picture shows naturally if the circles are spaced correctly.

**M12 — "an electromagnet needs an iron core."** It does not; a coil alone has
a field. The core *concentrates and multiplies* it. Corollary misconception:
"the core is the magnet". Also **soft iron vs steel**: soft iron loses its
magnetism when the current stops (which is why cranes and relays use it); steel
keeps it (which is how you make a permanent magnet). If the page has an
electromagnet crane with a *steel* core it has broken its own story.

**M13 — the solenoid's field.** Outside, it looks exactly like a bar magnet's
— which is the whole point and the reason the ends are called N and S. Inside,
it is strong and nearly uniform. Wrong drawings: circles wrapped around the
whole coil like a bundle; a field only at the ends; no lines through the middle.

**M14 — "more current always means a stronger magnet, without limit."**
Saturation is KS4, but a bench that lets current run to a silly value and prints
a proportional number is teaching an unlimited-linear model. Not necessarily a
finding at KS3 — but *heat* is: a coil at high current gets hot, and a crane
demo does trip a fuse. Worth an honest line.

**M15 — the motor.** The layered errors: (a) "the magnets pull the coil round"
— no, the force acts on the *current in the wire*; (b) the two sides of the
coil feel forces in the *same* direction — no, opposite, because the current
runs opposite ways through them, which is what makes a turning effect (a
couple; P10 should be able to lean on P6/P7 moments language if the estate has
it); (c) "the commutator makes it spin" — the commutator does one job: it
**reverses the current through the coil every half turn**, so that the force on
each side stays in the direction that keeps the rotation going. Without it the
coil swings to the vertical and stops. (d) split ring vs slip ring: a *split*
ring is two half-cylinders with gaps and it reverses; a *full* ring pair is a
slip ring and it does not (that is an AC generator). A drawing showing a
continuous ring under the word "commutator" is an S1.
(e) A wire *parallel* to the field feels no force — which is exactly why the
coil stalls at the vertical position and why real motors have many coils.

**M16 — magnetism is a non-contact force.** It should be named as one, in the
same words the estate's non-contact-forces lesson uses (P4). If P4 says "acts
at a distance" and P10 says "acts without touching" and P9 says "field", the
child meets three phrasings for one idea. Cross-unit consistency check.

**M17 — field lines are real.** They are a *representation*. The field is
everywhere in the region, not only on the lines. Drawing more lines does not
make a stronger field; drawing them *closer together* means a stronger field.
The honest sentence — "the lines are our drawing, not something you could
touch" — is rarely present.

### 2. Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- **"Magnetism is a kind of stickiness/suction."** Any analogy to glue, velcro
  or suction cups plants a contact model for a non-contact force.
- **"The field has an edge."** A drawn field with a boundary, or a bench whose
  "in range / out of range" logic snaps at a threshold, teaches that the field
  stops. It weakens with distance — steeply (a dipole falls roughly as 1/r³, so
  doubling the distance drops it by ~8×) — but it does not stop. A bench with a
  binary in-range flag is exactly the sim that behaves perfectly and teaches the
  wrong model.
- **"North-seeking" collapsed to "north".** The needle's red end is a
  *north-seeking* pole; saying "the north pole points north" is the sentence
  that makes M7 unresolvable, because it makes "north" mean two things.
- **"The current flows and then the magnetism happens."** Sequencing language
  ("first the current, then the field") installs a delay. The field is there
  whenever the current is.
- **"Electricity and magnetism are two topics."** The unit's payoff is that
  they are one thing. If lessons 1–3 never hint forward and lesson 4 opens cold,
  the join is lost.
- **Anthropomorphism**: poles that "want" to be together, a needle that "looks
  for" north, a motor that "tries" to turn. Same teleology tax as C8-13.

### 3. Where AQA / scientific language differs from everyday language

| Word | Everyday meaning a child brings | Physics meaning here |
|---|---|---|
| **field** | a place with grass; a subject of study | a region where a force is felt |
| **pole** | a stick; the North Pole; a nationality | one of the two ends of a magnet |
| **attract** | romantic; "attractive" = good-looking | pull towards |
| **induce / induced** | not in a child's vocabulary at all | magnetism produced in a material by a nearby magnet, temporarily |
| **permanent** | forever, no exceptions | keeps its magnetism *unless* heated, hammered or hit with AC |
| **core** | the middle of an apple; the Earth's core | the iron bar inside a coil — **and P10 uses BOTH senses, in lessons 3 and 4, three days apart** |
| **current** | a river; "currently" = now | flow of charge |
| **domain** | internet address | KS4 — should not appear |
| **dip** | a snack; to dunk | the angle the field makes below horizontal |
| **soft** (iron) | squashy | magnetically soft = loses magnetism easily. A genuinely misleading word that must be glossed or avoided |
| **commutator** | nothing | the split ring that reverses the current |
| **solenoid** | nothing | a coil of wire |

The **core** collision (lesson 3 Earth's core, lesson 4 electromagnet core) is
the one I most expect the unit not to have noticed, and it is one gloss away.

### 4. What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

1. **Field lines with no arrows.** A field line without an arrow is half a
   diagram; direction is half the information.
2. **Arrows the wrong way.** Convention: **outside the magnet, N → S**. Inside
   (if drawn at all), S → N, which is what closes the loop.
3. **Lines that cross.** Physically impossible — the field has exactly one
   direction at every point, so two lines through one point would mean two
   directions. Crossing lines are the classic mark-losing error and the classic
   drawing error.
4. **Lines that stop dead** at the magnet's side, at the page edge, or in mid
   air. Real field lines are closed loops. A truncated drawing is acceptable
   *if* the truncation is at the frame edge and reads as "continues"; a line
   that visibly terminates in white space does not.
5. **Uniform spacing.** If every line is equally spaced everywhere, "closer
   together = stronger" — the single most useful thing a field diagram teaches —
   is not on the page. Lines should crowd at the poles and thin out at the sides.
6. **Too few lines / a "cage".** Two lines look like a boundary.
7. **The Earth drawn with the internal bar magnet's N at the top.** The
   headline error (M7) in drawn form.
8. **A compass needle drawn not tangent to the line it sits on** — the needle
   must lie *along* the field.
9. **The wire's field drawn radially** (M11).
10. **A solenoid drawn with no field through its middle** (M13).
11. **The motor's two coil sides with force arrows the same way** — then nothing
    would turn (M15b).
12. **A continuous ring labelled "split-ring commutator"** (M15d).
13. **Neutral points** between two like poles drawn as a gap rather than as the
    place where lines from both magnets meet head-on and cancel.

### 5. The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"The Earth is like a giant bar magnet, with its north pole up in the Arctic."**

It is wrong (the Arctic pole behaves as a magnetic **south** pole), it is
*self-refuting* on the same page as "unlike poles attract, which is why the
compass points north", and it is repeated in worksheets, on displays and in
half the textbooks in the country. Lesson 3 of this unit is where it lives.

Second place: **"Magnets attract metals."** Third: **"The split-ring commutator
makes the motor spin."** (It reverses the current; the *motor effect* makes it
spin.)

### 6. Where a simulation here could work perfectly and teach the wrong model

- A **two-magnet track** that snaps to attract/repel at a fixed separation
  teaches "the field has a reach". Force should grow steeply as they close.
- A **compass-plotting bench** whose needle snaps to a *drawn* line rather than
  computing a direction: the needle then vouches for whatever the artist drew,
  including crossings and dead ends.
- A **dip circle** with `dip = latitude`. The dipole relation is
  `tan(dip) = 2 tan(latitude)` — at 45° latitude the dip is ~63°, not 45°; the
  UK (~53–55° N) reads ~68–70°, and real UK dip is ~66–67°. A linear sim is
  wrong by up to 20° in the middle of its range, and *works perfectly*.
- A **solenoid bench** where turning the current to zero still shows a field, or
  where the core alone (no current) shows a field, or where "turns = 0" is
  reachable and still magnetic.
- A **motor bench** that spins with the current off, or that keeps spinning with
  the commutator removed (it should stall at the vertical), or whose "no
  commutator" state is not offerable at all — in which case the commutator's job
  is *asserted* rather than *shown*, which is the weaker lesson.
- Any bench with a **fixed caption over a variable state** (SYS-5).

### 9. Reading load, scaffolding and safety predictions

- **Reading load**: "solenoid", "commutator", "induced", "dip", "declination"
  are five hard words in five lessons. Each needs a first-use gloss.
- **Scaffolding**: the motor is the hardest thing in the unit and comes last
  with the least time. Predict a scaffolding gap: the finished motor shown, with
  no worked route from "force on a wire" → "force on two opposite sides" →
  "turning effect" → "it would stall" → "so reverse the current".
- **Safety** (flag only, never draft): strong neodymium magnets are a real
  hazard — they pinch fingers hard, shatter into shards, and **swallowed
  multiple magnets cause bowel perforation** (a well-documented paediatric
  emergency; RoSPA and the CE toy rules both bite here). Magnets near pacemakers,
  near phones/cards/hard drives, near CRT screens. Electromagnet rigs: hot
  coils, short-circuited cells, low-voltage supplies only. Motor rigs: bare
  wires, spinning parts, eye protection. If any of these practicals appear
  without a safety line, it goes to Mide.

## how-a-motor-works

**Persona 1 (student, 390 px).** The hook is well chosen — swap two wires and the
same motor runs backwards — and the four explainers build the argument in the
right order: a current in a field is pushed sideways; the direction depends on
two things; bend it into a loop and the two sides go opposite ways, which turns
it; after half a turn the pushes would fight, so the split ring reverses the
current. Fitting the plain rings and watching the verdict change to "no — half
a turn and it stops" is the best single interaction in the unit, because the
split ring's job stops being an assertion. Turning the current down to 0.5 A and
finding it never starts at all is worth the friction number being there. The
Think-again ("copper is not a magnetic material, so there is nothing to
attract") kills `MAG-17` cleanly.

**Persona 2 (examiner).** The written physics is right and the pitch is exactly
"principles only" as the curriculum requires: no `F = BIL`, no newton metre, no
Fleming's left-hand rule anywhere on the page — the left-hand rule is named in
`ks4_becomes` where it belongs, which is the honest place for it. The turning
effect is built from a pair of opposite forces either side of an axle, and the
lesson correctly links to `p4`'s `moments` for that idea. The split-ring
commutator's job is stated correctly (reverses the current every half turn so
the push keeps driving the same way round), *demonstrated* by the plain-ring

makes the motor turn. The direction rule is implemented as the **sign of current

textbooks get wrong, got right here. The frozen-horizontal coil is disclosed,
and *Going further* gives the correct reason real motors use several coils (the
turning effect falls to nothing twice a turn, and a one-coil motor will not
start from the upright position). The generator stretch is accurate. All 32
states drive correctly: 8 never start, 12 turn and keep turning, 12 turn and
stop.

And then the bench draws a geometry in which the force is parallel to the
current, which is the one thing this lesson says cannot happen.
