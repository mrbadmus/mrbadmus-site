## Pre-audit deep think

Written in full BEFORE opening any P11 page or reading any P11 lesson source. Only the
chemistry audit's PROTOCOL/REPORT/`records/c1.md` and this run's PROTOCOL had been read.

P11 is the physics unit that shares the most ground with chemistry C1 ("Particles and their
behaviour"). A child meets BOTH. Anything the two units say differently about spacing,
motion, compressibility or what heating does to a particle is a contradiction the child has
to resolve alone — and at 12 they resolve it by believing whichever page they read last.
So half of this audit is a cross-unit consistency audit, not just a within-unit one.

### The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

1. **"Heating makes the particles themselves expand."** The single most-taught-wrong idea in
   British lower-school science. The truth is three-part and each part is separately
   forgettable: (a) particles gain kinetic energy and move faster; (b) the average SEPARATION
   grows; (c) each particle is exactly the same size as before. Any drawing with bigger
   circles in the hot panel is S1 on its own. So is any sentence of the form "the particles
   expand", "the particles get bigger", or the softer and commoner "the particles need more
   room" / "want more space" (teleology + expansion in one clause).
2. **"Heavy" = "dense".** "Lead is heavier than feathers." A child who has not had the
   *per unit volume* idea forced on them hears density as a synonym for weight. The classic
   teaching failure is to say "denser materials are heavier" without "…for the same volume".
   I expect to find at least one un-qualified "heavier" doing density's job.
3. **Mass vs weight.** Density is mass ÷ volume, never weight ÷ volume. If P11 says weight,
   or uses kg as a weight, it collides with P12 (mass-and-weight, gravity-and-weight) and P4
   (forces), which will have spent a lesson each insisting weight is a force in newtons.
   I will cross-check P12 and P4 explicitly rather than assert.
4. **Floating/sinking is about weight, not density.** "Heavy things sink." Every child holds
   this and it survives a single counter-example (a big ship). The honest KS3 line is that
   the comparison is density against the fluid's density. If P11 teaches "less dense floats"
   only for ice and never generalises, or generalises without naming the fluid, that is a gap.
5. **"We can see molecules under a microscope."** The standard Brownian-motion error. What is
   seen is a smoke or pollen particle — millions of molecules big. The invisible air/water
   molecules are the ones doing the hitting. If the page ever calls the visible speck a
   molecule, or says "you can see the particles moving", that is S1.
6. **Brownian motion is caused by convection / draughts / the particle being alive.** Brown's
   own 1827 problem. The lesson has to kill "it's a current in the water" or the randomness
   argument is unearned.
7. **Temperature IS energy / temperature IS heat.** Temperature is a measure of the AVERAGE
   kinetic energy per particle; internal (thermal) energy is the TOTAL of all the particles'
   kinetic (and potential) energies. The classic exam trap: a bath at 40 °C has more internal
   energy than a cup at 90 °C. If P11 never separates them, its own lesson title is unearned.
8. **"Heat" as a noun for a store.** AQA's stores-and-transfers model: there is a *thermal
   store*, and *heating* is a transfer. "Heat energy", "the heat in the water", "heat rises"
   all cost marks later. P1's energy unit will have taught the store/transfer split, so a
   drift to "heat energy" here is BOTH a science error and a cross-unit inconsistency.
9. **Cold is a thing that gets in.** "Close the door, you're letting the cold in." Energy
   transfers one way only, from hotter to cooler.
10. **At 0 °C everything stops.** Freezing ≠ stationary; particles in ice still vibrate.
    Absolute zero is the stop, and it is not 0 °C.
11. **Ice floats because it is lighter / because it has air in it / because it is solid.**
    The real reason is anomalous: water expands on freezing because the open hydrogen-bonded
    arrangement holds the molecules FURTHER apart than the jostling liquid does, so the same
    mass occupies more volume and the density falls (1000 → ~917 kg/m³). If the page says
    "ice is lighter than water" full stop, it has taught misconception 2 to explain
    misconception 11.
12. **"Solids are always denser than their liquids."** P11 will very likely make this claim
    in the density or particle-arrangement lesson AND then contradict it in why-ice-floats.
    If the contradiction is the lesson's payoff, excellent. If it is left standing unremarked,
    that is a within-unit contradiction and a finding. **This is my single sharpest prediction
    for this unit.**
13. **Gas has no mass / air weighs nothing.** Relevant to density-of-a-gas numbers.
14. **Bigger objects are denser.** Density is intensive: half a bar of gold has the same
    density. A worked example that never halves anything misses the chance to say so.

### Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- Saying particles "spread out when heated" WITHOUT saying they stay the same size is
  ambiguous to a child: they picture the circles inflating. The negative must be said out
  loud, not implied.
- Drawing a hot panel with the same particle count but a bigger box is honest; drawing it
  with FEWER particles teaches that heating destroys matter.
- Explaining Brownian motion as "the air molecules push it around" without "from all
  directions, randomly, and it is the momentary IMBALANCE that shows" installs a
  directed-push model — the same flaw C1 hunted in diffusion.
- Defining internal energy as "the energy stored in the particles" is circular and lands as
  a synonym for temperature. It needs the word TOTAL and it needs a counter-example
  (bath vs cup) or it will not stick.
- Explaining ice floating with "the molecules line up in a lattice with gaps" risks the child
  concluding the gaps are AIR. Nothing is in the gaps.
- A density calculation shown once, fully worked, then never faded, teaches the student to
  copy a template rather than rearrange. Chemistry's audit found scaffolding gaps of exactly
  this shape.

### Where AQA's language differs from everyday language

- **weight** — everyday: how much there is of something (kg). Physics: a force, newtons.
- **heavy** — everyday: does duty for massive AND dense. Physics: neither.
- **heat** — everyday: a noun, a substance. AQA: a verb / a transfer.
- **energy** — everyday: liveliness. AQA: a quantity in a named store.
- **matter** — everyday: "does it matter". Physics: the stuff itself. Watch for it being used
  in the unit title and never glossed.
- **particle** — physics uses it for atom/molecule/ion indiscriminately at KS3. Fine, but the
  unit must not silently switch to "molecule" for water and back.
- **dense/density** — everyday: dense fog, a dense person.
- **volume** — everyday: loudness. A real Year 7 trip hazard, and it is in the definition.
- **internal** — a child reads "inside the object", which is nearly right and needs saying.
- **random** — everyday: "weird". Physics: no preferred direction, unpredictable individually,
  predictable in aggregate.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- Solids drawn with visible gaps between particles (C1-08 — found live in chemistry).
- Liquids drawn half-way between solid and gas spacing (C1-08 again).
- Gas spacing drawn far too tight — real spacing ~10 particle diameters (C1-01's sibling).
- Hot panels drawn with bigger particles or redder/"glowing" particles (colour = temperature).
- Brownian-motion tracks drawn as smooth curves rather than straight segments with sharp
  corners; or the invisible molecules drawn the SAME SIZE as the visible speck.
- A density diagram that shows two cubes of different SIZE to compare densities — the whole
  point requires equal volume.
- Ice-vs-water molecular pictures where the ice picture simply has fewer molecules drawn,
  rather than the same molecules held further apart in an open arrangement.
- Anything captioned "drawn to scale" that is not (C10-02, C3-09 in chemistry).

### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms, for this topic

> **"When you heat something, the particles expand."**

Second place, and specific to this unit's fourth lesson:

> **"Ice floats because it is lighter than water."**

Third, and specific to its third:

> **"Temperature is how much heat energy something has."**

I will grep and read for all three, in those words and in their paraphrases.

### Where a simulation could behave perfectly and still teach the wrong model

- A heating bench whose particle radius is a function of temperature — even by 10% — teaches
  expansion of particles. Correct-looking, catastrophic.
- A Brownian bench where the visible speck drifts consistently in one direction (a seeded RNG
  with a mean offset) teaches directed motion.
- A Brownian bench where the "invisible" molecules are drawn — helpful, but then the caption
  must say they are drawn far too big and far too few, or it teaches that we can see them.
- A density bench that computes ρ = m/V correctly but only ever shows objects of the same
  volume: the child concludes density is just mass.
- A float/sink bench that decides by mass because every object has the same volume — right
  answer, wrong rule, undetectable.
- A speed slider whose zero position stops all motion in a SOLID or a LIQUID: teaches
  "particles stop when cold". Zero is a reachable edge and I will drive it.
- Any caption fixed while the instrument varies (SYS-5) — chemistry found seven.

### Ordering / prerequisite risks specific to this slot

- `density` first means ρ = m/V arrives before the particle model has been used to explain
  WHY densities differ. That is defensible (measure first, explain second) but the density
  lesson must not lean on packing language it has not established.
- `why-ice-floats` last is the right place for the anomaly, but it needs density (lesson 1)
  AND arrangement (which may live in chemistry C1, not here) — if it needs C1 and does not
  say so, a physics-only reader is stranded.
- `temperature-and-internal-energy` before `why-ice-floats` is fine; but if internal energy is
  introduced with a heating/particle-separation argument, lesson 3 has to be careful not to
  pre-empt or contradict lesson 4's anomaly.
- Does anything here need P1's energy stores? If P11 uses "thermal store" it must match P1's
  vocabulary exactly. If it invents its own, that is a cross-unit finding.

FINDING P11-23 · all four lessons · `figures: []` on every lesson · S2 · Design brief
  What's wrong: **"Matter and the particle model" contains no drawing of a

  · **`brownian-motion`** is a lesson about a *visible movement* whose entire

    paragraphs and drawn nowhere. A student finishes this lesson without ever
    having seen a random walk.
  · **`why-ice-floats`** turns entirely on "an open hexagonal cage with a gap in
    the middle" versus "the same molecules jostling and slipping past one
    another and, on average, sitting closer together". That is a two-panel
    diagram and it is the most-reproduced picture in this part of school
    science. It is prose here.
  This also removes the only route by which P11 could have carried the "same
  size, bigger gaps" drawing that P11-14 says is missing. (Because there are no
  particle drawings, chemistry's C1-08 defect — solids drawn not touching,
  liquids drawn sparse — has no analogue here; see Systemic probes.)

  `ks3_art/p11.py` module docstring §"EVERY VARYING FIGURE IS HTML TEXT".
  Proposed solution: two Design briefs.
  **(a) Brownian path.** A square cell. One visible speck drawn as a filled
  disc, with its past 12–15 positions joined by straight segments meeting at
  sharp angles — never a smooth curve — and a scatter of much smaller open
  circles for the molecules, drawn with arrows arriving at the speck from all
  sides. Caption inside the frame, non-negotiable: "the molecules are drawn
  thousands of times too big and millions of times too few — you could never see
  one." Animate it if cheap; a still with a traced path teaches most of it.
  **(b) Ice vs liquid water.** Two panels, same number of molecules in each,
  same molecule size in each, at one declared scale. LEFT (ice): the hexagonal
