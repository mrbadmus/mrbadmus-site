## Pre-audit deep think

### The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

1. **Vision is active — the eye sends something out.** The single deepest
   misconception in the whole topic, and the one that survives longest. Children
   draw the arrow from eye to object. Piaget-era "visual rays"; reinforced by
   every cartoon that draws beams from eyes, by "I looked daggers at him", by
   "cast your eye over this". The honest model: light leaves a SOURCE, travels
   to an OBJECT, scatters/reflects OFF it, and travels INTO the eye. Any diagram
   with an arrow pointing from the eye, or any sentence of the shape "your eyes
   pick up the object", installs it.
2. **We can see in complete darkness once our eyes adjust.** The corollary of
   1. Real classrooms say "you can see a bit after a while" — true only because
   real rooms are never fully dark. A lesson must say: no light, no seeing, full
   stop.
3. **Light "just is" everywhere — it doesn't travel, or travels instantly.**
   Light as a state of the room rather than something in transit. Related: no
   sense that light has a finite (enormous) speed.
4. **We see luminous and non-luminous objects the same way.** Children call the
   Moon a light source. The Moon, a book, a wall are NON-luminous — they are
   seen by reflected/scattered light.
5. **Shadows are "something" — a dark thing cast by the object.** Rather than
   the absence of light where an opaque object blocks it. Sims that draw a
   shadow as a grey object rather than as the un-lit region reinforce this.
6. **Reflection only happens in mirrors.** So a wall/book "doesn't reflect".
   The diffuse-vs-specular distinction is the fix, and the fix must be stated as
   *the surface differs, not the light differs* — rough surface scatters rays in
   all directions because the normal at each point differs; smooth surface keeps
   parallel rays parallel.
7. **Angles measured from the surface.** The classic exam-killer. i and r are
   measured from the NORMAL. A page that draws an angle to the mirror surface,
   or omits the normal entirely, teaches the error that will cost marks for the
   next five years.
8. **The image in a plane mirror is "on the mirror" / "behind is where the light
   goes".** The virtual image is as far behind as the object is in front,
   laterally inverted, same size, upright, virtual. "Left-right swap" is itself
   a half-truth — the true statement is front-back reversal.
9. **Refraction: "light bends because glass is denser."** Density is not the
   mechanism. The mechanism is a CHANGE OF SPEED: light travels slower in glass,
   so the wavefront that hits the boundary at an angle turns. If a page says
   "denser so it bends" with no speed, it has taught a label, not a cause, and
   the student cannot then predict which way. Also: "light bends" (it changes
   direction abruptly at the boundary and goes straight in between) — a curved
   ray inside glass is a drawing error.
10. **Which way it bends.** Into a denser/slower medium → TOWARDS the normal.
    Out into air → AWAY from the normal. Along the normal (i = 0) → no bending
    at all, which is the case sims most often get wrong or never show.
11. **Dispersion: "the prism adds the colours" / "white light is colourless."**
    Honest: white light is already a mixture; the prism separates it because
    each colour slows by a different amount in glass, so each refracts by a
    different angle — violet most, red least. A page that says "the prism splits
    light into a rainbow" without the differential-refraction cause has taught
    magic.
12. **Colour: "the object turns the light red" / "the object adds colour".**
    THE worst error in the topic after eye-rays. Honest: a red object reflects
    (scatters) red and absorbs the other colours. It adds nothing. A filter
    TRANSMITS its own colour and absorbs the rest — it does not "turn the light
    red". A page that says "the filter changes white light into red light" is
    S1. The decisive test case is the red object under blue light: it looks
    BLACK, because there is no red to reflect.
13. **Absorbed light "disappears".** Where does it go? It becomes stored
    thermally (the object warms). Not required at KS3 but a hedge here is
    better than a false claim.
14. **Black and white are colours of the object, not of the light.** White =
    reflects all; black = absorbs (almost) all, reflects almost none.
15. **Lenses: "a lens magnifies" as a property of the lens rather than of the
    arrangement.** Converging vs diverging by shape; real vs virtual image, and
    "virtual" used bare is a guaranteed stall.
16. **The eye "makes" the picture / the image on the retina is the seeing.**
    Also: the image on the retina is INVERTED, which children find impossible;
    and the lens is not the only refracting part (cornea does most of it).
17. **The camera/eye analogy overreached.** Iris ≈ aperture, retina ≈ sensor,
    pupil ≈ hole. But the eye focuses by CHANGING THE LENS SHAPE
    (accommodation, ciliary muscles) whereas a camera MOVES the lens. A page
    that says "the eye focuses by moving its lens" is S1.
18. **Transparent / translucent / opaque** blurred; "transparent" heard as
    "invisible".

### Where AQA/scientific language differs from everyday language

- **"Reflect"** — everyday means mirror-only; physics means any bounce off a
  surface, including a matt wall.
- **"Scatter"** — everyday means "throw about"; physics here means diffuse
  reflection off a rough surface.
- **"Normal"** — everyday means ordinary; physics means the line at 90° to the
  surface at the point of incidence. This word is the single most dangerous
  everyday/technical collision in the unit and MUST be flagged as such on the
  page.
- **"Image"** — everyday means picture; physics distinguishes real vs virtual.
- **"Virtual"** — everyday means "almost" or "computer"; physics means light
  does not actually come from there.
- **"Dense"** — everyday means heavy/thick; the refraction meaning students need
  is *optically* denser = slower, which is NOT the same as mass density.
- **"Ray"** vs **"beam"** — a ray is a line showing a path and direction; a beam
  is a bundle of rays. Students use them interchangeably.
- **"Absorb"** — everyday "soak up like a sponge, it's still in there".
- **"Spectrum"** — everyday "a range of opinions".
- **"Focus"** — everyday "concentrate".
- **"Luminous"** — everyday "glow in the dark" (which is actually
  phosphorescence, a different thing).

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- **Rays with no arrowheads.** The number-one drawing failure. A ray diagram
  without direction arrows is not a ray diagram; it is a set of lines. This is
  a standard mark loss at GCSE and the standard thing a KS3 page ships. I will
  check EVERY ray in EVERY figure, in the built SVG/canvas, not just prose.
- **No normal drawn**, or the normal drawn but not dashed / not labelled, or
  angles annotated to the surface instead of the normal.
- **The reflected ray drawn on the wrong side**, or i ≠ r visually while the
  caption claims they are equal.
- **Refracted ray bending the wrong way** entering glass; or bending at the
  first surface but drawn straight through the second; or curved inside the
  block; or emerging non-parallel to the incident ray when it should be parallel
  (rectangular block).
- **Dispersion drawn with the colours in the wrong order** (red should deviate
  LEAST, so red is the outer/top ray depending on geometry — the frequent error
  is drawing red bent most).
- **Eye diagrams with the arrow leaving the eye**, or with no arrows at all.
- **Shadow drawn as an object**, umbra/penumbra confusions.
- **Lens diagrams** with rays not drawn to the principal axis, no focal point
  marked, or refraction shown at the lens's mid-line without saying it is a
  convention.
- **Colour diagrams** showing the white ray "turning" red at the object rather
  than one component being reflected and the others stopping.

### Where a sim could teach the WRONG model while working perfectly

- A reflection protractor that reads angles from the SURFACE and always
  satisfies i = r — flawless behaviour, wrong convention, permanent damage.
- A refraction sim whose ray bends the correct way but whose caption says
  "denser" with no speed change shown — the child sees a rule, not a cause.
- A colour/filter sim that redraws the beam as red after the filter with no
  indication the other colours were absorbed — behaves right, teaches "the
  filter colours the light".
- A "what colour does it look" sim that never lets you put a red object under
  blue light, i.e. never reaches the case that discriminates the models.
- A vision sim animating light source → object → eye is the fix; one animating
  object → eye only (no source) is the trap.
- Any dispersion sim where the colours appear at the prism's exit face with no
  differential bending visible inside the glass.
- A slider whose caption is fixed while the value varies (SYS-5 family): "the
  ray bends towards the normal" printed at i = 0°, where it does not bend at
  all, is the specific instance I predict.

normal, incident, incidence, reflection vs reflexion-spelling, refraction,
medium/media, optically dense, specular, diffuse, scatter, dispersion,
spectrum, transmit, absorb, converging/diverging, convex/concave, focal length,

### Ordering and prerequisite predictions

Reflection needs "the normal" defined before use. Refraction needs speed-in-
a-medium, which needs "light travels" (lesson 1) to have said light has a
speed. Colour-and-the-spectrum needs refraction (dispersion IS refraction), so
if `why-things-look-coloured` or `colour-and-the-spectrum` is ordered before
`refraction` in the taught skeleton, dispersion cannot be explained honestly.
`lenses-and-images` needs refraction. `the-eye-and-the-camera` needs lenses.
So the only defensible order is: travels → reflection → refraction → colour →
why-coloured → lenses → eye. The brief's stated audit order matches that; the
FILE NUMBERING in `ks3_data/p7/` does not (04 lenses, 05 eye, 06 colour, 07
why-coloured). I will check which order the built pages' prev/next chain and
the unit index actually present, and whether refraction precedes colour.

### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"Light bends when it goes into glass because glass is denser."**

It is said in thousands of KS3 lessons every week. It is a label wearing the
costume of an explanation. It gives the student no way to predict which way the
ray turns, it collides with "dense" meaning mass-density everywhere else in
their science, and it is exactly the sentence that has to be un-learned at GCSE
when wave speed arrives. The honest KS3 version is: *light travels more slowly
in glass than in air, and that change of speed at the boundary is what turns
the ray — towards the normal going in, away from it coming out.*

Runner-up, and the one specific to this unit's worst-risk page: **"a red filter
turns the light red."** It doesn't. It lets red through and absorbs the rest.

### What I will go looking for, specifically

1. Arrowheads on every ray in every built figure. Count them.
2. Any eye-emits-light implication, in prose, in a diagram, in an instrument.
3. The normal: drawn, dashed, labelled, and angles measured from it.
4. Speed as the refraction mechanism; direction of bending consistent between
   every caption and every drawing.
5. `why-things-look-coloured`: reflect/absorb vs "turns it red"; filters
   transmit vs "change the colour"; red-under-blue = black.
6. Dispersion cause and spectrum order; indigo in or out, consistently.
7. "Virtual" defined before use; lenses pitched at KS3 not KS4.
8. Eye/camera analogy accuracy — accommodation vs moving lens; cross-check the
   biology estate for an eye lesson and flag any disagreement.
9. Safety: lasers, the Sun, bright sources — flag to Mide, never draft.
10. Every instrument at zero, maximum, and extreme angles; captions at edges.
