### The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

1. **A wave carries the stuff along.** The single deepest one. Children watch a
   wave cross a pond and conclude the water travels to the far bank; they watch a
   Mexican wave and (correctly, but without transferring it) know nobody moves
   seats. The honest model: the wave transfers ENERGY (and information) through
   the medium; each particle oscillates about a fixed rest position and ends up
   where it started. Any animation in which a marked particle drifts net-forward
   over a cycle teaches the wrong model perfectly, no matter how pretty it is.
2. **Sound looks like a squiggle.** The oscilloscope trace is a graph of
   pressure (or microphone voltage) against TIME. It is not a picture of the air.
   Because every textbook, every music app and every waveform icon draws sound as
   a transverse squiggle, the trace silently installs "sound is a transverse
   wave" — and then Y10 cannot say why sound will not polarise, or why it needs a
   medium. This is my primary hunt in this unit: any trace without axes, or any
   caption implying sound "looks like" or "is shaped like" that, is S1.
3. **Sound travels as moving air / a puff of air.** "The air goes from the
   speaker to my ear." Related: "you can hear because air is blown at you". The
   honest model: a local squeeze passes along; the air molecules jiggle back and
   forth about a mean position.
4. **A vacuum sucks / kills sound.** The bell-jar argument is routinely narrated
   as the vacuum "sucking the sound out", or the sound "dying". Neither is a
   mechanism. The correct argument: as particles are removed there is
   progressively less matter to pass the squeeze along, so less and less arrives;
   in a perfect vacuum there is nothing to carry it, so nothing is transmitted.
   Note also the honest complication a good lesson handles: sound still travels
   through the bench/stand by conduction, which is why the bell is suspended.
5. **Louder = higher pitched.** Everyday English blurs "high" (volume knob up)
   with "high" (pitch). The exam-mark error. Amplitude ↔ loudness, frequency ↔
   pitch, and the two are independent. Any sim that changes both at once, or a
   sentence that says "as it gets higher it gets louder", plants this.
6. **Frequency IS pitch (and amplitude IS loudness).** The subtler half of #5.
   Frequency is a physical quantity measured in Hz; pitch is what a brain hears.
   Amplitude is a physical quantity (metres, or trace height); loudness is a
   perception. They correspond but are not identical — perception is non-linear
   and person-dependent. KS3 need not do decibels, but the page must not write
   "amplitude means loudness" as a definition.
7. **Echo = "the sound bouncing back later because it got tired".** Or: the echo
   is a different, new sound. Also very common: "sound bounces off soft things
   too, just quieter" (it is absorbed, and the energy becomes a tiny amount of
   heating — not "destroyed").
8. **The there-and-back factor of 2.** The classic dropped mark in every echo
   and every sonar/ultrasound calculation. distance to the wall = (v × t) / 2,
   because the sound made the trip twice. Half the country's Y9 answers double
   the distance instead of halving it. Every worked example in this unit must
   make the halving explicit and say WHY.
9. **"Ultrasound is dangerous / it's a kind of X-ray."** Because the only
   ultrasound most children have met is a hospital scan, they file it with
   radiation. It is sound, above the human hearing range, and its safety versus
   X-rays is exactly the point worth making.
10. **The human range is "20 to 20,000" as a fact about all humans forever.**
    It is a rough adult-ish figure; the upper limit falls markedly with age (a
    Y7 hears well above 17 kHz; their teacher very likely does not — the whole
    "mosquito ringtone" phenomenon). A page that states 20 Hz–20 kHz flat, with
    no age qualification, teaches something the class can disprove in the room.
11. **Amplitude measured peak-to-trough.** The classic diagram error. Amplitude
    is measured from the MIDLINE (undisturbed/rest position) to a peak — half
    the peak-to-trough height. Wavelength is peak-to-peak (or any point to the
    next identical point), NOT peak-to-trough. Both mismeasurements are
    endemic in worksheets.
12. **Longitudinal waves "have no wavelength/amplitude".** They do: wavelength
    is compression to next compression; amplitude is how far a particle moves
    from its rest position (equivalently, how squeezed the compressions get).
13. **Water waves are a perfect model of all waves.** Real surface water waves
    are neither purely transverse nor simple (particles move in circles/ellipses;
    the wave is dispersive; it shoals and breaks). At KS3 the idealisation is
    fine — but a page claiming water particles "just go up and down" as a fact
    rather than a model is overclaiming, and the "does the duck move along?"
    demonstration is precisely where the idealisation earns its keep.
14. **Superposition = waves "bounce off" or "cancel forever".** Children think
    two waves meeting destroy each other permanently. They pass through and
    emerge unchanged; the cancellation exists only where and while they overlap.

### Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- **"Sound is a vibration" full stop**, with no medium in the sentence. The
  vibration is OF something. Drop the medium and you have licensed sound in space.
- **The domino/Newton's-cradle analogy** used without its limits: dominoes do not
  return, so the analogy quietly deletes the oscillation and installs a
  one-way-transfer-of-matter picture. A slinky is the honest apparatus.
- **"The wave slows down in air and speeds up in solids because solids are
  denser."** Density alone predicts the opposite; it is stiffness that dominates.
  At KS3 the safe form is "particles are closer together and more strongly
  connected, so the squeeze is passed on sooner". A page that says only "denser =
  faster" has taught something that fails on the first counterexample (lead).
- **Drawing the compression as "where the particles are moving fastest"** or
  colouring compressions as "loud bits". A compression is where particles are
  momentarily crowded; loudness is amplitude, not compression location.
- **"Higher frequency = faster wave."** Rife. Frequency and speed are
  independent; in a given medium the speed is fixed and f × λ = v means higher
  frequency simply means shorter wavelength. If this unit uses the wave equation
  at all, this is the point it must protect.
- **Anthropomorphised energy** — sound "wanting" to get through, energy "getting
  used up" like fuel. AQA's stores/transfers language matters even at KS3.

### Where AQA's language differs from everyday language

| everyday | physics |
|---|---|
| "loud/high" used interchangeably | loudness (perception) vs pitch (perception) vs amplitude and frequency (measured quantities) |
| "sound waves travel" | energy is transferred by the wave; the medium does not travel |
| "vibration" | oscillation about a rest/equilibrium position |
| "echo" | a reflection of sound from a hard surface |
| "soundproofing" | absorption — the energy is transferred to the internal (thermal) store of the material |
| "a wave" (a single hump) | usually a continuous train, with a repeat length |
| "noise" | any unwanted sound, not a synonym for loud |
| "amplitude" (colloquially = size) | displacement from the rest position at a peak |
| "wave goes up and down" | the PARTICLES oscillate; the wave travels sideways |
| "supersonic/ultrasonic" | supersonic = faster than sound; ultrasonic = above human hearing. Constantly swapped. |

Also: "medium" has an everyday meaning (middling, a psychic) that will land wrong
for some children; "pitch" means a football field; "wave" means a hand gesture;
"transverse" and "longitudinal" are both bare Latin to a Y7 and need their
meanings unpacked ("across" / "along"), not just their spellings.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- Amplitude arrow drawn peak-to-trough (see #11) — the most common single error.
- Wavelength arrow drawn peak-to-TROUGH rather than peak-to-peak; or drawn on a
  longitudinal diagram between a compression and the next RAREFACTION.
- No midline / rest-position line drawn at all, which makes amplitude
  unmeasurable and hides the "particles return to where they started" point.
- Unlabelled axes on the transverse curve: is the horizontal axis distance
  (a snapshot) or time (a history)? The two graphs look identical and mean
  entirely different things; wavelength lives on one and period on the other.
- Longitudinal diagrams drawn as evenly-spaced dots with an arrow, so there are
  no visible compressions; or drawn with particles that translate along the tube.
- The oscilloscope screen shown with no axis labels and no time base.
- The ear drawn as a funnel with sound "going in" and no mechanism.
- Sonar/echo diagrams drawing one arrow down and one up but labelling the total
  path "distance to the seabed".
- Bell-jar diagrams that show the bell touching the base plate (which would
  conduct sound out and undermine the whole demonstration).
- Wave-speed contrasts drawn as a longer arrow for "faster" without a time.

### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms (this topic)

**"Sound is a wave that travels through the air, and this is what it looks
like"** — spoken over a transverse oscilloscope squiggle. In one breath it (a)
implies the picture is a picture of the wave's shape rather than a graph, (b)
installs transverse for a longitudinal phenomenon, and (c) makes air the only
medium. Runner-up, and nearly as common: **"the amplitude tells you how high
the sound is"**, which fuses the amplitude/loudness and pitch confusions into
one four-word error.

### Where a simulation could teach the WRONG model while running perfectly

- Any longitudinal sim whose particles drift net-forward, or whose "particles"
  are re-drawn each frame from a formula that includes a translation term.
- A transverse wave sim with no fixed reference marker — without a marked
  particle and a rest line, a child cannot see that the medium does not travel.
  Correct behaviour, invisible model.
- A pitch/loudness sim where the drawn trace changes amplitude when the frequency
  dial moves (or vice versa) — cross-talk that teaches misconception #5
  mechanically.
- A bell-jar sim whose sound "fades" as a volume slider rather than as a
  particle-count consequence — right output, wrong cause.
- An echo timer that reports a distance without ever showing the /2, so pressing
  buttons produces correct answers and no method.
- A superposition sim where the waves stop at the meeting point, or where
  cancellation persists after they separate.
- Any sim with a fixed caption (SYS-5) over a variable state: "the wave gets
  taller" while the amplitude slider is at zero; "louder" at amplitude 0;
  "you can hear it" at particle count 0.

**Persona 2 (examiner).** Science is correct throughout and unusually careful.
Amplitude from the rest line, wavelength crest-to-crest, and *"Changing one does
not change the other"* stated three times and enforced by the instrument.
Steepness is computed as crest-to-trough height ÷ wavelength — the standard
definition — and the ~1-in-7 breaking limit is the real deep-water figure;
every one of the eight states I drove computed it correctly (40/300 = 1 in 8,
10/300 = 1 in 30, 80/300 = 1 in 4, 40/100 = 1 in 3, 40/500 = 1 in 13,
10/100 = 1 in 10, 80/500 = 1 in 6). The tsunami passage is right (amplitude
under a metre, wavelength to 200 km, steepness 1 in 200 000 — the arithmetic
checks). The Going-further paragraph volunteers the honest limit I predicted
would be missing: *"the water in a wave does not move in a straight line up and
down. Each patch travels round a small near-circle."*

**Figure scale verified, not assumed.** The figure captions itself *"drawn to
one scale in both directions: 0.40 m from crest to crest, and 0.08 m from the
still level up to a crest"* — the exact form of claim that produced chemistry's
C3-09. I measured the SVG: still level y=190, crest y=118, crests at x=140 and

crest, not trough to crest) and the wavelength marker `M140 78 H500` (crest to
crest). **The claim is true.** No C3-09 defect here.

## p6-02 transverse-waves-and-superposition

**Persona 1 (student).** The two-stones hook is strong and the wrong options
are the real wrong ideas ("the stronger set of ripples wins"). The
superposition bench is excellent: I set both waves to 12 mm crest-on-trough and
got *"Cancelling exactly… Both waves are still there, and both leave the
overlap at 12 mm"*; 12 and 2 mm crest-on-trough gave *"Cancelling in part"*
with 12 − 2 = 10. The **Your turn** panel is live-bound to the sliders — I
moved them to five different pairs and the task text tracked every time
(*"Your two waves: 12 mm and 2 mm, arriving crest on crest"*), which is exactly
what the standing line beneath it promises. Wrote "banana" into all five FIFA
lines and pressed Check: it marks against model lines with "You wrote: banana"
beside each and an "I had this" tick — self-marking, no dead end. Friction log:
one entry only — the second worked example uses centimetres against
millimetres ("A crest 1.2 cm high meets a crest 7 mm high"), which is the point
of it, and I had to re-read it once. That is intended difficulty, not a defect.

**Persona 2 (examiner).** Displacement is correctly defined as signed
("up counts as positive, down as negative") and the resultant is the sum at
every point. Both Think-agains are right and are the two that matter:
cancelling happens to a *place*, not to the waves; and the energy is
redistributed, not deleted (*"there are other places, half a wavelength away,
where the same two waves add"*). Pitch is right for KS3: superposition is in
the KS3 programme of study, path difference and phase are correctly deferred to
the "At GCSE this becomes" card, and standing waves appear only qualitatively
in Going-further. Noise-cancelling headphones and two-speaker quiet patches are
both accurate. Edge states are honest: at both sliders zero the verdict is
*"Nothing is running… This is not cancelling — cancelling needs two waves, and
there are none"*, and with one slider at zero it is *"Only one wave… crest-on-
crest and crest-on-trough give the same 8 mm"*. That is better edge-state
writing than most of the chemistry estate.

  on its own."* No student has ever heard their own shout come back off a school
  field, and this is the one claim in the unit a class can falsify at
  lunchtime. (The 20% reflection coefficient itself is defensible for
  grass-covered ground at normal incidence — which is why I have graded this S2
  rather than S1. The defect is the exemplar, not the physics: a playing field
  presents a horizontal surface, and the bench's model, as its own assumptions
  note says, treats every surface as *"flat and facing you squarely"*. Applied
  to grass that produces a vertical wall of turf, which is what generates the
  false prediction.)
  Evidence: rendered bench, Mown grass at 500 m: "About 20% of your shout comes
  back off mown grass, and the journey is 500 m out and 500 m back — 1000 m in
  all, which at about 340 m/s takes 2.94 s. Both conditions are met: enough
  returns, and it returns late enough to hear on its own." Figure row: "Mown
  grass / about 20% · a playing field", above the threshold marker "15% — BELOW
  THIS, NO SEPARATE ECHO".
  Proposed solution: two options, both one edit. (a) Change the exemplar to
  something that really is a vertical surface of that reflectivity — a hedge, a
  dense treeline or a wooden fence — keeping 20% and gaining a case students
  genuinely can test (a treeline echo is real). (b) Keep grass and move it below
  the threshold with a gloss naming the reason ("about 10% · a playing field,
  and it is under your feet rather than in front of you"). (a) is preferable
  because it preserves the figure's spread of five values and teaches something
  true.
  Who fixes: Code (standing authority) + Mide sign-off (science call)
  Effort: small

FINDING P6-17 · echoes-reflection-and-absorption · #s-bar, "Cover d" · S2
  What's wrong: The bar model's generic rule is "cover the part you want and
  read off what is left". Applied to a bar whose two parts are BOTH `d`, it
  yields **`d = s − d`** as the on-screen result — an equation with the unknown
  on both sides, which is the one form a KS3 student cannot use and, worse, the
  form that looks like an algebra error they have been trained to avoid. The
  page then has to spend two sentences undoing its own instrument (*"Both parts
  are equal, so this is the same as d = s ÷ 2"*, and again in the standing note).
  The instrument is right, the arithmetic is right, and the teaching move is
  right — but the headline output of the interaction is the least useful of the
  three things on screen, and it is what a student pressing the button sees
  first. This is the only place in the unit where an instrument makes a topic
  harder than the prose around it.
  Evidence: driven — pressing "Cover d" renders "d = s − d" with the note
  "Cover one part and the whole bar and the other part are left. Both parts are
  equal, so this is the same as d = s ÷ 2 — and that is the form every echo
  calculation uses." Pressing "Cover s" renders "s = d + d", which is fine.
  Proposed solution: special-case a bar whose two parts carry the same symbol —
  render `d = s ÷ 2` directly as the covered result, and keep `d = s − d` as
  the *reasoning* line beneath it rather than the answer line. The two sentences
  already written become the explanation of a correct headline instead of the
  correction of a confusing one. Contained to the bar renderer; check whether
  any other KS3 bar has two identically-labelled parts before changing the
  shared path.
  Who fixes: Code (standing authority), behaviour past Design
  Effort: small

FINDING P6-18 · echoes-reflection-and-absorption · #s-echo bench verdict strings · S4
  What's wrong: Three composed-caption defects in one instrument.
  (i) **Subject–verb agreement**: *"**Foam wedges sends** only about 3% of the
  sound back"* and *"**Heavy curtain sends** only about 10%"* — the surface name
  is interpolated into a sentence written for a singular subject, and "Foam
  wedges sends" is visibly wrong English on a page students read aloud in class.
  (ii) **A quantity word that does not track its number**: the near-field branch
  says *"**Plenty** is coming back — about X% off Y"* with the same word for
  90% (bare rock) and for 20% (mown grass), where 20% is five points above the
  page's own "not enough" line and the figure classes grass as an absorber.
  (iii) **A place word that does not track the scene**: the same sentence ends
  *"so **the room** sounds live rather than silent"*, printed for bare rock —
  which the figure glosses as "a quarry" — and for mown grass, "a playing
  field". Neither is a room.
  Evidence: rendered bench states — Foam wedges at any distance: "Foam wedges
  sends only about 3% of the sound back, and below roughly 15% there is not
  enough returning to hear as a separate sound." Mown grass at 10 m: "Plenty is
  coming back — about 20% off mown grass — so the room sounds live rather than
  silent." Bare rock at 10 m: identical sentence with "about 90%".
  Proposed solution: (i) compose as "%s sends back only about X%%" or give each
  surface a `verb` field; (ii) branch the quantity word on the percentage —
  "Plenty" above ~50%, "Enough to hear" between the threshold and there;
  (iii) replace "the room" with a neutral noun ("the place sounds live") or take
  the scene word from the surface's own gloss, which already exists in the
  figure data ("a quarry", "a bare sports hall", "a playing field").
  Who fixes: Code (standing authority)
  Effort: small

## p6-08 hearing-and-auditory-range

**Persona 1 (student).** The dog-whistle hook is the right one and its reveal is
the lesson's thesis in one line. Drove the bench through all 14 states
(7 listeners × min/max frequency) plus intermediate positions; every verdict is
correct and the reason is always given rather than just the yes/no. The
multiplying axis is explained *before* it is used and then again in the figure
caption (*"a bar reaching one mark further right is a range going ten times
higher — which is why a straight ruler scale would have squashed every one of
these bars into the same left-hand centimetre"*), which is real graph-literacy
teaching and rare at KS3. Nothing boring; the seven-animal comparison is the
kind of thing a class argues about.

**Persona 2 (examiner).** Every published range is right: human 20–20 000 Hz,
human at 50 20–12 000 Hz, dog 67–45 000, cat 45–64 000, mouse 1000–91 000,
bat 2000–110 000, elephant 16–12 000. My prediction #10 is fully handled — the
upper limit is qualified as declining with age in four separate places (the
"Human, at 50" listener, the Key fact's closing clause, the Think-again on
consonants, and the assumptions note's *"The 20 Hz and 20 000 Hz limits of the
human range are round conventions rather than measurements, and very few adults
reach 20 000 Hz"*). Ultrasound is defined as above 20 000 Hz and, better,
framed as a fact about **us** rather than about the sound — *"Those names are
about us, not about the sound… A bat hearing a 60 000 Hz call is not hearing
something exotic. It is just hearing."* That is the single best sentence in the
unit. The consonant paragraph (high frequencies carry s/f and t/k, above
4000 Hz; shouting does not help) is accurate and socially useful. Bat
echolocation wavelength reasoning is right (100 000 Hz → a few millimetres;
340/100 000 = 3.4 mm).

**Figure scale verified.** The seven range bars fit `x = 200.05 + 150·log₁₀ f`
to within 0.1 units at all fourteen endpoints — human young 395.2→845.2 for
20→20 000, dog 473.9→898.0 for 67→45 000, bat 695.2→956.3 for 2000→110 000,
elephant 380.6→811.9 for 16→12 000, mouse 650.0→943.9 for 1000→91 000, cat
448.0→920.9 for 45→64 000. The declared "each mark is ten times the one before"
is exactly true.

**No findings on this lesson.** It is the strongest of the nine.

**Safety wording — FLAGGED FOR MIDE, not rewritten.** This lesson carries the
unit's hearing-damage content, including *"Hearing loss from noise is a
physical injury, not a tiredness… they do not grow back in humans"* in Going
further, and a foot note: *"Hearing damage from loud sound builds up quietly and
does not repair itself, so ringing ears after a night out, or finding you keep
turning the volume up, are worth mentioning to someone rather than ignoring.
You can talk to a doctor, a school nurse or any adult you trust. Childline is
free, confidential and open at any hour, on 0800 1111, and you do not have to
give your name."* Per protocol I propose no wording. Two things for Mide's eye:
the content is present and reads well to me; and rung 4 asks students to
discuss teenager-deterrent devices ("a continuous tone at about 17 000 Hz
outside the door… one reason people have argued about whether such devices
should be allowed"), which is a live safeguarding-adjacent topic — deliberate
and, I think, valuable, but his call.
