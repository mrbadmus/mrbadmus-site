## Pre-audit deep think

**Written in full before opening any page, per protocol Phase A.** Nothing below
was informed by the pages; it is what I went hunting for.

### 1. What a real KS3 class walks in believing

Static electricity is the topic where children arrive with the most confident
wrong physics, because they have all *felt* it and nobody has ever explained it
to them. The beliefs I expect in the room:

- **"Rubbing makes electricity."** Friction as a *generator* of charge rather
  than a *transferrer* of it. This is the deep one — it is the same shape as
  "burning destroys matter" in chemistry, and it survives most teaching because
  the everyday verb "charge up" (phone, battery) means *fill with a substance*.
- **"The balloon has electricity in it"** — charge as a fluid/substance stored
  in an object, which can drain away, be used up, or run out. This is the same
  substance-model that wrecks current and voltage in P8, arriving here first.
- **"Static is a weak kind of current"** / "static electricity flows slowly".
  Children have no separating principle between *charge at rest* and *charge in
  motion*; the shared word "electricity" does all the damage.
- **"Positive charge moves"** — because nothing has told them otherwise, and
  because the arrows in every circuit diagram they will ever see point the
  other way from what actually moves.
- **"Opposites attract, likes repel — and that is the whole story"**, so a
  charged rod attracting a *neutral* scrap of paper is a contradiction they
  cannot resolve and mostly do not notice. Ask a Year 8 "is the paper charged?"
  and you get a confident "yes, negative" with no mechanism.
- **"Charged means it has more stuff"** — a positively charged object is
  imagined as having *gained something*, not lost something.
- **"Insulators don't have charges in them at all"** (rather than: their charges
  cannot move). This is the single most damaging insulator misconception,
  because it makes charging an insulator by rubbing literally impossible in the
  child's model.
- **Lightning is fire / lightning comes down / lightning is attracted to metal
  because metal is magnetic.** Magnetism–electricity conflation is rife at KS3
  and P10 (magnetism) sits right next door in this course.
- **Shock from a car door means the car "held" electricity**, rather than *you*
  charged by sliding on the seat.
- **Earthing = "the ground soaks it up"** with no sense that electrons move
  along a path.

### 2. Wrong models a *well-meaning* explanation installs

These are the ones I am most worried about, because a page can be entirely
accurate sentence-by-sentence and still leave one of these behind:

1. **The symmetric-transfer model.** "One object gains positive charge and the
   other gains negative charge" is *true as an outcome* and *catastrophic as a
   mechanism*: stated without naming electrons as the only movers, the child
   builds a picture in which pluses go one way and minuses go the other. Every
   subsequent question ("which way did the protons go?") then has an answer.
   **Protons are locked in the nucleus and do not move in rubbing. Ever.**
2. **Charge creation.** "Rubbing produces charge", "friction creates a charge",
   "generates static". Even as a loose verb this is an S1 in this topic, because
   conservation of charge is the whole intellectual content of lesson 1.
3. **Net-charge-from-nowhere in a drawing.** A before/after animation showing a
   cloth with 5 minus signs and a rod with 5 plus signs *appearing*, rather than
   5 minus signs *moving across* leaving 5 unbalanced pluses behind. If the
   before-state does not show BOTH objects neutral-because-balanced (equal + and
   −), the conservation story is not on screen at all.
4. **Charge as paint on the surface.** Not fatal at KS3 (static charge genuinely
   does sit on the surface of an insulator where it was put) but it becomes
   wrong the moment conductors appear.
5. **"Electrons are rubbed off"** — the abrasion image. Electrons transfer by
   contact, and rubbing simply multiplies contact area and points; nothing is
   scraped. Minor, but "rubbed off like dust" is a real classroom image.
6. **Attraction-only induction, unexplained.** A page that demonstrates the
   attracted-paper/attracted-water-stream party trick *without* the
   polarisation/induction mechanism has actively taught that the paper must have
   been charged. This is the fudge I most expect to find, because it is the one
   almost every KS3 textbook makes.
7. **Field lines as objects.** Field lines are a *drawing convention*, not
   physical strings. A page that never says "these lines are our drawing, not
   real things in the air" leaves children thinking a charge has visible spokes.
8. **The field as "the region around a charge"** full stop — with no mention
   that it is defined by *what would happen to another charge placed there*.
   The examinable definition is a region where a charge experiences a force.
9. **"Static electricity is dangerous/harmless"** stated flatly either way.
   Both are wrong: a spark from a jumper is harmless; the same physics empties a
   fuel tanker into a fireball.

### 3. Where AQA/physics language differs from everyday language

Words I will check are **defined at first use, and flagged where the everyday
meaning collides**:

- **charge** — everyday: what you pay, or what you do to a phone battery
  overnight. Physics: a property of matter, measured in coulombs, that comes in
  two kinds. "Charge the balloon" and "charge the phone" are different verbs and
  a child will not notice.
- **field** — everyday: a grassy area, or a subject you study. Physics: a region
  where a force is felt. The brief flags this explicitly and it is the number
  one candidate for an unglossed collision word in lesson 3.
- **attract / repel** — mostly safe, but "attract" carries a social meaning.
- **neutral** — everyday: not taking sides. Physics: equal amounts of positive
  and negative, NOT "no charge at all". A page that says "neutral means it has
  no charge" has planted a misconception that makes induction impossible to
  teach next lesson.
- **conductor** — the everyday meaning (orchestra, bus) is harmless; the risk is
  P8's definition and P9's definition drifting apart.
- **discharge** — sounds like "empty out a container".
- **earth / earthing / grounded** — everyday "the ground". Needs the
  path-for-electrons meaning.
- **static** — everyday: not moving (also: radio hiss, "static" on a screen).
  The correct connection ("charge that stays put, unlike current") is available
  and worth making explicitly.
- **induce / induction** — likely too much for KS3 as a word; if used, must be
  glossed, and must not collide with P10's electromagnetic induction. If both
  units use "induced" for different phenomena, that is a cross-unit consistency
  finding.
- **insulator** — collides with the thermal meaning taught elsewhere in KS3
  physics (P1/P2 energy). "Insulator" for heat and "insulator" for charge are
  different properties of the same materials list, mostly but not always
  agreeing.
- **force** — everyday: effort/strength. Already carried in from P3.

### 4. What diagrams of this topic classically get wrong

- **Charge symbols drawn as a count that does not balance.** Rod +3, cloth −5.
  Trivially checkable and surprisingly common.
- **Protons drawn as mobile**, or plus signs animated moving across the gap.
- **Both objects drawn blank before rubbing** rather than showing balanced +/−
  — so "neutral" reads as "empty".
- **Field lines with no arrowheads.** Half the point of the drawing is the
  direction.
- **Field lines pointing the wrong way** — the convention is *from positive to
  negative*, i.e. the direction of the force on a small **positive** test
  charge. Around a lone negative charge they point radially *inwards*.
- **Field lines that cross.** Physically impossible — a crossing means the force
  at that point has two directions. Around a dipole pair, badly drawn lines
  cross constantly. **This is a check I can only do on the actual rendered
  geometry, not the prose.**
- **Field lines that stop in mid-air** for no reason, or that do not meet a
  conductor's surface at right angles.
- **Uniform-field parallel plates drawn with lines fanning** rather than
  parallel.
- **Line density not tracking field strength** — lines evenly spaced far from
  the charge, so "closer lines = stronger" is asserted in text and denied by
  the drawing (the exact SYS-6 shape from the chemistry audit).
- **A charged rod attracting paper drawn with the paper's own charges unshown**,
  so the induction story has no picture.
- **Lightning drawn striking upward/downward without the charge separation**, or
  a cloud drawn uniformly negative when the standard model has a positive top.

### 5. The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"When you rub the two objects together, one becomes positively charged and
> the other becomes negatively charged."**

That sentence is on a thousand worksheets. It is not false — but taught alone it
is *the* installer of the two-way-transfer model, because it describes the
outcome while carefully saying nothing about the mechanism, and children fill
the gap with symmetry. The correct sentence is:

> **"Electrons move from one object to the other. The object that GAINS
> electrons becomes negatively charged; the object that LOSES electrons is left
> positively charged, because its protons are now unbalanced. No protons move,
> and no charge is made — it is only moved."**

I will read every sentence of `charging-by-rubbing` against that standard, and
every caption and animation frame in the unit against "which particle moved?".

Runners-up, both worth the same scrutiny:
- **"Only insulators can be charged by rubbing"** — stated without the *why*
  (charge put on a conductor is immediately conducted away through you to
  earth), it is a rule to memorise instead of a consequence to derive. And it is
  not even strictly true: an *insulated* conductor charges fine. KS3 does not
  need that caveat, but the page must not close the door in a way that
  contradicts P8.
- **"Like charges repel, unlike charges attract"** — complete as a statement
  about *two charged objects*, and silently incomplete about the neutral case,
  which is the case every demonstration in the topic actually shows.

### 6. Where a simulation could teach the wrong model while working perfectly

1. A rubbing animation where **plus signs move**, or where signs appear/vanish
   rather than translating across the gap.
2. A rubbing animation that is **not conservative on the count** — 6 electrons
   leave the cloth, 4 arrive on the rod.
3. A charge-pair force sim where **the neutral option is missing or inert**, so
   the induction case can never be reached — the "no reaction is a result"
   failure from C9's deep think, transposed.
4. A force sim where **attraction and repulsion look identical in magnitude at
   all separations**, so the inverse-square-ish intuition ("closer = stronger")
   is never available — or worse, where the force is drawn *constant* with
   distance while the text claims it grows.
5. A field-line renderer where **the line count is fixed** regardless of charge
   magnitude, so "more charge = stronger field = more lines" is contradicted on
   screen.
6. A field sim where a **test charge is dragged and the force arrow does not
   rotate to lie along the line**, or points the wrong way for a negative test
   charge.
7. An electroscope/leaf sim that **rises for one charge sign and not the other**
   — an electroscope responds to magnitude, not sign.
8. Any sim where **discharging** is shown as the charge "fading away" rather
   than electrons going somewhere.
9. A lightning sim that is **exciting and mechanically silent** — pretty bolt,
   no charge separation, no path, no earth.

### 7. Fairness / sequencing watch

- Lesson 1 must establish electrons-as-the-movers before lesson 2 can explain
  induction honestly. If lesson 2's neutral case needs "electrons in the paper
  shift", lesson 1 must have said electrons can shift *within* a material too —
  which is a genuinely different idea from transferring *between* materials, and
  is the join most likely to be missing.
- Lesson 3 (fields) needs lesson 2's force rule. Does it restate it or assume?
- Does anything here need P8 (circuits/current)? P9 is Year-ordered where in the
  taught skeleton? If lesson 1 leans on "conductor" and "insulator" as known,
  P8's conductors-and-insulators lesson must come first — and if it does not,
  that is a prerequisite defect. **Explicit cross-check ordered by the brief.**
- Ladder rungs: any rung about coulombs, electric field strength, `E = F/Q`,
  or Van de Graaff internals would be KS4/A-level creep.

### 8. Safety, flagged never rewritten

Static is one of the few KS3 topics with a genuine hazard register:
- **sparks and flammable vapour** — fuel tankers, aircraft refuelling, grain
  silos, flour mills;
- **lightning** — what to do, and the "rubber tyres save you" myth (they do not;
  the metal shell does);
- **Van de Graaff generators** in school — real, common, and the reason a school
  physics technician has a risk assessment;
- **electric shock** — the distinction between a static spark (high voltage,
  tiny energy, harmless) and mains (the killer). A page that says "static is
  just a small shock, don't worry" next to a page about circuits is a
  safeguarding-adjacent wording problem.
Anything missing or thin here is flagged **for Mide**, with no wording drafted.

**Persona 1 — average Year 8, 390px phone.**

The comb and the water is the right hook and the options are properly
tempting — "a thin thread of charge travelling from the comb to the water" is
what I would have said. The reveal ("Nothing crosses the gap… Pump the air out
and the effect is unchanged") is a genuinely startling sentence and it landed.

The three explainers are dense but readable, and the second one does the whole
job in two sentences: a field arrow points the way a *small positive charge*
would be pushed, so arrows point away from positive and towards negative, and a
negative charge feels the force the other way. That convention is then printed
across the bottom of the drawing at every state — `EVERY ARROW POINTS THE WAY A
SMALL POSITIVE CHARGE WOULD BE PUSHED` — which is exactly where a convention
belongs.

The field map is the best instrument in the unit. Four arrangements, a test
point you slide along, and four readouts including — the touch that makes it
teach — separate tiles for what a small **positive** and a small **negative**
charge would do at the same point. Sliding the test point onto a charge gives
"no value here" and says why. The null point between two positives is a place
the slider genuinely stops on, and pressing "Two positives" lands you on it
immediately; one step either way and the nearer charge wins, exactly as the note
promises.

**Friction log:**

1. **The direction tile contradicted itself.** Dipole, test point at the far
   left: "THE FIELD AT YOUR POINT / **to the left** / *from the positive charge
   towards the negative one*". The negative charge was on my right. I stopped and
   re-read the map to check I had not misunderstood the arrows. → P9-14.
2. **"which is why there is no opposite of mass"** — I read the gravity card
   twice trying to work out which fact was explaining which. → P9-15.
3. Minor: the "4 on this scale" reading label slightly overlaps the test-point
   arrow at the extreme left of the map. Legible, noted, not filed.

Nothing boring; I ran all four arrangements across the full slider willingly.
Rung 4 (why a car is safe from lightning, and it is not the tyres) is the best
transfer task in the unit.

**Persona 2 — examiner's re-read.**

This is a strong, honest treatment of a topic that is normally either skipped or
taught as decoration. Checking the specific things the brief asked for:

- **Field defined as a region where a charge feels a force** — yes, and better
  than the standard formula: "a charged object fills the space around it with an
  electric field… anything charged that finds itself in that space feels a force
  from the field where it is standing." The field-is-there-first point is made
  explicitly and then confronted again in *Think again* (`CHRG-09`).
- **Arrowheads** — every grid arrow carries a two-stroke head; verified in the
  emitted path, not just visually.
- **Direction** — computed from `k = q/r³` per charge and summed, so it is the
  true superposed direction at every sample, not a stylisation. Away from
  positive, towards negative, verified at both single-charge setups and at both
  ends of the dipole.
- **Do any lines cross?** No — and structurally cannot: the map is a grid of
  independent sampled vectors, not continuous field lines, so the
  crossing-lines defect that dogs hand-drawn dipole figures is designed out.
  Worth recording as a deliberate strength.
- **Conductors at right angles** — no conductors appear on the map, so the rule
  does not arise. Not a gap at KS3.
- **Line density vs strength** — the convention used is arrow *length*, not
  spacing, and the lead says so ("which way… and how hard"). Spacing is a fixed
  grid and is never claimed to mean anything. Consistent.
- **"Field" flagged as a collision word** — **no.** The everyday meaning is never
  named. This is the one word the brief singled out and the lesson does not warn
  about it. It is however defined immediately and unambiguously at first use, and
  the vocabulary card is precise, so I am recording it as observed rather than
  filing it: the risk the brief anticipated (a child importing "a grassy area")
  is not realised on a page that says "the state a charged object puts the space
  around it into".
- **Null point** — taught as *cancellation*, not weakness, in the note, the
  vocabulary card and rung 2's feedback. That distinction is worth marks at A
  level and is nailed here at KS3.

The `convention_note` is the best of the three: it declares the relative scale,
the arrow clamp, the blanked near-field, and — unprompted — that the test point
runs along the one line where the field happens to be horizontal.

## Safety wording — flagged for Mide, never rewritten

No P9 lesson carries a `safety_note`. Two of the three absences are defensible
and one is worth his eye. Per protocol I have drafted no wording.

1. **`electric-fields` — lightning. The one that needs his decision.** The
   stretch block *Going further* tells students "you are safe inside a car in a
   thunderstorm, and it is not the tyres… the outside surface takes the whole
   strike and the inside stays quiet", and rung 4 requires them to reproduce that
   reasoning ("Says the tyres are irrelevant — the protection is the metal shell
   surrounding you"). This is real-world safety information about a hazard that
   kills people in the UK most summers, given to 12-year-olds with no safety
   framing of any kind — no statement of what the claim does *not* cover (a
   convertible, a fibreglass boat, an open tractor cab, touching metal inside the
   car), and no line about what to do in a storm when you are not in a car. The
   physics as written is correct. Whether it ships without a safety line is
   Mide's gate alone. Note that the 21 Aug 2026 ruling recorded in the lesson's
   own docstring — "no Childline block anywhere in P9" — is about the
   *safeguarding-disclosure* block and does not speak to a practical safety note;
   the docstring's reasoning ("safety information a student is being GIVEN, not a
   risk they are being asked to disclose") is an argument about Childline, not
   about `safety_note`.
2. **`charging-by-rubbing` — sparks and fuel vapour.** Rung 4 asks students to
   explain why a road tanker is earthed before pumping, and its success criteria
   name a spark igniting fuel vapour. Nothing here invites a student to try
   anything, and the lesson's own practical is dry insulators — but the page
   discusses ignition of fuel with no safety line at all, and the *Going further*
   block ends on carpet-and-door-handle shocks, which some students will
   deliberately reproduce on each other. Mide's call whether either warrants a
   line.
3. **`forces-between-charges` — deliberate absence, and I concur.** Balloons,
   jumpers and modelled spheres on insulating stands. The stretch's industrial
   examples (electrostatic paint spraying, precipitators) are described, never
   proposed. No hazard.
4. **Van de Graaff generators are never mentioned in the unit.** Recording this
   because it is the piece of apparatus most likely to appear in a real KS3
   static lesson and the one with a genuine risk assessment; its absence means
   the unit carries no obligation it has not met, but a teacher may bring it into
   the room anyway. Input for Mide, not a defect.
