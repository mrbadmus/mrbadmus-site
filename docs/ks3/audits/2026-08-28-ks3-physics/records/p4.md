## Pre-audit deep think

Written in full BEFORE opening any page, source file or built HTML. P4 is the
unit I would nominate as the highest-risk in the whole of KS3 science. Forces
is the topic where the gap between what a child arrives believing and what
physics says is widest, where the wrong belief is *reinforced daily by lived
experience*, and where a well-meaning sentence installs a wrong model that
survives to A-level. Almost every KS3 forces lesson I have ever read contains
at least one sentence that a physicist would refuse to sign.

### The misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

**The motion family (the big one).**
1. **Motion requires a continuous force.** The Aristotelian default. "It's
   moving, so something must be pushing it." Every child holds this, because
   every real object they have ever pushed *did* stop when they stopped
   pushing. The physics answer (friction was the unbalanced force) is
   counter-intuitive and must be *argued*, not asserted.
2. **Impetus / force-as-a-possession.** "The ball has a force in it." Force is
   treated as a substance an object carries and gradually spends. Any phrasing
   like "the force of the ball", "the force runs out", "it loses its force" is
   this misconception in print.
3. **Force belongs to one object.** Force is an interaction between two things.
   A child names "the force of the box" rather than "the push of the hand on
   the box". Naming convention matters: `<type> of <A> on <B>`.
4. **Balanced forces ⇒ at rest.** The single most-taught-wrong idea in the
   topic (see below). Balanced forces mean *no change* in motion — stationary
   OR constant speed in a straight line.
5. **A resultant force causes motion (rather than a CHANGE of motion).**
   Corollary: no resultant ⇒ no motion. Also: "acceleration" read as
   "speeding up" only, when it also covers slowing and turning.
6. **Faster ⇒ bigger force.** Speed is read off as a proxy for force.

**The contact family.**
7. **"The table doesn't push back."** Passive, inanimate objects are believed
   incapable of exerting a force — a table is just "in the way". This is the
   named target of the whole normal-contact-force idea, and I expect it to be
   the most likely thing this unit gets wrong by *omission*: a book-on-table
   diagram drawn with only a weight arrow. If weight is the only arrow drawn
   on a resting object, the page has taught that a resting object has an
   unbalanced force on it — a flat self-contradiction with the
   balanced-forces lesson.
8. **Friction is only "bad", and only "slows things down".** Friction is also
   what lets you walk, what lets a car drive, what holds a knot, what stops a
   ladder sliding. A page that defines friction as "a force that slows moving
   things down" and stops there has taught a half-truth that fails the moment
   a child is asked how walking works. Also: static friction exists (things
   that are not moving have friction acting on them); smooth surfaces still
   have friction; friction is not caused only by "roughness" at KS3-visible
   scale.
9. **Air resistance is "wind" / is constant / doesn't depend on speed.** The
   speed dependence is the entire mechanism of terminal velocity. If a page
   asserts terminal velocity without asserting "drag grows as you go faster",
   the explanation is unsupported.
10. **Terminal velocity: "gravity runs out" / "weight gets smaller" / "air
    resistance becomes bigger than weight".** All three are wrong. At terminal
    velocity drag EQUALS weight — the forces are balanced, and the object keeps
    falling at a steady speed. (If drag exceeded weight the object would slow
    down, which is what actually happens just after a parachute opens — a
    lovely teaching moment usually botched.) Weight never changes on the way
    down.
11. **Heavier things fall faster.** In air they sometimes do — for reasons of
    drag, not gravity — so a page must be careful which it is claiming.
12. **Upthrust vs water resistance conflated.** Floating is upthrust
    (buoyancy); water resistance is drag. "Things float because they're
    lighter than water" is a density statement smuggled in as a weight one.

**The springs family.**
13. **Extension read as LENGTH.** The classic ruinous error: a student plots
    total length against force, gets a straight line that does NOT pass through
    the origin, and concludes the spring "already had force in it". Extension =
    stretched length − original (natural/unstretched) length. If the page's
    table or graph ever says "length" where it means "extension", that alone
    is a KS4-mark-costing S1.
14. **Hooke's law is always true.** It stops being true at the **limit of
    proportionality**. If the graph is drawn as an infinite straight line with
    no bend and no named limit, the page has taught a false universal.
    (Also worth watching: limit of proportionality ≠ elastic limit; they are
    different points and conflating them is a KS4 error.)
15. **Springs "get weaker"** rather than being permanently deformed
    (inelastic/plastic deformation).
16. **Gradient of a force–extension graph.** Which way up? Force on y and
    extension on x gives gradient = spring constant k in N/m. Force on x gives
    1/k. A page that says "the gradient is the spring constant" without saying
    which quantity is on which axis is teaching a coin-flip.

**The moments family.**
17. **"Moment" everyday meaning** — an instant of time. A word whose physics
    meaning is unrelated. Must be flagged explicitly at first use.
18. **A moment is a force.** It is not; it is the *turning effect* of a force.
    "Turning force" is common classroom shorthand and it is wrong.
19. **Distance from the pivot measured along the beam regardless of the force's
    direction.** The distance is the *perpendicular* distance from the pivot to
    the **line of action** of the force. At KS3 the forces are almost always
    vertical and the beam horizontal, so the two coincide — but if the unit
    ever draws an angled force or a bent spanner and still measures along the
    arm, it is wrong. Even in the easy case, the word "perpendicular" should
    appear, or the KS4 version breaks.
20. **Units of a moment.** N m (newton-metres). Watch for `Nm` (acceptable at
    AQA but inconsistent if the unit elsewhere writes N m), `nm` (nanometres —
    outright wrong), and above all **N/m**, which is the spring constant and
    will be sitting two lessons away in this very unit. A unit that uses N/m
    for both is a genuine S1 collision.
21. **Pivot not identified**, or "balance point" and "pivot" used
    interchangeably when they are not the same thing.

**The non-contact family.**
22. **Weight measured in kg.** Any "weight of 5 kg" is an immediate S1.
23. **Gravity needs air** ("no air in space, so no gravity"), and
    **astronauts float because there's no gravity** (they are in free fall).
24. **Weight = mass × gravity.** Sloppy. Gravity is not a quantity. It is
    weight = mass × **gravitational field strength** (`g`, in N/kg). At KS3
    `g` is usually 10 N/kg; AQA GCSE uses 9.8 N/kg. Whichever this course
    picks, it must pick ONE and hold it across P4 and P12 — with the same
    units and the same words. **Cross-check target: P12's mass-vs-weight and
    gravity-and-weight lessons.**
25. **Magnets attract all metals.** Only iron, nickel, cobalt (and steel).
    Aluminium and copper are the classroom counter-examples.
26. **Gravity is a one-way pull.** The Earth pulls the apple; the apple also
    pulls the Earth, equally. This is where Newton's third law is honest at
    KS3 without being named.
27. **Non-contact means "no force until they touch"** — the field acts across
    a gap, and the force gets weaker with distance.

**Newton's third law (whether or not the unit names it).**
28. **Third-law pairs drawn on the same object** — this is the conflation with
    balanced forces, and it is why the two topics must never share a diagram.
    A third-law pair acts on TWO DIFFERENT objects, is always the same type of
    force, and never balances anything (because you cannot balance forces on
    two different bodies). If P4 avoids the third law entirely that is a
    legitimate KS3 choice — but I must check nothing *accidentally* implies it
    (e.g. "the table pushes back with an equal force, so they balance" is the
    conflation in one sentence: the table's push on the book and the book's
    weight are NOT a third-law pair, they are two forces on one object that
    happen to be equal).

### Wrong mental models a well-meaning explanation can install

- **"Forces come in pairs that cancel out"** — muddles third law and balance.
- **"An object at rest has no forces on it"** — the zero-resultant-means-zero-
  forces error.
- **Arrow length as decoration.** If arrows are drawn all the same length while
  the text says "the bigger force wins", the drawing has taught that arrow
  length carries no information — and then every free-body diagram the child
  meets for the next five years is unreadable to them.
- **"Gravity pulls things down"** — down is a local convenience; gravity pulls
  toward the centre of the Earth. Harmless at KS3 *unless* the unit also does
  planets/space, in which case it collides.
- **Streamlining explained as "cutting through the air"** rather than reducing
  the drag force.
- **A sim that shows the right numbers for the wrong reason** — e.g. a
  terminal-velocity sim whose speed curve is scripted rather than computed from
  the force balance, so the child sees a plausible graph while the causal story
  (drag grows → resultant shrinks → acceleration shrinks → speed levels) is
  never visible on screen. This is the P4 analogue of chemistry's SYS-5, and
  the thing I most want to test.

### Where AQA's language differs from everyday language

**force** (everyday: strength, effort, compulsion) · **weight** (everyday: the
kg number on the scales) · **moment** (everyday: an instant) · **normal**
(everyday: usual; physics: perpendicular) · **reaction** (everyday: a response
in time) · **tension**, **compression** · **elastic** (everyday: a rubber band)
· **extension** · **resultant** · **balanced** (everyday: equal-looking) ·
**drag**, **upthrust**, **streamlined** · **pivot**, **fulcrum** ·
**field** · **contact / non-contact** · **attract / repel** · **friction**
(everyday: disagreement) · **acceleration** (everyday: speeding up only).

Every one of these must be defined at first use and not merely used.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

1. Force arrows not proportional to magnitude.
2. Arrows not starting at the object (floating arrows, or arrows starting at
   the ground and pointing at the object).
3. **Missing normal contact force** on a resting object.
4. Arrows labelled "force" or "gravity" instead of a force NAME
   ("weight", "air resistance", "normal contact force", "push of the hand").
5. A forward "motion" / "moving force" arrow drawn on a coasting object —
   drawing misconception 1 directly.
6. Two arrows of visibly different length labelled as balanced (or vice versa).
7. Weight arrow not drawn from the centre of mass.
8. On a spring diagram, the measuring line spanning the whole length instead of
   the extension.
9. Seesaw drawn with the pivot ambiguous, or with the distances not marked.

### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"When the forces on an object are balanced, it stays still."**

It is on countless worksheets and it is wrong by omission — balanced forces
also describe an object moving at a constant speed in a straight line, which is
the entire content of Newton's first law and the only route out of
misconception 1. Its twin, equally common:

> **"Friction is a force that slows moving objects down."**

Also wrong by omission, and it is the sentence that makes "how do you walk?"
unanswerable. My third nomination, specific to this unit's lesson 6:

> **"At terminal velocity, air resistance is bigger than weight."**

They are **equal**. I will grep for all three shapes explicitly.

### Unit-structure risks specific to this slot

- **Ordering.** The brief names the reading order as `what-a-force-is →
  what-forces-do-to-motion → drawing-and-adding-forces → balanced-and-
  unbalanced → …`, but the authored files are numbered `01 what_a_force_is,
  02 drawing_and_adding_forces, 03 balanced_and_unbalanced, 04 what_forces_do_
  to_motion, 05 friction, 06 air_and_water_resistance, 07 moments, 08 springs_
  and_hookes_law, 09 non_contact_forces` — i.e. two pairs are transposed
  (motion↔drawing/balanced, and moments↔springs). Whichever order the *page
  chain* serves, I must check that no lesson depends on a lesson served after
  it. Specifically: does `drawing-and-adding-forces` need "resultant" before
  `balanced-and-unbalanced` defines it? Does `balanced-and-unbalanced` need the
  motion lesson's Newton-1 content, or supply it?
- **Nine lessons is the largest unit.** Friction, air/water resistance and
  balanced/unbalanced overlap heavily; the risk is the same explanation given
  three times in three slightly different, slightly inconsistent forms.
- **`non-contact-forces` last** means gravity/weight arrive at the END of the
  unit, after eight lessons that will certainly have used the word "weight".
  If weight is used for eight lessons and only defined in lesson 9, that is a
  prerequisite inversion — and P12 (mass vs weight, gravity and weight) then
  teaches it a third time. All three must agree on `g`, its units, and its
  wording.
- **Springs is a calculation lesson.** It needs a worked route, faded practice,
  and a way back for a stuck child. Chemistry's audit found scaffolding gaps
  exactly here.

### What I will test that the seeds do not name

- Whether any instrument's *caption* survives its own edge states (SYS-5).
- Whether every arrow the unit draws anywhere — in a canvas, an SVG, or a

## springs-and-hookes-law

**Persona 1 walkthrough.** The strongest lesson opening in the unit: two
readings, a pattern, and a demand to predict at ten times the load. The spring
plot is the most ambitious instrument here — a spring, a clamped ruler, a
graph, a record button and a dashed straight line — and it is genuinely
investigative: I recorded every reading from 0 to 10 N and watched the points
leave the line at 7 N. The per-state notes are excellent, especially at the
limit ("*This is the last reading that obeys Hooke's law: the limit of
proportionality for this spring. **Nothing about the spring looks any
different.**"). The safety note is the most detailed in KS3 physics and reads
like a teacher wrote it.

**Friction log.**

**Persona 2 (examiner).** The core science is right and is the right selection:
**extension is defined as stretched length − natural length and distinguished
from length by name, in the explainer, in the relationship block, in the key
fact, in the key note, in the vocabulary and in a dedicated THINK AGAIN** —
which is the single most common cause of a wrong Hooke's-law graph and is

made reachable on the instrument. The graph's axes are labelled (extension
