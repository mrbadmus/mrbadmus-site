### Where this unit sits in the national curriculum

KS3 programme of study, *Motion and forces → Describing motion*, is three
bullets and only three:

1. speed and the quantitative relationship between average speed, distance
   and time (speed = distance ÷ time);
2. the representation of a journey on a distance–time graph;
3. relative motion: trains and cars passing one another.

P3's three lessons map one-to-one onto them. So **"only three lessons" is not
prima facie a coverage gap** — it is exactly the statutory slot. The real
prerequisite question is different and I will test it directly: *acceleration*
and *speed–time graphs* are NOT in this sub-strand (acceleration is KS4
ground), so if P4 Forces later leans on "acceleration", "resultant force
changes the speed", or reads a velocity–time graph, then P3 is the unit that
should have laid the ground and did not. I will check what P4 assumes rather
than asserting a gap from the lesson count.

### The misconceptions a real KS3 class walks in with

**Speed.**
- *Faster = went further.* Children compare journeys on distance alone and
  ignore time (or compare on time alone and ignore distance). Any activity
  that can be scored by "bigger number wins" without holding one variable
  fixed teaches this.
- *Speed is a property of the object, not of the journey.* "A cheetah's speed
  is 30 m/s" — as if it were a colour.
- *The formula triangle IS the physics.* d/v/t triangles are a memory trick
  that replaces rearrangement with a covered-corner ritual. A child who only
  ever meets the triangle cannot rearrange anything they have not been given a
  triangle for. I will check whether `v = d ÷ t` is ever *rearranged with a
  worked route* (multiply both sides by t, and why) or only shown finished.
- *Average speed = the speed it was going.* The deepest one. A journey with a
  stop in it has an average speed the object may never have been travelling at
  in any recognisable sense, and children read "the car's speed was 12 m/s" as
  a fact about every instant. **A page that computes distance ÷ time over a
  journey containing a stop and calls the result "the speed" is an S1**, not a
  wording quibble — it is the misconception, printed.
- *"Per" is opaque.* "Metres per second" is read as one word. The unit is a
  sentence: *how many metres in each one second*. If the page never unpacks
  "per", the unit is decoration.
- *Unit sloppiness:* stripping the units during the arithmetic and gluing them
  back on at the end; mixing m/s with km/h or mph without conversion; a
  division like 100 ÷ 3 printed to eight decimal places by a computer that
  does not know about significant figures.

**Speed vs velocity, distance vs displacement.** At KS3 the honest position is
that *speed* is the quantity and *velocity* is speed with a direction, met
properly at GCSE. The failure modes are both directions: (a) the page uses the
word **velocity** as a synonym for speed (an error a GCSE examiner will punish
later, and one that makes lesson 3 incoherent), or (b) the page *needs*
direction — which relative motion absolutely does, since two trains at 100
km/h towards each other and two at 100 km/h in convoy are the same speeds and
opposite outcomes — and refuses to give the class any language for it. I will
watch for **velocity used loosely** and for **relative motion done with
direction-free speeds**.

Similarly *distance* vs *displacement*: on a there-and-back journey, distance
travelled never decreases; distance *from the start* does. This one bites in
lesson 2 (below).

**Distance–time graphs.** This is the graph-literacy lesson and the single
highest-risk page in the unit.
- **The one sentence most often taught wrong in British KS3 classrooms is what
  a horizontal line means.** On a distance–time graph a flat line means the
  object is **stationary** — distance is not changing. Half the country's
  non-specialist teaching says "a flat line means it is going at a constant
  speed", importing the speed–time graph's reading. Constant speed on a d–t
  graph is a *straight sloped* line. I will read every sentence, every caption,
  every ladder rung, every feedback string and every instrument label on this
  page against that, and I will look for the reverse error too (a straight
  slope described as "speeding up").
- **The graph read as a picture of the route.** The classic: a sloped line is
  read as "going uphill", a steeper line as "a steeper hill", a flat line as
  "flat ground", a line that comes back down as "coming down the other side".
  Any animation that moves an object *along the shape of the plotted line*
  installs this permanently while behaving perfectly. This is my top candidate
  for "correct simulation, wrong model".
- **The descending line's axis.** If a journey returns home and the line comes
  back down to zero, the y-axis cannot honestly read "distance travelled" — it
  is *distance from home*. Textbooks mislabel this constantly. I will read the
  actual axis strings.
- **Gradient = speed, computed or asserted?** Steeper = faster is the
  qualitative half. The quantitative half is rise ÷ run with units attached,
  and a worked route to it. If gradient is only ever "steeper means faster",
  the lesson has taught half a skill; if the number appears with no route, it
  is a scaffolding gap.
- **Axes without units.** Quantity AND unit on both axes, every figure.
- Reading the y-value at a point and calling it the speed; assuming the
  crossing point of two lines means "same speed" (it means same place at the
  same time).

**Relative motion.**
- *There is a real, absolute speed, and everything else is an illusion.* The
  lesson exists to kill this. If it teaches "you think you are still, but you
  are really moving at 1000 km/h", it has swapped one absolute frame for
  another and taught the misconception in a lab coat. The honest sentence is
  that **there is no such thing as absolute rest**: "at rest" is only ever
  *relative to* something, and the something must be named every time.
- *"Relative to" as a floating phrase.* If the page never says **frame of
  reference** (or names an observer explicitly and consistently), "relative to
  the train" is a phrase children parrot without a referent.
- *Speeds always add.* Opposite directions subtract-as-signed / add-as-
  magnitudes; same direction subtract. A page that only ever does the
  head-on case installs "you add them".
- Danger of the pronoun: "it looks like it is going backwards" — *what* looks
  like *what*, to *whom*.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong
- Motion diagrams (dot traces) with evenly spaced dots used to depict a change
  of speed, or unevenly spaced dots used for constant speed.
- d–t graphs with unlabelled axes, or units on one axis only, or a y-axis
  reading "distance" on a return journey.
- Grid lines that do not line up with the plotted points, so a "read off the
  graph" instruction cannot actually be followed.
- Relative-motion drawings with arrows and no stated observer, or two trains
  drawn passing with the arrow lengths not matching the stated speeds.
- Speedometers used as a picture of *average* speed.

### Words whose everyday meaning differs, or that trip an 11-year-old
speed · velocity · average · constant · uniform · relative · stationary
(everyday: writing paper) · at rest · distance vs displacement · gradient /
slope (everyday: a hill) · rate · per · journey · frame of reference ·
instant / instantaneous · scale (graph axis vs size).
Any of these used before it is glossed is a finding, quoted exactly.

### Where a simulation could teach the wrong model while running perfectly
1. An animated runner whose sprite travels **along the plotted line** —
   graph-as-picture, installed by the best-behaved code in the unit.
2. A "stopped" segment during which the animated object still visibly creeps.
3. A speed slider whose caption is fixed ("the runner is speeding up") and
   still says so at the slider's zero, or at equal speeds.
4. An average-speed calculator that prints the answer with no route, so the
   instrument does the physics and the child does the pressing.
5. A relative-motion bench whose caption is composed once ("moving backwards
   relative to you") and is therefore false at the equal-speed edge, where
   relative speed is zero and neither train moves relative to the other.
6. A gradient tool that reports a speed to absurd precision (0.8333333 m/s).
7. Any d–t graph instrument that lets the line go *backwards in time* (x
   decreasing) without refusing it — time only runs one way, and a child who
   draws a vertical line has claimed teleportation.

5. Is average vs instantaneous named, and is any average called "the speed"?

## Coverage and prerequisites — the "only three lessons" question

Asked directly, and the answer is that **three lessons is right, and nothing P4
needs is missing.**

The KS3 programme of study's *Motion and forces → Describing motion* sub-strand
is exactly three statements: speed and the quantitative relationship between
average speed, distance and time; the representation of a journey on a
distance–time graph; relative motion, trains and cars passing one another. P3's
three lessons map one-to-one onto them (`KS3.P.MOT.01/02/03`), none twice, none
outside. **Acceleration and speed–time graphs are not in this sub-strand and are
not KS3**, so their absence is a curriculum decision, not a gap.

The real test is whether P4 leans on ground P3 never laid, and it does not.
`p4-04 what-forces-do-to-motion` deliberately never says "acceleration" (its own
docstring records the decision, and `F = ma` is reserved for GCSE); it works
throughout in "start it, or speed it up / slow it down or stop it / change its
direction", and it `references: ["speed"]`. Its hardest item — a satellite
circling at a steady speed with a force on it — needs the idea that a change of
direction is a change of motion even at constant speed, and `p3-02`'s own
think-again lays exactly that ground one unit earlier ("An object going round a
perfect bend at a steady speed draws a straight line here"). `p4-03` uses
"terminal velocity" and "accelerator" only as ordinary nouns in prose.

The one genuine handover risk is the word **velocity**, which P3 rules out of its
body copy and then prints three times, undefined, in its end-matter (**P3-26**).
That is the only prerequisite-shaped item I found, and it is an S4.
