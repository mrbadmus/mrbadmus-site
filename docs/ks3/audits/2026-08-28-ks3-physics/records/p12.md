### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

**"It is summer when the Earth is closer to the Sun."** This is the topic's
defining error and it is taught wrong in two distinct ways, only one of which
most audits catch:

1. **Stated outright in words.** Rarer now — most published schemes kill it
   explicitly.
2. **Reinstated by the drawing while the words deny it.** Endemic. The orbit is
   drawn as a fat ellipse (because a circle drawn in perspective on a flat page
   looks wrong, and because an ellipse gives the illustrator room to put four
   season labels around it), the Earth is drawn largest at one end, and a child
   reads the picture as authority over the paragraph. This is exactly
   chemistry's **SYS-6** — drawings that vouch for the wrong model against
   their own lesson's words — and the fix template already exists on-site:
   c10-05's ALWAYS-VISIBLE distortion footer sitting directly under the mix
   bar, which C10's auditor named as the pattern. I will check specifically
   whether P12 uses that template or repeats c10-02's mistake (confession
   locked behind completing an instrument).

   Sub-predictions for the seasons drawing: (a) orbit eccentricity drawn
   noticeably non-circular with no "not to scale" confession WHERE the drawing
   is; (b) the Sun drawn at the centre of the drawn ellipse rather than at a
   focus, which is *also* wrong but in the opposite direction and would make
   the ellipse purely decorative; (c) Earth–Sun distance and body diameters
   drawn at incompatible scales without saying so; (d) the tilt drawn but the
   tilt DIRECTION not held fixed in space around the orbit — if the axis is
   drawn leaning "away from the Sun" at every position, the lesson has
   destroyed its own mechanism, because the whole point is that the axis
   points the same way in space all year and the Sun moves relative to it.
   (d) is the deepest and least-caught version of the error.

   Also: the honest KS3 mechanism is TWO effects, not one — the angle at which
   sunlight strikes the ground (spread over more area when low) AND the number
   of daylight hours. A page that gives only "the days are longer" or only
   "the rays are more spread out" has given half the mechanism. And the
   perihelion fact is genuinely awkward: Earth is closest to the Sun in early
   JANUARY, i.e. northern-hemisphere winter — which is the single most
   powerful piece of evidence against the distance model, and also the fact
   most likely to be stated wrongly or vaguely if the page reaches for it.

### Misconceptions a real KS3 class arrives with

**Gravity and weight**
- "There is no gravity in space." At the ISS's altitude Earth's gravitational
  field strength is still ~89% of its surface value. Astronauts float because
  they are in FREE FALL — they and the station are falling together around the
  Earth. "Weightless because there is no gravity" is S1 wherever it appears,
  and it is the most common single sentence in KS3 space resources.
- "Gravity needs air" / "gravity stops at the edge of the atmosphere." Related
  to the above and often planted by the phrase "escaped Earth's gravity".
- "Gravity only pulls downwards" — i.e. gravity as a property of the ground
  rather than a mutual force between any two masses. The KS3-honest statement
  is: every mass attracts every other mass; bigger mass → bigger force; greater
  distance → smaller force. If the unit never says "any two masses", it has
  taught gravity as a local floor effect.
- "The Moon is held by the Earth's magnetic pull." A real and common slip
  (both in student writing and, occasionally, in resources). Watch for any
  magnet language anywhere near orbits.
- Gravity as something that "pulls the Moon in" with no account of why it does
  not arrive — the orbit hand-waved. The honest KS3 account is continuous
  falling: the Moon is falling towards Earth and missing, because it also moves
  sideways. If the page just says "gravity keeps it in orbit", a child has
  learned a label, not a mechanism.
- "Heavier things fall faster." Adjacent but almost certain to surface in a
  gravity lesson, and the Moon hammer/feather demonstration is the standard
  resolution. If the page runs the hammer-and-feather line, does it say WHY
  (no air resistance), or leave it magic?

**Mass vs weight — the gate for this unit**
- Weight given in kilograms. This is the error, and it is S1: a bathroom scale
  reading in kg is a mass reading, and "my weight is 60 kg" is everyday English
  that physics deliberately overrides. AQA marks W in newtons. Any page giving
  a weight in kg, or any instrument whose weight readout carries kg, is S1.
- "Mass changes on the Moon." Mass is the amount of stuff and is invariant;
  only weight changes because g changes.
- `g` treated as "gravity" the noun rather than gravitational field strength in
  N/kg. The value: 9.8 N/kg is the physically standard figure; many KS3 schemes
  use 10 N/kg for arithmetic. Either is defensible; the unit MUST pick one and
  hold it, and it must agree with P4's non-contact-forces lesson and with
  anything P11 says about mass. If P12 says 10 and P4 says 9.8 (or vice versa),
  that is a real cross-unit finding, not pedantry — a child doing homework
  across two lessons gets two answers.
- The Moon's g: ≈1.6 N/kg, which is ~1/6 of Earth's. If the page rounds to
  "one sixth" AND gives a numeric g, check the arithmetic is consistent with
  BOTH (9.8/6 = 1.63; 10/6 = 1.67 — a page that says g_Moon = 1.6 while using
  g_Earth = 10 and claiming exactly one sixth has a small internal
  inconsistency worth naming if the numbers are then used).
- W = m × g rearrangement: does the unit show m = W/g, and are units carried
  through? (kg × N/kg = N is a genuinely satisfying cancellation and a place
  where units teach the equation — a scaffolding opportunity usually missed.)
- Newtons per kilogram vs "newtons per kilo" vs N/kg written as Nkg. Notation
  drift is likely.

**The Sun, stars and galaxies**
- "The Sun is not a star" / "the Sun is bigger than all stars". The Sun is an
  ordinary star, unremarkable except for proximity — and that IS the point.
- Hierarchy inversion: galaxy inside a solar system, "the universe is in our
  galaxy", solar system used as a synonym for galaxy. The clean order is
  moon < planet < star < solar system < galaxy < universe, and it must be
  stated in one consistent order everywhere in the unit.
- Our galaxy unnamed, or "the galaxy" used as if there were only one. It is
  the Milky Way; there are ~2×10^11 galaxies (order of magnitude only at KS3).
- "Stars are burning" — combustion language. Stars fuse hydrogen; nothing is
  burning in the chemical sense. Fusion is arguably above KS3, but if the page
  reaches for it, "burning" is the wrong word and "nuclear fusion of hydrogen
  into helium" is the right one, stated once without machinery.
- Planets shining by their own light; stars twinkling because they are moving;
  constellations as physical groupings (they are line-of-sight coincidences at
  wildly different distances) — the last is a lovely KS3 point that is usually
  missing rather than wrong.
- Sun colour: "the Sun is yellow" (it is white; the yellow is atmospheric). Low
  priority but a nice honesty check.

**Day/night vs year**
- Rotation (24 h, day/night) conflated with orbit (365.25 d, year). The classic
  student answer "it's night because the Earth goes round the Sun" is the
  single most common conflation, and any lesson touching seasons must keep the
  two motions apart in words AND in any animation.
- "The Sun goes round the Earth" / "the Sun moves across the sky" used without
  correction — everyday language that a page can propagate carelessly.
- Moon phases caused by Earth's shadow (that is an eclipse). If phases appear
  in this unit at all, this is the trap.

**Scale and distance — Mide named this explicitly**
- A light-year given as a TIME. The classic error; a light-year is a DISTANCE:
  the distance light travels in one year, ≈9.5 × 10^12 km (9.46 trillion km).
  A definition that says "how long light takes" is S1 outright; a definition
  that says "how far light goes in a year" without saying light's speed is
  incomplete but not wrong.
- Speed of light 300,000 km/s (3 × 10^8 m/s). If the page derives the
  light-year, the arithmetic must actually work: 3×10^5 km/s × 60 × 60 × 24 ×
  365 = 9.46×10^12 km. I will check every intermediate.
- Powers of ten dumped rather than built. A 12-year-old cannot hold 10^12 as a
  quantity; they can hold "if the Sun were a football in London, the next star
  is a football in New York". The finding to look for is a FINISHED RESULT
  presented with no worked route — protocol's scaffolding gap — and no fading
  (worked → partial → independent).
- Solar-system diagrams claimed to be to scale. No diagram on a page can be to
  scale in both body size and separation; if a page claims it, that is SYS-6.
- Light-travel time as a look-back time ("you see the Sun as it was 8 minutes
  ago") — correct and powerful, but check the 8 minutes and any star figures.
  Proxima Centauri is 4.2 ly; Andromeda 2.5 million ly; Milky Way ~100,000 ly
  across.
- Astronomical unit vs light-year mixed, or km and ly used interchangeably
  without a bridge.
- "Millions" and "billions" used loosely and interchangeably. UK convention now
  matches US (10^9), but the words are still slippery for this age.

### Where AQA's language differs from everyday language
- **Weight** — everyday: how heavy, in kg. Physics: a FORCE, in newtons,
  = m × g. The single largest everyday/physics collision in the whole of KS3,
  and it lives in this unit.
- **Mass** — everyday: weight. Physics: amount of matter, kg, invariant.
- **Gravity** — everyday: a downward pull near the ground. Physics: a mutual
  attraction between any two masses.
- **Field strength** — not an everyday phrase at all; N/kg needs a gloss.
- **Star** — everyday: any bright dot in the night sky (includes planets and
  the ISS). Physics: a self-luminous fusing body.
- **Universe / space** — used interchangeably in speech.
- **Light-year** — sounds like a time to every non-physicist alive.
- **Orbit** — everyday: a circle drawn round something. Physics: a trajectory
  under gravity.
- **Weightless** — everyday: no gravity. Physics: in free fall.
- **Satellite** — everyday: a human-made box. Physics: anything in orbit,
  including the Moon.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong
1. The seasons ellipse (see above) — top of my list.
2. The tilt direction not held fixed in space around the orbit.
3. Solar-system diagrams with planets evenly spaced and comparably sized.
4. Earth–Moon separation drawn at ~5 Earth diameters when the true figure is
   ~30 (this is the most-wrong number in astronomy illustration).
5. Sun–Earth drawn with the Sun only slightly larger (true ratio ~109:1 in
   diameter).
6. Orbits drawn as flat rings viewed obliquely and then reasoned about as if
   flat — fine, but the ellipse/circle honesty note is still needed.
7. Force arrows on the Moon drawn tangentially (as motion) rather than
   radially (as gravity), or a single arrow doing both jobs.
8. Weight arrows drawn from the top of an object, or drawn in kg.
9. Star-size comparisons that are honest about size but silent about distance.

### Where a simulation could work perfectly and still teach the wrong model
- A seasons sim where dragging Earth round the orbit changes the drawn
  Earth–Sun distance visibly: correct behaviour (the labels and daylight hours
  update right) plus a wrong drawing = a child who leaves believing distance
  did it. **This is my single highest-probability S1 for the unit.**
- A weight calculator that outputs a bare number without units, or with the
  unit hard-coded in a caption that does not follow the body chosen (SYS-5
  shape: a fixed caption on a variable instrument).
- A gravity slider that lets mass or distance go to zero and produces a
  division-by-zero, `Infinity`, `NaN`, or a caption asserting "the pull is
  stronger" when the number fell.
- A planet-weight picker whose caption says "your mass changes" — or, subtler,
  one where the MASS readout changes when you switch planet. That is the whole
  gate of lesson 2 and would be S1 at the instrument.
- A light-year distance instrument that renders 10^12 as `1e+12` or
  `[object Object]`, or that rounds to something arithmetically wrong.
- A scale sim whose "to scale" claim is only true along one axis.
- An orbit sim where the Moon is dragged and simply follows the cursor —
  correct-looking, but it teaches that orbits are a track rather than a
  balance of falling and moving sideways.

### Safety (flag only, never draft — Mide's gate)
- **Looking at the Sun.** Any lesson that invites a student to observe the Sun,
  find it in the sky, use a telescope or binoculars, or photograph it needs a
