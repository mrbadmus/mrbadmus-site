## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page, any source file, or the built HTML. P8 is the unit the
brief singled out: it carries the two misconceptions Mide named by name. Electricity is
the topic where British KS3 teaching most reliably installs a wrong model that survives
to A-level, because the model is invisible and every analogy leaks.

### The two named misconceptions — what they actually look like on a page

**1. "Current gets used up."** This is not one error, it is a family, and the family
members look different on the page:

- **The consumption model.** A bulb "uses up" current; the ammeter after the bulb reads
  less than the one before. Diagnostic question literature (Driver, and the classic
  "Two ammeters" item) puts 30–50% of KS3 students on this model *after* teaching.
- **The clashing-currents model.** Current comes out of both terminals and "meets" in
  the bulb. Rarer but lethal, and it is planted by any drawing showing arrows leaving
  both ends of a cell.
- **The attenuation model.** Current is highest nearest the battery and drops as you go
  round. Planted by any diagram where arrow thickness, arrow count or arrow colour
  changes around a series loop, or by a sim that animates fewer/slower dots after a
  component.
- **The sequential model** (the deep one). The circuit acts in sequence: what happens
  downstream cannot affect upstream; adding a second bulb "after" the first does not
  change the first. Planted whenever a lesson narrates a circuit left-to-right as a
  journey — "the current leaves the cell, travels to the bulb, then …". Nearly every
  KS3 book does exactly this.
- **The energy/current conflation.** "Current is used up" is usually a garbled version
  of a TRUE statement — *energy* is transferred at the bulb. If the unit never gives
  students the correct thing that IS used up, the misconception has nowhere to go and
  regrows. **A unit that only says "current is not used up" and never says "energy is"
  has done half the job.** I will hunt for whether the true statement is supplied.

What I will check, concretely, for every lesson: every ammeter reading at every state of
every instrument; whether any series-loop state produces two unequal readings; whether
any arrow/dot animation thins, slows or reduces after a component; whether any sentence
uses "uses", "used up", "consumed", "runs out", "loses current", "less current left",
"weaker current" of current; and — the converse — whether the unit ever explicitly
*states* current is conserved, and where.

**2. "Voltage as a substance."** Also a family:

- **Voltage flows.** "Voltage flows through the bulb", "12 volts flowing round". Voltage
  is not a thing that moves; it is a difference *between two points*.
- **Voltage is in the wire / in the battery / in the current.** "The wire has 6 V in it."
  A battery does not contain volts; it maintains a pd across its terminals.
- **The battery "gives out" voltage** as an emitted quantity, symmetric with current —
  which then makes voltage feel like a second fluid.
- **Voltage as strength of current** — "high voltage means lots of current" without the
  resistance term. This one is half-true and therefore stickier.
- **Voltage as a property of one point.** The single hardest idea at KS3: pd is
  ALWAYS across two points. If the page ever says "the voltage at the bulb" instead of
  "across the bulb", that's the tell.

The instrument tell is placement: a **voltmeter must be in PARALLEL** (across the
component) and an **ammeter in SERIES** (in the loop). If any diagram, instruction,
instrument state, ladder rung or feedback line has a voltmeter in series or an ammeter
in parallel, that is S1 immediately — and it is a very common authoring slip, because
the "measuring" lesson usually draws the ammeter first and then reuses the drawing.

I will also check whether **"voltage" vs "potential difference"** is used consistently.
The AQA position: potential difference is the technical term, "voltage" is the everyday
synonym and is acceptable, but a KS3 unit that switches between them without ever saying
they are the same thing leaves students thinking they are two quantities. And "potential"
is an everyday word with a completely different meaning ("she has potential") — the page
must not assume the physics meaning is obvious.

### The other classic misconceptions of this exact unit

3. **Charge vs current conflation.** Current is the *rate of flow of charge*; 1 A = 1 C/s.
   KS3 does not need coulombs, but it does need the per-second idea, otherwise "current"
   is just a vague synonym for "electricity". Watch for "current flows" (defensible
   shorthand but strictly current IS the flow — "charge flows, and current is how fast"),
   and for "electricity" used as a mass noun for four different quantities.
4. **Electrons vs conventional current.** Conventional current is + to −; electron drift
   in a metal is − to +. Both are defensible at KS3 — but a unit that draws conventional
   arrows in one lesson and talks about electrons moving from the negative terminal in
   another, without reconciling them, silently contradicts itself. This is a real risk
   because P9 (static electricity) will have talked about electrons. I will read P9 for
   consistency and say so.
5. **Drift speed vs signal speed.** "Electrons whizz round at the speed of light."
   Actual drift speed is ~0.1 mm/s; the *effect* is near-instant because all electrons
   start at once. If a lesson says electrons travel very fast, or a sim animates dots
   racing round, and the light-comes-on-instantly point is made from the dot speed, the
   model is wrong. The honest version — "everything starts moving at once, like a bike
   chain / a tube of marbles" — is available and a good unit uses it.
6. **The battery as a current source.** "A cell supplies a fixed current." It doesn't; it
   maintains a pd, and the circuit decides the current. A lesson that says "the cell
   pushes 2 A round" plants a source-of-current model that resistance then contradicts.
7. **"Bulbs in parallel are brighter."** Ambiguous and usually taught wrong. Compared to
   *what*? Two bulbs in parallel are each as bright as one bulb alone; two in series are
   each dimmer. The comparison must be stated or the claim is meaningless. A page that
   says "parallel bulbs are brighter" full stop is teaching a memorised phrase.
8. **Parallel = "the current splits in half".** Only if the branches are identical. If
   any page states halving as a rule rather than as the equal-branch case, that's S1.
9. **Insulators "have no electrons" / "block electricity".** The honest KS3 line is that
   in an insulator the electrons are not free to move. Also the trap: an insulator is not
   a substance that "stops" electricity like a wall; it is one where nothing is free.
   And "conductors let electricity through" is circular unless the free-electron idea
   comes with it.
10. **"A switch adds/removes current"** rather than completing/breaking the path. And the
    related one: an open switch means "no current *there*" rather than nowhere in the loop.
11. **Resistance "resists" — the circular definition.** "Resistance is how much a
    component resists current" tells a student nothing. The mechanism (electrons collide
    with the vibrating lattice / the path is harder) must be there, and R = V/I must be
    introduced as a *ratio* — how many volts you need per amp — not as a magic formula.
12. **Short circuits.** A short is often drawn as "the current takes the easy path" as if
    the current chooses. Also the safety dimension: shorting a cell gets hot and can
    burst — that is a real hazard in a real KS3 classroom.
13. **Mains vs cells.** Any lesson that shows a circuit and then mentions mains without
    a clear "never with mains" line is a safety flag.

### Where AQA's language differs from everyday language

- **"Current"** — everyday: "electricity", "power", "charge" all used loosely, plus
  "current" meaning a flow of water or air. Physics: rate of flow of charge, symbol I,
  unit A.
- **"Potential difference"** — "potential" everyday means unrealised capability.
- **"Power"** — everyday means strength/authority; also "the power" meaning mains supply
  ("the power went off"). If the unit uses "power" loosely at all it must be flagged.
- **"Charge"** — everyday: to charge a phone (which is not adding charge in the physics
  sense at all — it's storing energy), to charge money, to charge at someone.
- **"Resistance"** — everyday: opposition, "the resistance"; also "resisting" as an act
  of will, which feeds the teleology risk.
- **"Circuit"** — everyday: a lap, a route. Fine, actually helpful.
- **"Cell"** vs **"battery"** — everyday: "battery" means one cell. Physics: a battery is
  two or more cells. The symbol difference is examinable at GCSE and is the single most
  common circuit-symbol error in KS3 books.
- **"Load", "supply", "consume"** — all import the consumption model.

### What diagrams of this topic usually get wrong

- Cell symbol drawn with equal-length lines (that's not a cell — long line = positive,
  short thick line = negative), or a "battery" drawn as one cell.
- Bulb symbol: the correct BS/IEC lamp is a circle with a cross ⊗ (or circle with two
  crossing lines). A circle with a squiggle is a *filament* variant; a circle with a
  loop is not standard. Any hand-drawn "lightbulb shape" in a circuit diagram is wrong.
- Ammeter/voltmeter: circle with A / V. Common error: the voltmeter drawn inline.
- Switch: an open switch drawn as a gap with no lever; correct is a hinged lever.
- Resistor: BS/IEC is a plain rectangle. The zigzag is the US symbol. A UK KS3 page
  using the zigzag is a real (if minor) finding — students copy what they see.
- Corners: wires drawn as curves rather than right angles (cosmetic but it makes circuits
  look like "spaghetti", which is a known barrier to reading them).
- Junction dots: a T-junction without a dot, or crossing wires WITH a dot. This is exactly
  the thing that makes a parallel circuit unreadable.
- Arrows: current arrows on only part of the loop (implying it stops), or arrows changing
  size/number round the loop.

### Where a simulation could work perfectly and still teach the wrong model

- Dots animating round a loop where the dots visibly slow, thin out or disappear after a
  bulb — the attenuation model, drawn.
- A sim where the ammeter reading is computed per-position rather than from one loop
  current — even if the numbers happen to match, a code path that *can* produce unequal
  series readings is a bug waiting for an edge state.
- A parallel sim that always halves, so a student never sees unequal branches and
  concludes halving is the rule.
- A resistance slider driving brightness with no ammeter shown — the causal chain is
  hidden and the student learns "more resistance = dimmer" without the current step.
- A voltmeter that reads a value with nothing said about *which two points* it is across.
- A sim where opening a switch dims one bulb but not another in a series loop.
- A short-circuit state that just shows "0 Ω" with no consequence — a real short heats
  up. Conversely a sim that lets you short a cell with no hazard note at all.
- Any sim where the battery is modelled as a current source: change the resistance and
  the current does not change.

### The single sentence most often taught wrong in British classrooms

> **"The current is used up as it goes round the circuit."**

That is the one. Its close runner-up, and the one I expect to find in subtler form
because it is the *well-meaning* version, is:

> **"The battery gives the current energy, and the bulb uses the current up."**

— a sentence that is half right (energy) and half catastrophic (current), and which is
what a teacher says when they know current is conserved but have not been given the
words for what actually is transferred. If P8 contains anything in that shape, it is the
unit's most important finding.

### What I will call a gap, not just an error

If the unit never explicitly states that **current is the same all the way round a
series circuit and nothing is used up**, and never states that **energy is what is
transferred at the bulb**, then the absence itself is a finding, because a KS3 unit on
circuits whose students leave without those two sentences has not done the job the unit
exists for.

five of those six are in string composition rather than in physics. The prose is the strongest
I have audited: it kills both of the misconceptions Mide named, by name, with instruments that
demonstrate rather than assert, and it does so across 132 states without once producing a
current that shrinks past a bulb or a voltage that flows. `resistance` in particular teaches

at Year 8. What needs fixing is almost entirely behind buttons: the `conductors-and-insulators`
