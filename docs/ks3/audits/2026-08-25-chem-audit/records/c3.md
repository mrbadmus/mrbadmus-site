## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page in this unit.

### The misconceptions this topic classically breeds

1. **Dissolving = disappearing (or melting).** The single biggest one. "The
   sugar melted in the tea"; "the salt is gone". Any sim where solute particles
   shrink, fade, or vanish teaches the wrong model perfectly. The honest model:
   solute particles separate and spread BETWEEN water particles; mass is
   conserved; the solute is still there (taste, evaporate it back).
2. **Mass not conserved on dissolving.** 100 g water + 10 g salt "weighs 100 g
   because the salt dissolved". A lesson must land 110 g explicitly, ideally
   with a balance drawing.
3. **Dissolving as a chemical change.** "Salt water is a new substance." KS3
   answer: dissolving is reversible, no new substance made.
4. **Filtration separates "solids from liquids" unqualified.** Then students
   expect filtering salt water to give salt on the paper. The word INSOLUBLE is
   load-bearing and must arrive before or with the claim. Filter paper model:
   holes bigger than dissolved particles, smaller than insoluble grains.
5. **The salt evaporates too / the water is destroyed.** In evaporation the
   water becomes vapour (goes into the air, recoverable); the dissolved solid
   stays. If a sim shows all particles leaving, or the water simply shrinking
   with no vapour shown, it plants "water disappears".
6. **Boiling bubbles are "air" (or oxygen/hydrogen).** They are water vapour.
   Distillation lessons trip this constantly.
7. **Distillation without condensation.** Students remember "boil the water
   off" and forget the whole point is CATCHING it. The condenser's cold-water
   jacket direction and the thermometer position (at the still-head, measuring
   the vapour, not dipped in the liquid) are classic diagram errors.
8. **Chromatography: the colours are created by the paper/water.** Honest
   model: the ink was ALREADY a mixture; the paper reveals it. Also inverted
   solubility ("the colour that sticks best travels furthest"), start line in
   ink (must be pencil), and start line below the solvent level (spots wash
   off). At KS3 there should be no Rf calculation — that is KS4; if present,
   pitch flag.
9. **"Pure" — the everyday vs chemical meaning.** "Pure orange juice", "pure
   mountain water" are mixtures. Chemically pure = one substance only (one
   element or one compound). A lesson that never confronts the everyday use
   leaves the misconception standing.
10. **Purity tests: melting/boiling point.** Pure substances melt/boil at a
    sharp, fixed temperature; mixtures over a range. Danger of overclaiming
    ("impurities ALWAYS raise the boiling point") or of a graph that shows an
    impure sample with a single sharp melting point. Temperature must be FLAT
    during a pure substance's melt/boil on any heating curve.
11. **Saturation.** "Keep stirring and more will dissolve forever." Stirring
    and temperature change RATE vs AMOUNT confusion.
12. **Evaporate-to-dryness vs crystallisation.** Boiling a solution to
    dryness spits and can decompose the salt; the taught method is heat gently
    to the crystallisation point then leave to cool/evaporate. Also a safety
    surface (spitting, hot equipment) — safety wording gets flagged for Mide,
    never rewritten by me.

### Models that are hard to render honestly

- **Particle view of a solution**: solute particles must persist, stay the same
  size, distribute between water particles; total particle count constant. A
  sim that deletes particles or lets them overlap the container teaches wrong.
- **Evaporation at particle level**: water particles leave from the SURFACE
  (not just when boiling), solute particles never leave. Rate vs temperature.
- **Distillation end-to-end**: boil → vapour rises → condenses on cold surface
  → collected. Four stages; sims often skip the condensation stage or show
  the solute travelling with the vapour.
- **Chromatography**: two competing attractions (paper vs solvent). If a sim
  just animates spots upward at different speeds with no cause given, it works
  perfectly and teaches nothing (or worse, "some colours are faster").

### Words that trip an 11-year-old

solute / solvent / solution (three near-identical words in one lesson),
soluble vs insoluble (and "insoluble" heard as "in-soluble = it dissolves
in"), saturated, filtrate vs residue (which is which), condense, distillate,
crystallisation, chromatogram, dissolve vs melt, pure vs impure, vapour.
Each must be defined at first use, and the lesson should not need more than
~3 new terms per page.

### Where a sim could teach the WRONG model while working perfectly

- Dissolving sim: particles fade out → "disappearing" model. Or solute drawn
  smaller after dissolving → "broken into something else".
- Evaporation sim: solution level drops with no vapour particles shown →
  "water destroyed"; or any white particle leaving with the steam.
- Distillation sim: collected liquid appears without visible condensation, or
  temperature keeps climbing while boiling (should hold ~100 °C for water).
- Chromatography sim: spots move with no solvent front, or furthest spot
  described as "strongest colour".
- Any counter/score that rewards clicking without the model being read.

### Predictions to test page-by-page

- P1 (pure-or-mixture): does it confront everyday "pure"? Are air/salt water
  correctly classed as mixtures? Element/compound/mixture particle diagrams
  correct (compound = bonded different atoms, mixture = separate particles)?
- P2 (dissolving): conservation of mass with numbers; "dissolving is not
  melting" said explicitly; solute/solvent/solution all defined; saturation.
- P3 (filtration): "insoluble" load-bearing; why salt passes through.
- P4 (evaporation/crystallisation): where the water goes; gentle heating vs
  boil-to-dryness; salt stays.
- P5 (distillation): condenser stage present; bubbles = water vapour;
  thermometer placement; safety wording (heating, sealed apparatus) → Mide.
- P6 (chromatography): pencil line, spot above solvent, colours pre-existing;
  no Rf at KS3.
- P7 (proving pure): sharp vs range melting/boiling; flat sections on curves;
  links back to chromatography (one spot = pure).
