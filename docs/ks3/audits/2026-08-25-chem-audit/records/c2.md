## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page or source file.

**The chemistry of this unit, and where it classically goes wrong.** This is the
first time most Year 7/8 students meet the particle model applied to *identity*
rather than *state*. The unit's whole job is to build three clean categories —
atom, element, compound — and the misconceptions it breeds are the most durable
in all of school chemistry:

1. **Atoms inherit macroscopic properties.** Students conclude a copper atom is
   orange and shiny, a sulfur atom is yellow, a water molecule is wet. Any
   diagram that colours atoms (and every diagram must colour atoms to tell them
   apart) risks planting this unless the text explicitly severs atom-colour from
   substance-colour. I will look for one sentence somewhere saying "the colours
   are labels, not what atoms look like" — its absence is a real risk, not
   pedantry.
2. **Compound = mixture of elements.** The single most important idea in the
   unit is that a compound is a NEW substance with NEW properties, not an
   average of its ingredients (water doesn't burn although hydrogen does; salt
   is safe although sodium and chlorine are vicious). If the compounds lesson
   doesn't land the "properties change completely" point with a concrete
   before/after example, that's an S2 at minimum. A particle diagram alone
   cannot carry it — particle diagrams make compounds LOOK like mixtures
   (coloured balls sitting near each other) unless bonding/joining is drawn.
3. **Dalton's model presented as current truth.** The lesson is titled "the
   atom — Dalton's model". Dalton says atoms are indivisible and indestructible;
   KS4 will immediately break both. Honest rendering = "this is a model, models
   get refined". Dishonest rendering = "atoms are the smallest thing and cannot
   be split", stated flatly. KS3 NC does teach the Dalton-level model, so
   simplification is right — but it must be framed as a model. I'll check the
   exact wording.
4. **Molecule / compound / element blur.** O₂ is a molecule but an element;
   students routinely think molecule ⇒ compound. If "molecule" appears in this
   unit it must be handled with care or deferred.
5. **Symbols: case is meaning.** Co ≠ CO is the classic. Also Latin-derived
   symbols (Na, Fe, Pb, K, Cu…) feel arbitrary and need their story told, or
   students assume the symbol is an abbreviation of the English name and
   invent "So" for sodium.
6. **Formulae: what the little number scopes over.** H2O = 2 H + 1 O; students
   read the 2 as belonging to O, or as "2 water". Coefficient-vs-subscript is
   KS4, but even at KS3 with flat formulae (ruled: H2SO4 not H₂SO₄) the number
   placement is ALL the information there is — flat formulae make "the number
   binds to the letter BEFORE it" harder to see, since there's no visual
   subscripting cue. I will watch whether any student-facing explanation of
   number-scope exists, and whether flat rendering demonstrably hurts it
   (candidate for "Ruled items causing observed harm", not a defect claim).
7. **Conservation of mass: gases don't count.** Students believe mass is lost
   in burning (smoke "goes away") and can't explain why magnesium GAINS mass in
   air. A conservation simulation is the highest-risk artefact in the unit: a
   sim that shows a reaction in an open beaker with a steady balance reading
   teaches the wrong model *while working perfectly* — the honest sim must
   distinguish sealed vs open, or show the gas being counted. Also the
   reverse: a fizzing open flask losing mass must be explained by gas ESCAPE,
   not by mass destruction.
8. **"Atoms are used up / destroyed" in reactions.** Conservation lesson must
   say atoms are rearranged, none created or destroyed — and the sim must not
   make atoms visually vanish or pop into existence mid-animation.

**Models hard to render honestly.** (a) Atoms as circles: fine at KS3, but
touching-vs-spaced and relative sizes imply things. (b) A compound's particle
diagram vs a mixture's — joined circles vs adjacent circles is the entire
semantic payload; sloppy spacing destroys it. (c) Any balance/scales animation:
the needle must move for the right physical reason.

**Words that trip an 11-year-old:** substance, chemically joined/bonded,
property, ratio, symbol vs formula, reactant/product, conserved, "in a fixed
proportion". Each needs defining on first use in THIS unit — C1 (particles) is
the only prior chemistry.

**Where a sim could teach the wrong model while running green:** the
conservation balance (open-system trap, above); an atom-builder that lets you
"split" an atom or delete atoms mid-reaction; an element/compound sorter that
accepts particle pictures where the compound's atoms aren't drawn joined; a
symbol quiz that accepts wrong case (co for Co) — accepting "co" would
actively teach that case doesn't matter.

**Fairness watch:** ladder questions must be answerable from THIS lesson —
symbols/formulae not yet introduced (e.g. asking about CO2 before formulae
lesson) is the likely failure shape, plus anything needing KS4 knowledge
(protons, electrons, balancing equations).

**Boredom watch:** symbols and formulae lessons are list-shaped by nature; if
they render as tables of 20 symbols with no activity, an average student stops.

## Ruled items causing observed harm

**Flat formulae (ruled: H2SO4 not H₂SO₄) blocking the exact teaching point
of c2-05.** Input for Mide, not a defect claim. The formulae lesson's core
claim is positional: "The small number written LOW, after a symbol, counts
the atoms" (explainer), and the think block asks students to commit on "a
big number at the front" vs "a small number at the back". But in the ladder
and think OPTIONS the ruled flat rendering erases the distinction the
student is being asked to see:

- Ladder rung 2 asks "What is the difference between 2CO2 and C2O4?" with
  every character the same size — the two formulae differ on screen only by
  character order, and option B's "the big number counts particles and the
  small number counts atoms" refers to sizes that do not exist in what is
  rendered. Evidence: evidence/c2-05-ladder-flat.png.
- Rung 1 asks about "H2SO4" and its feedback says "The sulfur has no small
  number" — on a page whose own hook and reveals DO subscript (H<sub>2</sub>O
  renders properly in #s-hook, 2H₂O in the think reveal).

So the page is half-subscripted: prose and reveals honour the "written low"
claim, ladder/options flatten it, and the flattening is heaviest exactly
where the big-vs-small distinction is the thing under test. If the mirror
consistency constraint binds only the question mirror, one candidate
resolution is: keep stored strings flat, render <sub> at display time in
ladder options the way the hook already does. Mide's call entirely.
