## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page in the unit.

### The chemistry of this unit and what it classically breeds

**C4-01 Chemical vs physical change.** The single most persistent misconception
here is the *reversibility heuristic*: "physical = reversible, chemical =
irreversible". It is taught as a rule of thumb and then breaks in both
directions (chopping wood is physical and effectively irreversible; thermal
decomposition of ammonium chloride and many chemical changes are reversible).
The honest discriminator is *whether a new substance is formed*. I will hunt
for any sentence, table, or sorting activity that lets a student get answers
right USING reversibility alone — a sorter that never punishes the heuristic
teaches it. Second classic trap: **dissolving**. Students file dissolving as a
chemical change ("the sugar is gone / turned into water") or conflate
dissolving with melting. If the lesson uses dissolving as an example, the
wording must be exact. Third: state changes described with chemical language
("the water becomes a new gas"). Fourth: signs-of-reaction lists (fizzing,
colour change, temperature change) taught as *proof* — boiling also bubbles,
mixing paint also changes colour; signs are evidence, not proof, and an honest
lesson says so.

**C4-02 Reactions rearrange atoms.** Danger models: atoms being *used up*,
*destroyed*, *created*, or *changing into different atoms* mid-reaction. A
particle diagram or simulation where an atom changes colour/size between the
reactant and product frame quietly teaches transmutation. Also the "mixing"
model — products drawn as reactant molecules sitting next to each other rather
than re-bonded. I will count atoms in every before/after diagram: an unbalanced
picture in THIS lesson is an S1 because atom conservation is the whole point.
Vocabulary risk: "rearrange" is fine, but "bonds break and new bonds form" must
not drift into bonds as physical objects that get consumed.

**C4-03 Word equations.** The arrow: students read "→" as "equals" or "and
then". "Reactant" and "product" arrive as new words — if used before defined,
that is friction. The + sign on the product side confuses ("do I add them?").
Also: word equations that quietly omit a reactant (oxygen in combustion/
rusting) or a product (water in neutralisation) teach students that equations
list only the interesting substances. Naming conventions matter: metal + acid →
salt naming (chloride/sulfate) is KS3 but must be consistent; "sulfate" not
"sulphate" per UK AQA convention.

**C4-04 Mass in a reaction.** Conservation of mass — the misconception
minefield: (a) mass "lost" when a gas escapes in an open system (student
concludes conservation is false); (b) magnesium GAINING mass when burned
(counter-intuitive — the oxygen from the air must be made visible in the
model); (c) gases believed weightless, so a fizzing reaction "should" lose
mass; (d) weight/mass conflation — the page must say mass and grams, never
weight. Any balance/scale simulation must model the open/closed distinction
honestly: a sim where the reading stays constant during a gas-producing
reaction in an OPEN flask teaches the wrong physics of the right law. Also,
precipitation reactions in closed flasks are the classic clean demo.

**C4-05 Symbol equations and balancing.** THE classic error: changing
subscripts to balance (H2O → H2O2) instead of coefficients. Any balancing
interactive must physically prevent or explicitly punish subscript-editing, or
at minimum the text must name the rule "never change the little numbers". Also:
diatomic elements (O2, H2, N2...) arrive unexplained; "2H2O" — does the 2
multiply everything? Pitch risk both ways: full balancing of e.g. combustion of
hydrocarbons is KS4-leaning; but balancing Mg + O2 is legitimate KS3 stretch.
NOTE the ruled item: flat formulae (H2O not H₂O) are deliberate — not a
finding. But the *pedagogical collision* is worth watching: in flat notation
"2H2O" the coefficient and subscript look identical in size, which makes the
subscript-vs-coefficient lesson HARDER — if the lesson never addresses which
"2" is which, that is a finding about scaffolding, not about the ruled
notation.

### Words that trip an 11-year-old
substance, reactant, product, conserved/conservation, chemical bond,
precipitate, decompose/decomposition, coefficient (if it appears — likely
should not at KS3), "state symbols" (KS4 — should not appear), reversible.
Watch for any of these used before explained.

### Where a simulation could teach the wrong model while working perfectly
- A physical/chemical sorter scoreable by reversibility alone.
- A reaction animation whose atoms morph, appear, or vanish between frames.
- A balance-scale sim whose mass reading ignores whether the system is open.
- A balancing game that accepts subscript changes, or that renders coefficients
  and subscripts indistinguishably in flat notation.
- Any before/after particle picture with unequal atom counts.

### Predictions to test explicitly
1. Count atoms in every particle diagram in lessons 02, 04, 05.
2. Try to pass every sorter/classifier using ONLY the reversibility heuristic.
3. In lesson 04, check whether open vs closed system is modelled and whether
   the magnesium-gains-mass case appears (it should — it is the NC anchor).
4. In lesson 05, try to answer balancing questions wrong in the "change the
   subscript" way and see what feedback comes back.
5. Check "weight" never appears where "mass" is meant.
6. Check the arrow is explicitly read as "reacts to make", not "equals".

## Ruled items causing observed harm

**Flat formulae inside the one lesson that teaches big-vs-small numbers
(c4-05).** The flat-formula ruling (mirror consistency; plus the engine
constraint that ladder options and feedback are escaped, so `<sub>` would
ship as tag soup) means the ladder and vocabulary on c4-05 print `2Mg + O2
makes 2MgO` and `H2O2` with coefficient and subscript at identical size — on
the page whose entire argument is that the two kinds of number are different
("The small numbers inside a formula…"). Rung 1's correct answer speaks of
"small numbers" while every number the ladder shows is the same size; the
student must fall back on position (front vs after-symbol), which the lesson
never explicitly names as the fallback cue. The main instrument and prose DO
render real subscripts, so the concept is drawn correctly where it is taught
— the harm is confined to the ladder/vocab strings. Input for Mide: either
extend the engine to allow `<sub>` in rung strings (an engine change, not a
lane change), or add one sentence to the ladder intro/explainer naming
position as the cue ("in flat text, the number in FRONT is the big one").
Evidence: rendered rung 1 options and rung 2 question on c4-05; explainer
"The small numbers inside a formula are part of the substance's name".
