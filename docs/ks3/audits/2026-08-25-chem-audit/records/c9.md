## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page, per protocol.

### The chemistry of this unit and where it classically goes wrong

**1. The reactivity series.** Classic misconception territory:

- **"Reactive" read as "dangerous" or "explosive"** rather than "forms compounds
  readily / loses electrons easily (KS4)". At KS3 the honest framing is
  observational: how vigorously a metal reacts with water/acid/oxygen. If a page
  says potassium is "the most reactive" without anchoring it to observable
  evidence (fizzing, speed, flame), it teaches a league table as revealed truth —
  students memorise an ordering with no idea where it comes from. The series is
  an *empirical ranking built from experiments*; that provenance is the whole
  KS3 point.
- **Carbon and hydrogen in the series.** They are non-metals but sit in the
  series because they matter for extraction (carbon) and acid reactions
  (hydrogen). If the lesson includes them without saying WHY a non-metal is in a
  "metal" league table, that's a guaranteed "wait, carbon isn't a metal?"
  stall. If it omits them from lesson 1 but lesson 3 (extraction) suddenly
  relies on "carbon is above iron", that's a fairness/sequencing defect.
- **"Gold is unreactive so it's weak/soft" conflation.** Reactivity ≠ hardness,
  strength, or melting point. Watch for any wording that lets chemical
  reactivity blur into physical properties (that blur then contaminates lesson 4
  on material properties).
- **Speed vs violence vs amount.** A more reactive metal reacts *faster and more
  vigorously* — it does not produce "more gas" from the same amount of acid.
  A sim that renders reactivity as bubble COUNT rather than bubble RATE quietly
  teaches "more reactive = more product", which is wrong (same moles of Mg and
  Zn give the same H2 volume; only rate differs).
- **Tarnish/oxide layers.** Aluminium is a trap: it's high in the series yet
  seems unreactive in the lab because of its oxide layer. KS3 usually omits
  this; but if aluminium appears mid-table with a claim like "aluminium doesn't
  react with water", that's imprecise-in-a-way-that-costs-later.

**2. Displacement.** The misconception minefield:

- **"The more reactive metal wins the fight"** — anthropomorphism is fine as
  scaffold, but the model must be *the more reactive metal ends up in the
  compound; the less reactive metal ends up as the element*. Students routinely
  invert which one "gets" the partner. A sim must show the compound partner
  (sulfate/oxide/chloride ion or the word) actually TRANSFERRING, not both
  metals just swapping colours.
- **Mass/matter conservation.** In copper sulfate + zinc, the copper doesn't
  "appear from nowhere" — it was IN the solution as copper (part of the
  compound). Classic wrong idea: "the zinc turns into copper". If the sim shows
  a grey strip turning orange without showing the copper coming OUT of the
  blue solution (and the blue fading as copper sulfate becomes zinc sulfate),
  it actively plants transmutation.
- **The colour change of the solution** (blue → colourless for CuSO4 → ZnSO4)
  is the key observable. Omitting it wastes the best evidence; getting it
  backwards is an S1.
- **No reaction is a result.** Copper + zinc sulfate: NOTHING happens. A sim or
  ladder that never exercises the no-reaction case teaches "mixing always
  reacts". Predicting displacement is precisely the skill of saying "no
  reaction" half the time.
- **Word equations direction.** zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper.
  Watch for the products written the wrong way round or "zinc copper sulfate"
  chimeras. Also watch pitch: symbol/ionic equations are KS4 — flat symbol
  formulae in a KS3 word-equation context would be pitch creep (though flat
  formulae per se are a ruled item).

**3. Extraction of metals.** Where it classically breaks:

- **"Ore" vs "rock" vs "the metal is inside like a nut in a shell".** An ore
  contains a metal COMPOUND, not trapped nuggets of shiny metal. If any diagram
  draws flecks of orange copper sitting inside a rock waiting to be picked out,
  that is the single worst drawing this topic can ship. Extraction is a
  *chemical* change (except native metals), not sieving.
- **Carbon extraction = displacement in disguise.** The link back to lesson 2
  (carbon displaces metals below it) is the intellectual spine of the unit. If
  lesson 3 presents "heat with carbon" as an arbitrary recipe rather than
  displacement-by-carbon, the unit loses its through-line.
- **Reduction wording.** "Reduction = loss of oxygen" is fine at KS3/KS4
  boundary; electron transfer is KS4+. If the word "reduction" appears it must
  be defined as oxygen loss, or not appear.
- **Why gold is found native but sodium never is** — the payoff question. Its
  absence would be a curriculum-coverage gap; its presence unexplained
  ("gold is just found in the ground") wastes the reactivity series.
- **Electrolysis for metals above carbon** — KS3 only needs "more reactive
  metals need a more powerful method (electricity)". Watch for either
  over-teaching (electrode half-equations = pitch error) or a false claim like
  "carbon can extract any metal".
- **Conservation again:** the ore doesn't "turn into" metal wholesale — the
  oxygen has to GO somewhere (CO2). A sim where rock-in → metal-out with
  nothing leaving teaches mass vanishing.

**4. Ceramics, polymers, composites.** Different failure mode — this is a
properties-and-classification lesson, so the risks are:

- **Property words used without operational meaning.** "Brittle" (breaks
  without bending — not "weak"!), "hard" (resists scratching — not "strong"),
  "stiff" (resists bending), "durable", "malleable". Brittle-vs-weak is THE
  confusion: ceramics are brittle yet very hard and strong in compression.
  If the page uses "strong" loosely for ceramics or "weak" for polymers, it
  plants the blur.
- **Polymers = "plastic" only.** A polymer is a long-chain molecule; plastics
  are one family. Wool, rubber, DNA are polymers. If the lesson equates
  polymer=plastic flatly, that's oversimplified-past-honesty; but going full
  monomer/polymerisation is KS4 creep. The KS3 sweet spot: long chains of
  repeating units, mostly made from crude oil, "plastics are synthetic
  polymers".
- **Composites: the point is the COMBINATION keeps both components' useful
  properties** (matrix + reinforcement). Steel-reinforced concrete, fibreglass,
  carbon fibre, MDF, plywood... watch that examples are genuinely composites
  (an alloy is NOT a composite — it's a mixture of metals; if alloys appear
  under composites that's an S1).
- **"Natural = good, synthetic = bad" moralising**, or the reverse — watch tone.
- Whether this lesson connects at all to the first three (it's the odd one out
  in the unit — pure materials science). Expect the join to be "choosing
  materials by properties"; if there's no join the unit ends on a non sequitur.

### Words that trip an 11-year-old (watch on every page)

ore, native, tarnish, vigorous, displace/displacement (the everyday meaning is
"move out of position" — helpful, but must be cashed out), extract/extraction,
smelting, reduction, brittle, ceramic (do they know a mug is one?), polymer,
monomer (shouldn't appear), composite, matrix (shouldn't appear unexplained),
reinforced, malleable, sonorous, corrode/corrosion, compound vs mixture
(assumed from earlier units — C9 leans hard on "compound" being secure).

### Where a sim could teach the wrong model while working perfectly

1. A reactivity sim that renders vigour as *amount* of product, not *rate*.
2. A displacement sim where colours swap but no PARTICLE/partner visibly moves
   — the "swap dance" with no conservation story; or the blue solution stays
   blue after all the copper has left it.
3. An extraction sim where rock → metal with no CO2 leaving and no carbon
   consumed — mass vanishing, carbon as magic catalyst.
4. A "test the metal" sim where potassium in acid is shown calmly bubbling —
   under-dramatising a genuine hazard (safety wording check: K/Na in acid
   should NOT be presented as a doable class practical).
5. A properties tester where "hard" and "strong" and "tough" are a single
   slider — collapsing the distinctions lesson 4 exists to make.
6. Any sim where the "no reaction" case is unreachable.

### Fairness watch

Ladder questions on: order of specific metals not taught on the page; carbon's
position if not taught; "aluminium extracted by electrolysis" if electrolysis
never mentioned; property-word definitions if the page used but never defined
them.

### Sequencing watch

L1 must establish the series WITH evidence; L2 must reuse it; L3 must reuse L2's
displacement idea for carbon; L4 must be joined on via properties/choice of
material. Also: does L1 include carbon (needed by L3)? Does any lesson lean on
acids (C6?) or oxidation (C8?) beyond what those units established?
