# C1 — Particles and their behaviour — audit record

Auditor: unit-agent C1 · 25 Aug 2026 · live vs local parity checked per lesson.

## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page. C1 is the foundation unit of the whole KS3
chemistry course — every later unit (atoms, reactions, Earth chemistry) leans
on the particle model being installed CORRECTLY here. It is also the unit with
the best-documented misconception literature in all of school science, so the
bar is known and high.

### Misconceptions this topic classically breeds (what I will hunt for)

1. **Particles themselves change when the substance changes.** Particles
   melt, expand, get hot, get soft. The single most common KS3 error:
   "particles expand when heated" (instead of: the SPACES/speed change, the
   particles don't). Any sentence like "the particles expand" or a heating
   simulation that draws particles growing or changing colour (red = hot)
   plants this directly.
2. **Particles in solids don't move.** Solid = static grid. The word
   "fixed" is dangerous if not immediately paired with "vibrate in place".
3. **Something is between the particles.** Students assume air or "stuff"
   fills the gaps. A diagram whose background reads as a medium (e.g. blue
   background = water around the water particles) reinforces it. The honest
   statement — nothing at all between particles — is rarely made explicitly.
4. **Liquid spacing drawn wrong.** The classic textbook error: liquids drawn
   with particles far apart, "half way" between solid and gas. Real liquids
   are ~just as close as solids (that's why liquids are nearly incompressible
   and why melting changes volume only a few %). If the sim/diagram shows
   big gaps in the liquid, it teaches wrong density and wrong compressibility
   reasoning, and contradicts "liquids can't be squashed" if the drawing
   plainly shows room to squash.
5. **Mass/conservation errors at state change.** Evaporated water "vanishes";
   condensation is water "created" from cold. Also "bubbles in boiling water
   are air" (they're water vapour).
6. **Melting vs dissolving blur** — less central here (dissolving is another
   unit) but any loose use of "disappear" is a risk.
7. **Anthropomorphism in diffusion/pressure.** Particles "want to spread
   out", "try to escape", "need more room". Diffusion is NOT particles
   deciding to move to the empty side — it is random motion that statistically
   evens out. A diffusion sim where particles head purposefully toward the
   gap teaches directed motion, which is wrong.
8. **Gas pressure mechanism.** Pressure = collisions with the container
   walls. Misconception: pressure is a stored "push" or particles pressing
   outward statically. A sim must connect MORE/FASTER/HARDER collisions to
   higher pressure; if temperature raises "pressure" on a gauge without the
   collision rate visibly changing, the sim asserts rather than shows.
9. **Heat vs temperature conflation**, and "cold gets in" as a substance.
10. **Particles stop at freezing / at 0°C everything stops.** Absolute zero
    confusion; freezing ≠ particles stationary (still vibrating).
11. **Evaporation only happens at boiling point.** Puddles dry below 100°C —
    if the lesson only presents boiling, that gap breeds it.

### Models that are hard to render honestly

- **Empty space**: what colour is nothing? Whatever the background is, a
  student may read it as substance.
- **Scale**: particles drawn at ~1cm implies particles are visible/countable.
  A good lesson says the drawing is a model and hugely magnified.
- **Speed↔temperature**: animation frame rates cap how "fast" hot gas can
  look; if hot and cold look similar, the model fails silently.
- **Randomness**: pseudo-random walks that visibly loop or synchronise teach
  pattern where there is none.
- **Particle identity**: drawing all particles as identical circles is fine
  at KS3, but the lesson should not accidentally say "atom" when it means
  particle, or imply water particles are single spheres AND then call them
  molecules later (C2 handles atoms — consistency of the word "particle"
  matters within this unit).

### Words that trip an 11-year-old (must be defined at first use)

particle, model, vibrate, random, dense/density, compress(ible), expand/
contract, evaporate vs boil, condense, sublimation (if present — arguably
beyond core), diffusion, concentration, pressure, collide/collision,
"states of matter", "property". Also "matter" itself. Watch for any word
used a paragraph before its definition.

### Where a simulation could teach the WRONG model while working perfectly

- Heating sim where particles grow, change colour, or where the CONTAINER
  of a solid expands dramatically (overstated expansion ~50% instead of ~1%).
- State-change sim where melting instantly scatters particles to gas-like
  spacing (skipping the "liquid = still touching" truth).
- Diffusion sim with directed particle motion, or where diffusion stops when
  "mixed" (motion never stops; mixing just stops CHANGING).
- Pressure sim where the gauge is driven by the slider, not by collisions —
  cosmetically perfect, causally empty.
- Any sim where pausing/reset freezes particles in a solid — reinforcing
  "solids don't move" if the resting state is static.

### Unit-structure risks specific to this slot

- Lesson 6 "testing the model" is unusual and excellent IF it honestly shows
  limitations (spheres aren't real, forces not drawn, scale wrong, water's
  ice-floats anomaly breaks the simple density story). Risk: it becomes a
  revision quiz instead of actual model-testing.
- Gas pressure before diffusion is fine; but diffusion in LIQUIDS being
  slower than gases should be stated with a fair reason (closer packing,
  more collisions), not "liquids are thicker".
- Balloon/syringe examples: "air is sucked" phrasing (nothing sucks;
  pressure differences push).
