# C5 — Types of reaction — audit record

Auditor: unit agent C5. Lessons in order: combustion, thermal-decomposition,
oxidation, displacement, which-reaction-is-this.

## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page in the unit.

### Misconceptions this topic classically breeds

**Combustion**
- "Burning destroys the fuel / mass disappears." The products (CO2 + water
  vapour) are invisible, so students conclude the candle "melts away to
  nothing". Any page or sim that shows a shrinking fuel without showing oxygen
  ENTERING the reaction and gases LEAVING teaches mass loss.
- Oxygen not registered as a reactant at all — "fire needs air" as a vague
  condition, not oxygen as a consumed chemical with mass.
- Candle confusion: melting of wax (physical) happening alongside burning of
  wax vapour (chemical). If the lesson uses a candle without splitting these,
  it fuses melting and burning.
- "fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water" stated as THE combustion equation.
  It is only true of hydrocarbon fuels; burning magnesium gives MgO only. If
  the page generalises without saying "fuels containing carbon and hydrogen",
  that's imprecision an examiner would flag.
- Smoke/soot misread as "the fuel escaping" rather than incomplete combustion.
- Flame treated as a substance rather than the glowing region of reacting gas.

**Thermal decomposition**
- Confusion with melting — both start with heating. "It broke down because it
  melted" is the classic wrong answer.
- Confusion with combustion — students think the heated carbonate is burning.
  Decomposition needs NO oxygen; a lesson must say the heat alone breaks the
  compound, and ideally contrast one-substance-in vs fuel+oxygen-in.
- "Decomposition" collides with biological decay/rotting (Year 7 ecology).
  Needs an explicit "not rotting" disambiguation or at least careful framing.
- Mass: an open crucible LOSES mass (CO2 escapes) — if a sim or text shows
  mass dropping without accounting for the gas, it undermines conservation of
  mass taught in C-earlier units.
- Is it one specific reaction? metal carbonate → metal oxide + carbon dioxide
  is the KS3 canonical case; limewater as the CO2 test should appear or be
  cross-referenced.

**Oxidation**
- At KS3 oxidation = gain of oxygen. Fine. But rusting specifically needs
  water AND oxygen — "iron + oxygen → rust" without water is wrong for
  rusting (and 'rust' should be named iron oxide, strictly hydrated). Watch
  exactly how the rusting example is worded.
- "Rust eats the metal away" → mass loss intuition. Reality: the oxide GAINS
  mass over the metal consumed (classic steel-wool experiment). A lesson that
  never confronts the mass-gain surprise misses the best teaching moment; one
  that implies mass loss is a defect.
- Fast vs slow oxidation: combustion IS oxidation. If the unit's taxonomy
  presents combustion and oxidation as disjoint categories, the final
  classification lesson will force false dichotomies.

**Displacement**
- Needs the reactivity series, which at KS3 may not have been met yet
  (depends on unit ordering — check whether C5 precedes the metals unit). A
  ladder question requiring an order not given on the page is unfair.
- "The displaced metal disappears." In zinc + copper sulfate, copper should
  visibly DEPOSIT as a solid coating; a sim where the copper just vanishes
  teaches the wrong model.
- The word "displace" itself: an 11-year-old reads "replace"/"push out" only
  with help. Needs a plain-English anchor (e.g. taking someone's seat).
- Colour observations: copper sulfate blue fading as copper is displaced —
  colour direction must be right (blue → paler/colourless with zinc; stays
  blue-green with iron sulfate... actually iron(II) sulfate is pale green).
  Any stated colour must be checkable.
- Spelling: sulfate, not sulphate (UK AQA convention).

**Which-reaction-is-this (classification)**
- Highest structural risk in the unit: combustion is a subset of oxidation,
  and displacement reactions are also redox. If the classifier demands ONE
  right label per scenario, the wording of each scenario must make the
  intended label unambiguous (e.g. "slowly, over weeks" → oxidation;
  "burns with a flame" → combustion). A scenario markable two ways is unfair;
  a marking scheme that says burning "is NOT oxidation" is a science error.
- Thermal decomposition vs "just melting" scenarios need the product evidence
  (gas given off, new substance) stated, or classification is guesswork.

### Models hard to render honestly
- Combustion particle/flame sims: must show O2 in and CO2/H2O out or they
  teach disappearance. O2 must be drawn as molecules, not lone atoms.
- Thermal decomposition sims: the escaping gas must carry visible mass away.
- Displacement sims: the less reactive metal must appear as solid, not vanish.

### Words that will trip an 11-year-old
thermal, decomposition (vs rotting), reactant/product, hydrocarbon, oxide,
displace, reactivity, limewater, exothermic (if it appears), corrode, tarnish.

### Where a sim could work perfectly and still teach the wrong model
- An oxygen slider that only changes flame size but never mentions soot/CO
  while the text mentions incomplete combustion.
- A displacement drag-drop that scores the PAIRING right but animates the
  loser metal off-screen.
- A decomposition heater where the solid shrinks with no gas accounting.

### Fairness watch-list
- Reactivity series order given on-page before any question needs it.
- Any question about limewater/tests only if the test is taught in-lesson.
- Classification quiz scenarios each carrying enough cues for exactly one label.
