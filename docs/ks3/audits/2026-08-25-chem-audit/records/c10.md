## Pre-audit deep think

Written before opening any page.

### Misconceptions this topic classically breeds

**Inside the Earth**
- "The mantle is a sea of liquid magma and the crust floats on it." The mantle
  is overwhelmingly SOLID rock that flows plastically over geological time.
  Any diagram with an orange liquid-looking mantle plants this.
- Outer core vs inner core states swapped or blurred (outer = liquid, inner =
  solid — and the inner core is solid BECAUSE of pressure, despite being
  hotter). If the page gives temperatures, are they defensible values?
- Layer diagrams not to scale (crust drawn km-thick) without saying so — a
  Year 7 will take a drawn diagram literally.
- "We have drilled to the core / we know by digging." We know via seismic
  waves; the deepest borehole (~12 km) barely scratches the crust. If the
  lesson claims direct observation, that's an error.

**Three ways to make a rock**
- Crystal-size rule inverted: SLOW cooling → LARGE crystals (granite,
  intrusive); FAST cooling → SMALL crystals (basalt, extrusive). This is the
  single most-inverted fact in KS3 rocks. Watch every sentence and any sim.
- Sedimentary formation as "drying out" or "squashing only" — needs both
  compaction AND cementation (minerals from water glue grains). Watch for
  "pressure turns sand to stone" alone.
- Metamorphic rocks "melt and reform" — NO. If it melts it becomes magma →
  igneous. Metamorphism is heat AND pressure WITHOUT melting. A sim that
  animates any melting on the metamorphic path teaches the wrong model.
- Magma vs lava naming (underground vs surface) used interchangeably.
- Rock examples mismatched (e.g. marble from limestone, slate from
  mudstone/shale) — check any pairs given.

**The rock cycle**
- The cycle as a fixed one-way loop (sedimentary → metamorphic → igneous →
  …). Real point: ANY rock can take ANY path; arrows should show shortcuts.
- Timescale: students imagine years or centuries; the honest scale is
  thousands to millions of years. If a sim runs the cycle in 10 seconds
  without a timescale caption, it teaches "rocks change quickly".
- Weathering vs erosion conflated (weathering = breakdown in place; erosion =
  transport). KS3 marks are lost on this distinction later.

**A planet with limits**
- "Recycling is free / always better" without the honest trade-off framing;
  "we will run out of X in exactly N years" presented as settled fact.
- Finite vs renewable definitions; ores as "rocks with metal in" — fine at
  KS3, but check nothing claims metals are "made" rather than extracted.
- Watch pitch drift into KS4 (electrolysis, phytomining, LCAs are GCSE).

**What's in the air**
- THE classic: "air is mostly oxygen." The lesson must land 78% N2 / 21% O2 /
  ~1% Ar / 0.04% CO2 hard. Any pie chart must show CO2 as a sliver — if the
  slice is enlarged "to be visible" without saying so, it quietly confirms the
  "lots of CO2" misconception fuelled by climate coverage.
- Argon omitted, or "1% is CO2" (CO2 is ~0.04%, argon is the ~0.9%).
- "Oxygen is used up and disappears" in combustion/respiration framing.

**Carbon dioxide, humans and climate**
- Greenhouse effect conflated with the OZONE HOLE — the most common climate
  misconception at this age. Any mention of "the ozone layer" near warming is
  a red flag.
- "Greenhouse gases trap heat like a blanket/glass" — acceptable KS3 model,
  but a sim showing rays literally BOUNCING off a solid layer implies a
  physical barrier; the honest KS3 version is absorb and re-emit.
- The natural greenhouse effect framed as bad. Without it Earth is ~-18 °C;
  the problem is the ENHANCED effect. A lesson that says "the greenhouse
  effect causes global warming" full stop plants the wrong idea.
- CO2 framed as toxic/pollution per se; carbon vs carbon dioxide used
  interchangeably; weather vs climate conflated.
- Data honesty: CO2 ppm values and temperature-anomaly figures should be
  current-ish and sourced-sounding, not invented; graph axes not cropped to
  exaggerate without honesty about it.

### Models that are hard to render honestly
- Earth cutaway (scale + states of each layer at once).
- Crystal-size vs cooling-rate (needs time compression WITH a caption).
- Rock cycle (many-path, million-year timescale in a 10-second sim).
- Greenhouse effect (re-emission, not a mirror layer).
- Atmosphere composition pie (a 0.04% slice is sub-pixel).

### Words that trip an 11-year-old
igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic, intrusive/extrusive, compaction,
cementation, sediment, magma vs lava, crust/mantle/core, plastic (meaning
"flows", not the material), finite, sustainable, ore, extraction, atmosphere,
combustion, deforestation, anomaly, emission, greenhouse (a literal
greenhouse may be unfamiliar to many students).

### Where a simulation could work perfectly and still teach the wrong model
- A rock-cycle clicker that always goes one way, or completes instantly.
- A cooling-rate sim where the mapping reads inverted or crystals "grow" from
  nothing without nucleation framing.
- A greenhouse sim with a solid "CO2 layer" reflecting arrows.
- A pie chart with a fattened CO2 slice.
- An Earth cutaway where the mantle glows liquid-orange.

Audit below tests these predictions per lesson and stays open to surprises.

infrared the Earth gives off"). Rail 5/5; console clean; 390px no overflow.
Reading pace check: the longest page in the unit by argument density, but
every paragraph is doing work — nothing I would cut, nothing I abandoned.

**Persona 2.** This is the best KS3 treatment of the greenhouse effect I
have seen. Both classic misconceptions I predicted are actively dismantled
rather than avoided: ozone/warming conflation (its own think-again) and the
solid-lid/trapped-rays model (the four steps + the name-critique panel).
The natural-vs-enhanced distinction is everywhere ("Natural, and
