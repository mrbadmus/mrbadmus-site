How CHD develops

Fatty material builds up. Flow falls. The muscle runs short of oxygen.

In coronary heart disease, layers of fatty material build up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them. This reduces the flow of blood through the coronary arteries, resulting in a lack of oxygen for the heart muscle.

Risk factors such as smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, and lack of exercise are linked to a higher rate of CHD. Risk rises with age because fatty material has longer to build up.

Treatments · drugs, devices, transplants

Every treatment has a cost as well as a benefit.

{{ stentFig }}
{{ valveFig }}

Statins · a drug

Reduce blood cholesterol, which slows the rate at which fatty material is deposited.

For: no surgery. Against: taken long term; side effects in some people; slow to act; don't clear what is already there.

Stents · a device

A mesh tube that holds a coronary artery open, so blood can flow to the heart muscle. The fatty material stays.

For: quick recovery; flow restored at once. Against: risks of the operation; the artery can narrow again.

Faulty valves

A valve may not open fully, so less blood gets through, or may leak, so blood flows backwards. Either way less oxygenated blood reaches the body: the person is tired and breathless. Faulty valves can be replaced with biological or mechanical valves.

Transplants and artificial hearts

In heart failure, a donor heart, or heart and lungs, can be transplanted. Artificial hearts are occasionally used to keep a patient alive while waiting for a transplant, or to let the heart rest as an aid to recovery.

Against: a shortage of donors; risk of rejection, so drugs to suppress the immune system for life. Artificial hearts are temporary, with risks of blood clots and infection.

Key fact

In coronary heart disease, fatty material builds up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing the flow of blood, so the heart muscle lacks oxygen. Statins, stents and transplants each have advantages and disadvantages.
