Three processes · one membrane

Which way, what moves, and is energy needed?

{{ membraneFig }}

Diffusion

The spreading out of particles of a gas or a solute, resulting in a net movement from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration. No energy needed.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide in gas exchange; urea from cells into the blood plasma. Faster with a bigger concentration difference, a higher temperature, or a larger membrane surface area.

Osmosis

The diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. Water only. No energy needed.

Active transport

Movement from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against the concentration gradient. This requires energy from respiration.

Root hairs taking in mineral ions from very dilute soil water; sugar molecules absorbed from the gut into the blood.

Osmosis in cells

Plant cells have a wall. Animal cells don't.

{{ cellsFig }}

In a dilute solution

Water moves into the cell by osmosis. A plant cell swells until its membrane presses against the cell wall: it becomes turgid, but the wall stops it bursting. An animal cell swells and may burst.

In a concentrated solution

Water moves out of the cell by osmosis. A plant cell loses turgor and becomes flaccid, so the plant wilts; in the extreme, the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis). An animal cell shrinks.

{{ rpLabel }} · the AQA method

Potato in sugar solutions: weigh, soak, weigh again.

  1. Cut potato cylinders of the same length and diameter. Blot dry and record the mass of each.
  2. Put one cylinder in each of a range of sugar (or salt) solutions, and one in distilled water. Same volume of solution each time.
  3. Leave them for the same time, for example 30 minutes, at the same temperature.
  4. Remove each cylinder, blot dry the same way, and record its final mass.
  5. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each and plot it against concentration.
{{ graphFig }}

Percentage change, not change in mass, lets you compare cylinders that started at different masses. Where the line crosses 0%, there is no net movement of water: the solution has the same concentration as the cell contents.

Surface area to volume · exchange surfaces

The bigger the organism, the smaller its SA:V.

{{ cubesFig }}

A single-celled organism has a large surface area compared with its volume, so diffusion across its surface meets its needs. In a large multicellular organism, the surface area to volume ratio is small and the inner cells are far from the surface, so it needs exchange surfaces and a transport system.

An exchange surface works better with a large surface area, a thin membrane for a short diffusion path, an efficient blood supply (animals), and ventilation (animals, for gas exchange).

Lungs

Many alveoli, thin walls, dense capillaries, ventilated.

Small intestine

Villi give a large surface area; thin walls; good blood supply.

Gills in fish

Many thin filaments, large surface area, good blood supply; water flows over them.

Roots

Root hair cells greatly increase the surface area for water and mineral ions.

Leaves

Flat and thin; stomata let gases in and out; air spaces inside.

Key fact

Diffusion and osmosis go down the concentration gradient with no energy; osmosis is water only, through a partially permeable membrane. Active transport goes against the gradient and needs energy from respiration.
