Conduction · conductors and insulators

Higher conductivity, higher rate.

{{ particleFig }}

The higher the thermal conductivity of a material, the higher the rate of energy transfer by conduction across it.

Metals are good conductors: they have free electrons that carry energy through the metal quickly, as well as vibrating particles. Wood, plastic, wool and air are poor conductors, so they make good insulators.

Air is a very poor conductor, because its particles are far apart and only occasionally collide. Trapped in small pockets, it also can't move about, so it can't form convection currents.

Reducing unwanted energy transfers

Insulation slows the transfer. It never stops it.

Loft insulation

A thick layer of fibreglass, a poor conductor full of trapped air, slows energy transfer through the roof.

Cavity wall insulation

Foam traps air in small pockets: trapped air is a very poor conductor, and it stops convection currents forming in the cavity.

Double glazing

A layer of air or gas between two panes conducts far less than glass alone.

Lubrication

Oil reduces friction between moving parts, so less energy is dissipated as thermal energy.

Which material insulates best, and does thickness matter?

{{ rpFig }}
  1. Put a small beaker inside a larger one. Pack the gap with one insulating material.
  2. Pour the same volume of hot water into the small beaker. Fit a lid with a hole for the thermometer.
  3. Record the temperature at the start and every few minutes, for example for 10 minutes.
  4. Repeat with each material, starting at the same temperature each time.
  5. For the second part, change the thickness of one material (for example, the number of layers) and keep everything else the same.

With the same starting temperature, volume of water and time, the temperature drop over 10 minutes is a fair comparison: the smaller the drop, the better the insulator. You can also compare the gradients of temperature–time graphs.

Key fact

The higher the thermal conductivity of a material, the higher the rate of energy transfer by conduction across it. A building cools more slowly if its walls are thicker and have a lower thermal conductivity.
