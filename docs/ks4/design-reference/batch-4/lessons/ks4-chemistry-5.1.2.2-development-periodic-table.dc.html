The steps, in order

From atomic weights to atomic numbers.

  1. STEP 1

    Ordered by atomic weight

    Before protons, neutrons and electrons were discovered, early tables put the elements in order of atomic weight. They were incomplete, and some elements were in the wrong groups.

  2. STEP 2

    Newlands' octaves

    Every eighth element seemed similar. But the pattern only worked for the first elements, he forced elements into groups where they didn't fit, and he left no gaps. His idea was rejected.

  3. STEP 3

    Mendeleev's gaps and swaps

    He also used atomic weight, but changed the order of some pairs, such as tellurium and iodine, to keep similar elements together, and left gaps for elements he thought had not been discovered.

  4. STEP 4

    Predictions tested

    Elements were later discovered to fill the gaps, such as germanium (his eka-silicon), and their properties closely matched his predictions. Passing that test is why his table was accepted.

  5. STEP 5

    Isotopes explain the swaps

    Knowledge of isotopes made it possible to explain why the order based on atomic weights was not always correct. Today's table is in order of atomic number.

Why weight misleads · isotopes

Fewer protons, but heavier on average.

{{ pairFig }}

Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons: these are isotopes. Relative atomic mass is an average that takes account of how abundant each isotope is.

Tellurium has 52 protons and iodine 53, but much of natural tellurium is made of its heavier isotopes, so its relative atomic mass (127.6) is higher than iodine's (126.9). Ordered by atomic number, tellurium comes first, just where Mendeleev put it. Argon and potassium, found later, show the same thing.

Key fact

Mendeleev ordered elements by atomic weight but left gaps and swapped some pairs. Elements found later matched his predictions, so his table was accepted. Isotopes explain why atomic-weight order was not always correct.
