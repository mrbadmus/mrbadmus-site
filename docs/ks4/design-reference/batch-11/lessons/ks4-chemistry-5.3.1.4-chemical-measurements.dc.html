Apparatus and reading it

Three ways to measure a volume.

Volumes are measured in cm3 or dm3 (1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; 1 cm3 = 1 mL). Read the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level. A measuring cylinder’s scale runs upwards; a burette’s runs downwards, and a titre is final reading minus initial reading.

Four words, used exactly

Accurate, precise, repeatable, reproducible.

AQA’s definitions, word for word. Tap each.

Measurements are affected by random error, where results vary in unpredictable ways; more measurements and a mean reduce it. Systematic error makes every result differ from the true value by a consistent amount, such as a balance that was not zeroed. A student’s three readings cluster closely but are all 2 g too high. Equations · mean, range, uncertainty

Use the range of a set of measurements about the mean as a measure of uncertainty. Three lines, one after the other; none is a triangle. Leave an anomalous result out of the mean: it does not belong to the cluster, so it would drag the mean away from the true value.

Step 1 · mean = sum of readings ÷ number of readings

Step 2 · range = largest − smallest

Step 3 · uncertainty = ± range ÷ 2, written as mean ± uncertainty

Extension · not on the GCSE specification

Percentage uncertainty.

Some courses compare an uncertainty with the size of the reading. It explains why a large cylinder is a poor choice for a small volume, but it is not examined at GCSE. Tap the quantity you want to find; the × 100 is its own line.

% uncertainty = fraction × 100

Worked (verbatim): 25.0 cm3 measured with a cylinder of uncertainty ±0.5 cm3. % uncertainty = (0.5 ÷ 25.0) × 100 = 0.02 × 100 = 2.0%.

Key fact

Whenever a measurement is made there is always some uncertainty about the result. Repeat, leave out anomalies, take the mean, and report mean ± half the range.
