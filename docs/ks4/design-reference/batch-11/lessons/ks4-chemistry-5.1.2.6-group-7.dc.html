What halogens are

Seven outer electrons, two atoms a molecule.

The halogens are non-metals with seven electrons in their outer shell. They exist as molecules of pairs of atoms: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. With metals they form ionic compounds containing halide ions with a charge of −1 (sodium chloride, Na+Cl−). With non-metals they form covalent molecular compounds, such as hydrogen chloride, HCl.

H2 + Cl2 2HCl

Down the group the relative molecular mass, melting point and boiling point increase, because the intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules.

The reactivity trend

Less reactive down the group.

A halogen reacts by gaining one electron. Fluorine and chlorine both have seven outer electrons; what differs is how far the outer shell is from the nucleus.

Higher · ionic equations

Who takes the electrons.

Leave out the metal ions, which do not change. The halogen molecule gains electrons (reduction); the halide ions lose them (oxidation).

Cl2 + 2Br− 2Cl− + Br2

Chlorine is reduced: each Cl2 gains two electrons to make 2Cl−. Bromide is oxidised: each Br− loses one electron. Charges balance: 2− on each side.

Key fact

Halogens gain one electron. Down the group the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so the attraction for an incoming electron is weaker and reactivity decreases. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.
