### 1. Development of the Model of the Atom
`model-of-the-atom` · Chemistry / atomic-structure · AQA 8464 5.1.1.3; 8462 4.1.1.3 (common content with physics 8464 6.4.1.3; 8463 4.4.1.3) · true routes CF CH TF TH

**Distinct from physics.** Batch 5's physics `development-atomic-model` covers the same spec text and is built on the alpha-scattering experiment (INVESTIGATION). Combined pupils get both pages. This chemistry page is built on the chain of models instead: each model, the evidence that broke it, the one that replaced it. It ends on the nuclear atom with protons, neutrons and electrons at fixed distances, which the next chemistry lessons (`subatomic-particles`, `electronic-structure`) build on. Scattering appears as one link in the chain, not as the flagship.

**Family:** PROCESS. Changed from BATCH-PLAN's MODEL. The spec's lead sentence is about how models change ("New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced"), so the page is a sequence. MODEL would duplicate `subatomic-particles`, and INVESTIGATION is the physics twin's.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A four-step timeline (solid sphere → plum pudding → nuclear → Bohr, then protons and neutrons). At each step the pupil plays the new evidence card and picks which feature of the old model it breaks.

**Route layers:** none. All base.

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:** None.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Most alpha particles bounced back." → Most went straight through; very few bounced back (5.1.1.3).
- "The ones that bounced back hit electrons." → They were repelled by a tiny positive nucleus where the mass is concentrated (5.1.1.3).
- "Plum pudding has empty space in it." → It is a solid ball of positive charge with electrons embedded; empty space came from scattering (5.1.1.3).
- "Thomson's model had no electrons." → It was built because of the electron (5.1.1.3).
- "Scientists were wrong, so science can't be trusted." → Changing a model when evidence disagrees is how science is meant to work (5.1.1.3, WS 1.1).
- "Neutrons were found with the nucleus." → About 20 years later, by Chadwick (5.1.1.3).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Everyone has changed their mind when a replay or new fact turned up. Should an idea stay the same, or change when new evidence disagrees with it?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, both usable verbatim on all four routes (q1, q2). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 1 (`figlib/physics.py history_of_atom()`). Must draw: the model sequence (solid sphere; plum pudding with electrons in positive charge; nuclear atom with tiny nucleus; Bohr shells), and alpha tracks (straight through / large angle / back) as the evidence card between plum pudding and nuclear.

### 2. Subatomic Particles
`subatomic-particles` · Chemistry / atomic-structure · AQA 8464 5.1.1.4–5.1.1.5; 8462 4.1.1.4–4.1.1.5 · true routes CF CH TF TH

**Distinct from physics.** Batch 5's physics `structure-of-atom` (8464 6.4.1.1) is built on scale and on electrons moving between energy levels by absorbing or emitting EM radiation. This chemistry page is built on the particle table (charge, relative mass, where each particle is) and on **ions**: what changes when an atom gains or loses electrons, and counting p, n, e in an atom or ion. Scale gets one short section here.

**Family:** MODEL. BATCH-PLAN's suggestion is kept. The three-particle nuclear model explains why atoms are neutral and how ions get their charge.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** An atom builder. The pupil sets protons, neutrons and electrons from a nuclear symbol, then removes or adds electrons and watches the charge (and only the charge) change, e.g. Mg → Mg²⁺: 12 p, 12 n, 10 e.

**Route layers:**
- Higher: ion formation written as half equations (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻; Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻). 8464 5.1.1.1 / 8462 4.1.1.1 "(HT only) write balanced half equations and ionic equations" (F3).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:** Counting, base. No equation, no CFIFA. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number; electrons in an ion = protons − charge (e.g. ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺ → 13 p, 14 n, 10 e). **Chains equations? no**: these are two separate subtractions. Units to convert: none. Scale reading in standard form (1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m vs 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m; MS 1b).

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none. Mass number = p + n is a sum, not a triangle.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Mg²⁺ has 12 electrons." → The charge tells you 2 were lost: 10 (5.1.1.5).
- "A positive ion gained protons." → The proton number never changes in a reaction; electrons are lost (5.1.1.4).
- "Protons and neutrons cancel out." → Neutrons have no charge; protons balance electrons (5.1.1.4).
- "Electrons weigh about as much as protons." → Their relative mass is very small; almost all mass is in the nucleus (5.1.1.5).
- "Every atom has neutrons." → Hydrogen-1 has none (F1).
- "The nucleus is 10 000 times smaller." → Its **radius** is less than 1/10 000 of the atom's (5.1.1.5) (F2).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** A rubbed balloon sticks to a wall because charges can be moved about. Is the balloon charged because it gained something, or because it lost something? Either guess leads into electrons moving.

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, both usable verbatim on all four routes (q1, q2). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 2 (`figlib/physics.py atom_diagram()`; `shared/ks4-diagrams.js atom()`). Must draw: a labelled atom (nucleus with p and n, electrons on shells), the charge/mass/location table, an atom and its ion side by side with counts, and a not-to-scale note with the radius comparison.

**Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 3. Relative Atomic Mass, Atomic Number and Isotopes
`relative-atomic-mass` · Chemistry / atomic-structure · AQA 8464 5.1.1.6 (+5.1.1.5); 8462 4.1.1.6 (+4.1.1.5) · true routes CF CH TF TH

**Family:** QUANTITATIVE. BATCH-PLAN's suggestion is kept. The spec's one skill here is calculating Ar from percentage abundances.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** An isotope balance. Abundance bars for Cl-35 and Cl-37 sit on a beam; the pupil slides the abundances and watches Ar move toward the more common isotope, then writes the bracketed sum.

**Route layers:** none. All base. The source's `higher` field (mass spectrometry) is not in GCSE and must not become an HT layer (F2).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:**
- Ar = Σ(% abundance × mass number) ÷ 100. Base. **Chains equations? no.** It is one equation: multiply each isotope, add, then ÷ 100, with the whole top bracketed. The frozen worked example's Insert line is mis-bracketed; use it corrected (F1). Convert: the frozen boron example has nothing to convert. CFIFA's second example needs a Convert step: give the abundances as a ratio (Cl 3 : 1 → 75 %, 25 %), or give one abundance and have the pupil find the other (100 − 75 = 25 %).
- Neutrons = mass number − atomic number. Base. **Chains? no.** Convert: none.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none. Ar = Σ(% × mass number) ÷ 100 is "Learn it" (chemistry papers have no equation sheet). It is a weighted sum, so a triangle does not apply; show it as one line with the bracket. Mass number = p + n and neutrons = A − Z are sums or differences; no triangle.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Ar is the average of the two mass numbers." → It is weighted by abundance: 60 % at 63 and 40 % at 65 gives 63.8, not 64 (5.1.1.6).
- "(20 × 10) + (80 × 11) ÷ 100 is the same as dividing the total." → Without the outer bracket only the second product is divided (F1).
- "Isotopes have different numbers of protons." → Same protons (same element), different neutrons (5.1.1.5).
- "The bigger number on the periodic table is the mass number." → It is the relative atomic mass. Mass number belongs to one isotope (F4).
- "Ar is never a whole number." → Often not; C 12, O 16 and Na 23 are (F3).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Three friends score 10 and one scores 20. Is the group's average closer to 10 or to 20?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen quiz items, both usable verbatim on all four routes (q1, q2). The frozen FIFA is usable only with the Insert line corrected (F1). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 3 (`figlib/physics.py isotope_notation()`). Must draw: the nuclear symbol (mass number top, atomic number bottom) next to a periodic-table box (Ar, atomic number), labelled differently; isotope pair diagrams (Cl-35/Cl-37 nuclei); and the weighted-mean bar/balance figure (the one figure still needed).

**Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 4. Electronic Structure
`electronic-structure` · Chemistry / atomic-structure · AQA 8464 5.1.1.7 (+5.1.2.1); 8462 4.1.1.7 (+4.1.2.1) · true routes CF CH TF TH

**Family:** MODEL. BATCH-PLAN's suggestion is kept. Shell filling (2, 8, 8) is the model that explains group, period and similar chemistry.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A shell filler. The pupil drops Z electrons onto shells (inner first, 2 then 8 then 8), the number form (2,8,1) writes itself, and the element lands on its group and period in a mini periodic table.

**Route layers:** none. All base. The source's `higher` field is base content (configurations 1–20, group/period from structure) and goes to every route (F1).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:** None.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Potassium is 2,8,9." → For the first 20 the third shell holds 8, then the fourth starts: 2,8,8,1 (5.1.1.7).
- "Group = number of shells." → Group = outer electrons; period = number of shells (5.1.2.1).
- "Elements in a group are similar because they have the same number of electrons." → Same number of **outer** electrons (5.1.2.1).
- "Helium has 2 outer electrons, so it's in Group 2." → It is a noble gas: full outer shell of 2 (5.1.2.4) (F2).
- "Electrons go wherever there's room." → They fill the lowest available level (innermost shell) first (5.1.1.7).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** People getting onto an empty bus. Do they fill the seats nearest the door first, or spread out to the back?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, both usable verbatim on all four routes (q1, q2). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 4 (`figlib/chemistry.py electronic_configuration()`, H–Ca; `shared/ks4-diagrams.js atom()`). Must draw: the diagram form (electrons on concentric shells, which the spec requires alongside numbers, F3) for Na (2,8,1) and a few others; a periodic-table strip showing group = outer electrons and period = shells.

**Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 5. Group 7 — Halogens
`group-7` · Chemistry / atomic-structure · AQA 8464 5.1.2.6; 8462 4.1.2.6 (HT layer 8464 5.4.1.4; 8462 4.4.1.4) · true routes CF CH TF TH

**Family:** CLASSIFY. BATCH-PLAN's suggestion is kept. The examined task is sorting halogen + halide pairs into "reacts" / "no reaction" from the reactivity order, and predicting properties from position.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A 3 × 3 displacement grid (chlorine, bromine, iodine water against chloride, bromide, iodide solutions). The pupil predicts each cell from the order, then reveals the colour; the empty cells prove the order.

**Route layers:**
- Higher: ionic equations for displacement (Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂) and which species is oxidised or reduced. 8464 5.4.1.4 / 8462 4.4.1.4 "Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (HT only)"; half equations 8464 5.1.1.1 / 8462 4.1.1.1 (HT only). Not in the source; written from the spec (F5).

**Required practical:** none. (Halogen displacement is an AT 6 opportunity, not an AQA RP.)

**Calculations:** None.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none. Three balanced symbol equations (Cl₂ + 2NaBr; Cl₂ + 2KI; Br₂ + 2KI), plus their HT ionic forms.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Like Group 1, halogens get more reactive going down." → Reactivity decreases down Group 7 (5.1.2.6).
- "Fluorine is more reactive because it has fewer electrons / more protons." → Its outer shell is closer to the nucleus, so it attracts an incoming electron more strongly (5.1.2.6) (F4).
- "Iodine can push chlorine out of sodium chloride." → Only a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one (5.1.2.6).
- "Boiling halogens breaks the bonds in the molecules." → Only the weak intermolecular forces are overcome, and these grow with molecule size (5.2.2.4) (F2).
- "Chlorine is Cl." → Halogens are diatomic: Cl₂ (5.1.2.6).
- "Halogens only make salts." → With non-metals they form covalent molecules, e.g. HCl (5.1.2.6) (F3).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** A magnet picks up a paperclip. Does it pull harder when the clip is close, or when it is far away?

**Practice (rule 4):** 3 frozen items. q1 and q3 are usable verbatim on all four routes (2). q2 is not usable as written: wx1 implies fluorine has fewer outer electrons (F4); usable with wx1 replaced. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 5 (`ks3_art/c8.py r_halogen_grid()`; a KS4-styled static version for consistency). Must draw: the displacement grid with solution colours (orange bromine, brown iodine; optional cyclohexane-layer colours); F and Cl atoms side by side showing the outer-shell distance; a boiling-point trend chart using **boiling points only** (−188, −34, 59, 184 °C) (F1).

### 7. Mass Changes in Reactions
`mass-changes-reactions` · Chemistry / quantitative · AQA 8464 5.3.1.3; 8462 4.3.1.3 · CF CH TF TH (HT calculation layer on CH TH)

- **Family:** CONTRAST — gas leaves (reading falls) vs gas joins (reading rises) vs sealed (no change); BATCH-PLAN kept.
- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** Two balances side by side, open flask and sealed flask, same acid + carbonate: one reading falls, one holds.
- **Route layers:**
  - higher — predicting the mass gained/lost from the balanced equation (th2; FIFA): 8464 5.3.2.1–5.3.2.2 (HT only). Unbadged in the data (MCR-F2).
- **Required practical:** none.
- **Calculations:**
  - Mass of gas lost or gained = mass before − mass after. Chains equations? no. Convert: none normally (g throughout); a kg → g example serves CFIFA's convert case. Base.
  - (HT) Mass of oxygen gained / product formed from a given mass of reactant. Chains equations? **yes** — Step 1 n = m ÷ Ar (4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2 mol Mg); Step 2 molar ratio (2 : 1 → 0.1 mol O₂); Step 3 m = n × Mr (0.1 × 32 = 3.2 g). The frozen FIFA uses a mass-ratio route instead (Step 1 48 g : 80 g; Step 2 scale factor 0.1; Step 3 8.0 g) — teach one method, show the other as a check. Convert: none. HT.
- **Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** (HT) n = m ÷ Mr — Learn it (chemistry has no equation sheet) — top m; bottom n × Mr.
- **Misconceptions to confront:**
  - "Burning destroys stuff, so the burnt metal weighs less." — the oxide is heavier than the metal (5.3.1.3).
  - "The mass changed, so mass wasn't conserved." — a gas entered or left an open system (5.3.1.1, 5.3.1.3).
  - "Gas weighs nothing." — gas particles have mass; CO₂ escaping lowers the reading.
  - "A sealed flask gets lighter when a gas is made." — closed system: no change.
  - (HT) "0.2 mol Mg needs 0.2 mol O₂." — use the 2 : 1 ratio from the coefficients.
- **"Start here" access note (rule 1):** an open fizzy drink left on kitchen scales — does the reading go up or down?
- **Practice (rule 4):** q1, q2 usable on all four routes (2 per route). The FIFA is HT only. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size, including a base before/after subtraction item.
- **Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 7 — `figlib/chemistry.py conservation_sealed()` and `gas_syringe()`. Must draw: open vs sealed flask on a balance; metal + oxygen particles joining into an oxide.
- **Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 8. Chemical Measurements
`chemical-measurements` · Chemistry / quantitative · AQA 8464 5.3.1.4; 8462 4.3.1.4 (+ WS 3.4, 3.7) · CF CH TF TH

- **Family:** INVESTIGATION — repeat readings, find the mean, state the uncertainty as half the range; BATCH-PLAN kept.
- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** Three repeat titres dropped on a number line; stretch the range bracket and read off mean ± half the range.
- **Route layers:** none — all base. (% uncertainty is not on any route: an extension only, CM-F2.)
- **Required practical:** none on this page.
- **Calculations:**
  - Uncertainty from repeats (the spec's core — missing from the data, CM-F1). Chains? **yes** — Step 1 mean = sum ÷ number of readings (anomalies left out); Step 2 range = largest − smallest; Step 3 uncertainty = ± range ÷ 2; write mean ± uncertainty. Convert: dm³ → cm³ (× 1000); mL = cm³. Base.
  - (Extension, labelled) % uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100. Chains? no. Convert: both values in the same unit.
- **Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none on the spec (uncertainty = range ÷ 2 is one step). If the % uncertainty extension is kept: Learn it (no chemistry sheet) — top uncertainty; bottom measured value × (% ÷ 100); the × 100 is its own line.
- **Misconceptions to confront:**
  - "Precise means correct." — accurate = close to the true value; precise = readings cluster closely (WS 3.7).
  - "Repeating gets rid of every error." — repeats and a mean reduce random error, not systematic error (WS 3.7).
  - "The uncertainty is the whole range." — it is half the range about the mean (5.3.1.4).
  - "Read the top of the curve." — bottom of the meniscus, at eye level.
  - "A bigger measuring cylinder is more accurate." — bigger graduations, bigger uncertainty.
  - "A burette reads like a cylinder." — its scale runs downward; titre = final − initial reading.
- **"Start here" access note (rule 1):** two kitchen jugs, one marked every 100 mL and one every 10 mL — which measures 30 mL closer?
- **Practice (rule 4):** q1 usable on all four routes as an extension item only (CM-F2); q2 not usable (CM-F3). Core usable: 0 per route. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size, including mean ± half-range items and an accurate/precise classification item.
- **Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 8 — nothing exists. Must draw: burette, pipette and measuring cylinder side by side with graduations; meniscus close-ups showing a cylinder scale running up and a burette scale running down; repeat readings on a number line with mean and half-range.
- **Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 9. Moles
`moles` · Chemistry / quantitative · AQA 8464 5.3.2.1; 8462 4.3.2.1 (HT only) · CH TH

- **Family:** QUANTITATIVE — moles from mass and particles from moles; BATCH-PLAN kept.
- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** One-mole piles on a balance — 12 g carbon, 18 g water, 58.5 g salt — each tagged with the same 6.02 × 10²³ count.
- **Route layers:**
  - The whole page is higher: 8464 5.3.2.1 (HT only), 8462 4.3.2.1 (HT only).
  - % by mass (th3, equation 4) is base, 8464 5.3.1.2 — a recap here; its home is `relative-formula-mass` (MOL-F4).
- **Required practical:** none.
- **Calculations:**
  - n = m ÷ Mr (and m = n × Mr). Chains? no. Convert: kg → g (× 1000) — the "wrong unit" worked example (e.g. 2.7 kg Al → 2700 g → 100 mol). HT.
  - particles = n × Nₐ (and n = particles ÷ Nₐ). Chains? no. Convert: none; standard form. HT.
  - Mass from a particle count (th2 ex 4; q2). Chains? **yes** — Step 1 n = particles ÷ Nₐ; Step 2 m = n × Mr. And the reverse, mass → particles: Step 1 n = m ÷ Mr; Step 2 particles = n × Nₐ. HT.
  - % by mass of an element. Chains? only if Mr is not given (Step 1 Mr from Ar; Step 2 %). Base recap.
