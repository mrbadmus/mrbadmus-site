## 5. For the lesson author
**Misconceptions seen in AQA marking**
- Using the final (or initial) temperature as Δθ; a negative Δθ carried into an energy "released".
- Mass left in grams (the commonest single error); c quoted in J/kg (missing °C) or ΔE in J/kg.
- Temperature and energy treated as the same thing ("the water has more heat because it is hotter"); a bath at 40 °C stores more energy than a cup at 90 °C.
- "High SHC means it gets hot quickly" (reversed).
- RP: calculated c **higher** than the data-book value is explained as "the thermometer was wrong" rather than energy lost to the surroundings (not all supplied energy raises the block's temperature).
- RP: reading the thermometer immediately the heater is switched off — the temperature keeps rising for a while; record the maximum.

**Command words**: Calculate, Determine (from a graph gradient), Describe (a method), Explain, Suggest (an improvement), Give a reason, Evaluate (the method).

**Typical questions** ⚑ examiner-drafted
- *Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 250 g of water from 18 °C to 65 °C. c = 4200 J/kg °C. [3]* — m = 0.25 kg and Δθ = 47 °C (1); ΔE = 0.25 × 4200 × 47 (1); = 49,350 J (accept 49 000 J / 49 kJ) (1).
- *A 1.0 kg aluminium block is heated by a 50 W heater for 300 s. Its temperature rises by 15 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium. [4]* — E = 50 × 300 = 15,000 J (1); 15,000 = 1.0 × c × 15 (1) rearranged c = 15,000 ÷ 15 (1); = 1000 J/kg °C (1).
- *The value is higher than the data-book value of 900 J/kg °C. Explain why. [2]* — energy is transferred to the surroundings / not all energy heats the block (1); so the temperature rise is smaller than it should be, giving a larger c (1).
- *Describe a method to determine the specific heat capacity of a metal block. [6]* — Levels: **L3 (5–6)** coherent method that would produce valid results: mass measured on a balance; heater and thermometer in the holes (a drop of water/oil for thermal contact); block insulated; energy measured by joulemeter or by V, I and t (E = VIt / E = Pt); temperature recorded at regular intervals (or start and finish); c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ) or from the gradient of temperature–time. **L2 (3–4)** method with key measurements but one omission (e.g. how energy is found) or not fully in order. **L1 (1–2)** some relevant steps; would not give a value of c. Indicative: measure mass; insulate; switch on heater; measure V and I (or joulemeter readings); start stopclock; record temperature every minute for 10 min; calculate energy supplied; calculate Δθ; c = E/(mΔθ). ⚑

**Required practical — 8464 RP14 / 8463 RP1 (Specific heat capacity)**
- *Apparatus:* 1 kg metal block (aluminium/copper/steel) with two holes; 12 V immersion heater; thermometer (or temperature probe); low-voltage power supply; ammeter + voltmeter (or joulemeter); stopclock; balance; insulation (lagging) around the block; a few drops of water in the thermometer hole.
- *Method:* measure the mass; insulate; record the starting temperature; switch on and start the clock; record V and I; record temperature every minute for ~10 minutes; switch off; E = V × I × t (or joulemeter reading); plot temperature against time — the straight section's gradient gives c = P ÷ (m × gradient), or c = E ÷ (m × Δθ).
- *Variables:* independent — time of heating (energy supplied) [or the material, when comparing]; dependent — temperature (rise); control — mass of block, heater power (p.d.), starting temperature, insulation.
- *Risks:* heater and block get hot — burns: do not touch, allow to cool on a heat-proof mat; low-voltage supply only; secure the thermometer so it cannot roll and break.
- *Typical results:* aluminium measured ~1000–1200 J/kg °C vs 900 data-book; copper ~400–500 vs 385. **Always high**, because energy is dissipated to the surroundings and to the heater/thermometer; insulation reduces (not removes) this. Temperature lag at start (heater warming up) — use the linear section of the graph.

**Equations**: ΔE = m c Δθ — **on the sheet** (8463 Appendix A sheet list eq 5; printed on both June 2026 sheets); recall list: not on it. Supporting E = P t — spec **recall list** (8463 eq 21) and **printed on both June 2026 sheets**; P = E/t likewise (eq 12; on both sheets). Note the difference: the spec says *recall* for E = Pt; the June 2026 sheets print it anyway. Teach it as one to know, and chip it per the sheet the page links to.
