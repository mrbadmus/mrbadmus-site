## 5. For the lesson author

**What this lesson owns:** EM vs LM (magnification and resolution only), how EM increased understanding of sub-cellular structures, a brief history of microscopy, the magnification equation with unit conversion (mm, µm, nm) and standard form, and RP1 in full (see R9).

**Misconceptions seen in AQA marking**
- Resolution defined as "how big" or "how clear the image is" with no reference to distinguishing two points.
- "Electron microscopes magnify more, so they can see living cells." (They cannot: vacuum.)
- Unit mismatch: dividing mm by µm. Converting mm → µm by ×100 or ×10⁶.
- Inverting the equation (A ÷ I), which gives a magnification below 1.
- Giving magnification a unit (mm), or writing "×" twice.
- Total magnification taken as the objective only, or eyepiece + objective instead of eyepiece × objective.
- Drawings: shading, sketchy double lines, labels with arrowheads or crossing lines, no title, no magnification or scale.
- Onion epidermis cells "have chloroplasts". (They do not: underground leaf bases. Pupils label chloroplasts they cannot see.)
- Standard form slips: 1.5 × 10⁴ for 0.00015.

**Command words:** Give, Describe, Explain, Compare, Calculate, Use (the scale bar), Suggest, Draw.

**Typical questions** ⚑ examiner-drafted
- ⚑ 2 marks, Give, **base**: *"Give two advantages of an electron microscope compared with a light microscope."* — higher magnification (1); higher resolution / resolving power, so more detail and smaller structures seen (1). Do not credit "bigger image" alone, or "clearer" alone.
- ⚑ 3 marks, Calculate, **base**: *"A cell is drawn 36 mm long at ×1200. Calculate its real length in µm. Give your answer in standard form."* — 36 ÷ 1200 = 0.03 mm (1); × 1000 = 30 µm (1); 3.0 × 10¹ µm (1).
- ⚑ 2 marks, Calculate, **base**: *"The eyepiece is ×10 and the objective ×40. Calculate the total magnification."* — 10 × 40 (1); ×400 (1).
- ⚑ 4 marks, Explain, **base**: *"Explain how the electron microscope has increased our understanding of cells."*
  - (1) EM has much higher resolution / resolving power.
  - (1) So it can distinguish structures smaller than about 200 nm.
  - (1) Sub-cellular structures (for example ribosomes, or the internal structure of mitochondria) were seen for the first time.
  - (1) Higher magnification as well, so finer detail is visible.
  - (1) Allow: this led to an understanding of their function. Max 4.
- ⚑ 6 marks, Describe, **base**, the RP1 6-marker: *"Describe how a student could prepare a slide of onion cells and use a light microscope to produce a labelled drawing."*
  - **Indicative content:**
    - peel a thin layer of epidermis with forceps;
    - lay it flat on a slide in a drop of water or iodine;
    - lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles;
    - place on the stage and start with the lowest-power objective;
    - turn the coarse focus to bring the stage up, watching from the side so the lens does not hit the slide;
    - focus with coarse, then fine;
    - switch to a higher objective and refocus with fine focus only;
    - draw with a sharp pencil: clear continuous lines, no shading;
    - label with ruled lines;
    - add a title and the magnification or a scale bar.
  - **Level 3 (5–6):** a logical sequence covering preparation, focusing and the conventions for scientific drawing.
  - **Level 2 (3–4):** a sequence with some omissions, for example no focusing order or no drawing conventions.
  - **Level 1 (1–2):** isolated steps.

**Required practical — RP1 (8464 RP1 / 8461 RP1)**
- **Method:** as in the 6-marker above. For cheek cells (if the school uses them): use a cotton bud and methylene blue. Many schools now use prepared slides or onion only.
- **Variables:** none. RP1 is an observation and drawing practical, not an investigation, so do not invent an IV/DV.
- **Risks:**
  - iodine and methylene blue: irritant and staining, so avoid skin and eyes, wear eye protection;
  - glass slides and coverslips: sharp, so handle carefully and report breakages;
  - microscope lamp: hot;
  - cheek swabs: biological hazard, so dispose of them in disinfectant.
- **Typical results:**
  - onion epidermis: brick-shaped cells in rows; cell wall, membrane (not distinguishable from the wall), nucleus stained yellow-brown, large vacuole; **no chloroplasts**;
  - cheek cells: irregular shape, nucleus stained blue, no wall;
  - cell size by estimation: field of view at ×400 ≈ 0.4–0.5 mm, divided by the number of cells across. Typical onion cell ≈ 0.1–0.3 mm long; cheek cell ≈ 50–60 µm.

**Equations to learn** (GCSE Biology has no equation sheet; all must be recalled)
- magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object. **Base.** Rearrangements: real size = image ÷ magnification; image = real × magnification.
- total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification. **Base** (RP1 context).
- Unit ladder: 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm.

**CFIFA examples the author must write new** (the pack has none with a genuine *Convert*-first step)
- Nothing to convert: FIFA 1 qualifies, with C = "nothing to convert".
- Wrong unit arriving: *"A chloroplast is 5 µm long. In a drawing it is 25 mm long. Calculate the magnification."*
  - Convert: 25 mm → 25 000 µm (or 5 µm → 0.005 mm).
  - Formula and Insert: M = 25 000 ÷ 5.
  - Answer: ×5000.
- One answer that must go into standard form (R5), with nm. For example: *"A ribosome of 25 nm appears 50 mm wide in a micrograph."*
  - Convert: 25 nm = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mm.
  - Answer: M = 50 ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 2 × 10⁶ = ×2 000 000.
  - Use this one on Higher-demand rungs only. It is not Higher *content*.
