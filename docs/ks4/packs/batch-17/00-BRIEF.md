### 1. Moments, Levers and Gears
`moments-levers-gears` · Physics / forces · AQA 8463 4.5.4 (physics only; not in 8464) · TF TH

**Family:** QUANTITATIVE — the principle-of-moments calculation carries the idea; levers and gears are explained with the same M = F d.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A balance beam: place weights at distances, predict tilt or balance, then find the missing force or distance; the same beam becomes a lever and a pair of gears.

**Route layers:**
- Triple (whole page, TF and TH): moments, principle of moments, levers and gears. 8463 4.5.4 (physics only). No HT content — no Higher badge anywhere (MLG-F5).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:**
- Moment, M = F d (and F = M ÷ d, d = M ÷ F). Chains equations? no. Convert: cm → m (mm → m). Base (triple).
- Balanced object, find a missing force or distance. Chains equations? **yes** — Step 1: moment of the known side with M = F d (add the moments if more than one force); Step 2: principle of moments, that moment = the unknown side's moment, rearrange M = F d. Convert: cm → m before Step 1. Base (triple). The frozen FIFA is this calculation folded into F₁d₁ = F₂d₂ — set it out as Step 1 / Step 2.
- Gear ratio: off-spec, none (MLG-F6).

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
- M = F d — On the sheet (8463). Triangle: M on top; F | d on the bottom.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Use the length of the lever." → d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force (4.5.4).
- "Heavier side always goes down." → Balance depends on force × distance on each side, not force alone (4.5.4).
- "Tweezers make your fingers stronger." → With the effort near the pivot, the output force is smaller (4.5.4; MLG-F3).
- "A big gear gives more force." → Same force at the teeth; it acts further from the axle, so the moment is bigger and it turns slower (4.5.4; MLG-F4).
- "Moment = F ÷ d." → Moment = F × d (4.5.4; MLG-F1).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Opening a heavy door: is it easier pushing near the hinge or near the handle?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, 0 usable as written: q1 (MLG-F1) and q2 (MLG-F2) each have a false wrong-answer explanation; corrected copies in the flags make both usable TF TH. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 1 (`moments()`). Must draw: a beam on a pivot with forces at marked distances; a lever with pivot, effort and load; two meshed gears (small driving large) showing opposite rotation and the force at the teeth acting at different radii.

**Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 2. Pressure in a Fluid
`pressure-in-a-fluid` · Physics / forces · AQA 8463 4.5.5.1.1, 4.5.5.2 (physics only); 8463 4.5.5.1.2 (physics only, HT only); not in 8464 · TF TH

**Family:** MODEL — changed from BATCH-PLAN's QUANTITATIVE (p = hρg): that equation is HT only, so on TF it would leave the flagship empty. One picture — particles colliding and the amount of fluid above a point — explains pressure on surfaces, the rise with depth and the fall with altitude; p = F/A and p = hρg sit inside it.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A column of fluid above a point: move the point down a tank or up a mountain and predict the pressure; TH adds density and reads off p = hρg.

**Route layers:**
- Triple (whole page, TF and TH): fluid = liquid or gas; force normal to any surface; p = F/A; atmosphere model and why pressure falls with height. 8463 4.5.5.1.1, 4.5.5.2 (physics only).
- Triple Higher: p = hρg; why pressure rises with depth and density; pressure differences between depths; upthrust from the pressure difference; floating and sinking. 8463 4.5.5.1.2 (physics only, HT only) (PIF-F1). Upthrust is taught fully on `upthrust-floating` — one line and a link here (PIF-F4).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:**
- p = F ÷ A. Chains equations? no. Convert: cm² → m² (÷ 10,000), mm² → m², kN → N. Base (triple).
- Pressure from a mass on an area. Chains equations? **yes** — Step 1: W = m g; Step 2: p = W ÷ A. Convert: g → kg, cm² → m². Base (triple). Needed before any item that gives a mass.
- p = h ρ g. Chains equations? no. Convert: cm → m; g/cm³ → kg/m³ (× 1000); Pa → kPa in the answer. HT.
- Pressure difference between two depths. Chains equations? no if taught as Δp = Δh × ρ × g (recommended); yes if each pressure is found then subtracted (Step 1 p at each depth; Step 2 subtract). HT.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
- p = F / A — On the sheet (8463). Triangle: F on top; p | A on the bottom.
- p = h ρ g — On the sheet (8463, HT). Triangle: p on top; h | ρ | g on the bottom.
- W = m g (supporting) — On the sheet (8463). Triangle: W on top; m | g on the bottom.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Pressure only pushes down." → The force from fluid pressure is at right angles to every surface (4.5.5.1.1).
- "A bigger force always means a bigger pressure." → p = F/A: the same force on a bigger area gives less pressure (4.5.5.1.1).
- "Air pressure is just the air's weight pressing." → Molecules colliding with the surface cause it; less air above is why it falls with height (4.5.5.2; PIF-F3).
- "Water is squashed denser at the bottom." → Liquids barely compress; pressure rises because the column above is taller (4.5.5.1.2, HT).
- "Steel ships float because the sea is denser than steel." → Upthrust from the pressure difference equals the ship's weight (4.5.5.1.2, HT).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Walking on soft snow: do you sink further in trainers or in wide snowshoes?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, both usable as written on TH only (HT content, PIF-F1); 0 usable on TF. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size, with TF items on p = F/A and atmospheric pressure.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 7 (KS3 precedents `r_depth_probe`, `r_altitude_column`; no KS4 figure). Must draw: arrows at right angles to every surface of an object in a fluid; the atmosphere as a thin layer with molecules thinning with height; TH: pressure against depth in a liquid column.

**Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 3. Upthrust and Floating
`upthrust-floating` · Physics / forces · AQA 8463 4.5.5.1.2 (physics only, HT only); not in 8464 · TH

**Family:** MODEL — agrees with BATCH-PLAN: one structure (pressure greater on the bottom than the top) explains upthrust, floating, sinking, ships and submarines.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** Lower a block into water and watch the pressure arrows on top and bottom grow; predict float or sink as weight and density change.

**Route layers:**
- Triple Higher (whole page): upthrust from the pressure difference; factors in floating and sinking. 8463 4.5.5.1.2 (physics only, HT only).
- Archimedes' principle and Upthrust = ρVg are off-spec — not a layer; at most one untested line (UPF-F1).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:**
- None required by the spec. Optional (HT): upthrust on a block with vertical sides. Chains equations? **yes** — Step 1: Δp = Δh × ρ × g (Δh = the block's height); Step 2: F = Δp × A. Convert: cm → m, cm² → m². Every frozen calculation (ρVg, fraction submerged) is off-spec.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
- p = h ρ g — On the sheet (8463, HT). Triangle: p on top; h | ρ | g on the bottom. (Only if the optional calculation is used.)
- p = F / A — On the sheet (8463). Triangle: F on top; p | A on the bottom. (Only if the optional calculation is used.)

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Heavy things sink, light things float." → It depends on the object's density against the fluid's (average density for a hollow ship) (4.5.5.1.2).
- "Upthrust is only there if it floats." → Any partly or fully submerged object has upthrust; it sinks when upthrust is less than its weight (4.5.5.1.2).
- "Water only pushes down on things." → The pressure on the bottom is bigger than on the top, giving a resultant upward force (4.5.5.1.2).
- "A denser object floats higher." → It floats lower; denser than the fluid, it sinks (4.5.5.1.2; UPF-F2).
- "A steel ship floats because the sea is denser than steel." → The hollow hull's average density is less than water's; flooded, it sinks (4.5.5.1.2).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Holding an empty plastic bottle under water in the bath: does it push back up at your hand, or stay put?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, 0 usable: q1 and q2 both need the off-spec upthrust equation (UPF-F1). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size with items on the pressure-difference explanation and floating/sinking factors.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 9 (KS3 precedent `r_float_tank`; no KS4 figure). Must draw: an upright block in a liquid with pressure arrows larger on the bottom face than the top; floating vs sinking with weight and upthrust arrows; a ship hull and a submarine's ballast tanks.

### 4. Distance, Speed and Velocity
`distance-speed-velocity` · Physics / forces · AQA 8464 6.5.4.1.1–6.5.4.1.3; 8463 4.5.6.1.1–4.5.6.1.3 · CF CH TF TH

**Family:** CONTRAST — agrees with BATCH-PLAN: one discriminating difference (direction) separates distance from displacement and speed from velocity; s = v t sits inside it.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A walker on a map: trace a path that doubles back and watch distance keep growing while displacement shrinks; then the same for speed vs velocity.

**Route layers:**
- Higher (CH TH): motion in a circle is constant speed but changing velocity. 8464 6.5.4.1.3; 8463 4.5.6.1.3 (HT only) — one badged line linking to `motion-in-a-circle` (DSV-F2).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:**
- s = v t (v = s ÷ t, t = s ÷ v). Chains equations? no. Convert: km → m, minutes/hours → s, km/h → m/s (÷ 3.6). Base.
- Average speed for non-uniform motion. Chains equations? no when total distance and time are given (add, then divide); **yes** when a leg is given as speed and time — Step 1: s = v t for each leg, add distances and times; Step 2: v = total s ÷ total t. Convert as above. Base.
- Displacement: magnitude and direction by scale drawing. No equation. Base.

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
- s = v t — On the sheet (8464 and 8463). Triangle: s on top; v | t on the bottom. Use s for distance, as the sheet does (DSV-F5).

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Speed and velocity are the same thing." → Velocity is speed in a given direction; a vector (6.5.4.1.3).
- "Distance and displacement are always equal." → Only for one straight line in one direction; a round trip has displacement 0 (6.5.4.1.1).
- "Average speed = the two speeds added and halved." → Average speed = total distance ÷ total time (6.5.4.1.2).
- "Steady speed means steady velocity." → A change of direction changes velocity (6.5.4.1.3; circle case HT).
- "Use minutes or km/h straight in the equation." → s in m, t in s, v in m/s (6.5.4.1.2).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** One lap of a running track back to the start: have you ended up 400 m from where you began, or nowhere?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items, 0 usable as written. q1: option 4 contradicts its own working (DSV-F3) — corrected copy usable on all four routes. q2: two correct options (DSV-F1) — corrected copy usable CH TH only. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 2 (no direct figure; KS3 precedents `r_light_gates`, `r_passing_speeds`). Must draw: a path that changes direction with distance (along the path) and displacement (straight arrow, with direction) both marked; an out-and-back journey.

### 5. Motion in a Circle
`motion-in-a-circle` · Physics / forces · AQA 8464 6.5.4.1.3 (HT only); 8463 4.5.6.1.3 (HT only); 8463 4.8.1.3 (physics only, HT only) · CH TH (site ships TH; moving under the route-flag PR)

**Family:** MODEL — agrees with BATCH-PLAN: one idea (velocity is a vector, so a turn is an acceleration needing an inward resultant force) explains the car, the ball on a string and the orbit.

**Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A ball on a string going round at steady speed with its velocity arrow turning; cut the string at any point and predict the path.

**Route layers:**
- Higher (whole page, CH TH): constant speed, changing velocity; acceleration and resultant force towards the centre; force removed → straight line. 8464 6.5.4.1.3; 8463 4.5.6.1.3 (HT only).
- Triple Higher: gravity provides the force for an orbit; for a stable orbit the radius must change if the speed changes (faster → smaller radius). 8463 4.8.1.3 (physics only, HT only) (MIC-F5).

**Required practical:** none.

**Calculations:** none (F = mv²/r is off-spec, MIC-F4).

**Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):** none.

**Misconceptions to confront:**
- "Steady speed means no acceleration." → Direction changes, so velocity changes: it is accelerating (6.5.4.1.3).
- "There's a force pushing it outwards." → The resultant force points towards the centre (6.5.4.1.3 with 6.5.4.2.2).
- "Cut the string and it flies straight out from the centre." → It moves off in a straight line along the tangent (6.5.4.2.1).
- "Circular motion has its own special force." → An existing force (friction, tension, gravity) supplies the inward pull (MIC-F4).
- "A faster satellite needs a higher orbit." → A faster stable orbit has a smaller radius (8463 4.8.1.3, TH; MIC-F1).

**"Start here" access note (rule 1):** Swing a bag round and let go: does it fly straight out from your hand, or off in the direction it was already moving?

**Practice (rule 4):** 2 frozen items. q1 usable as written on CH TH. q2 not usable as written (MIC-F1) — corrected copy usable TH only. Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.

**Diagrams:** `05-diagram-library/README.md` row 10 (nothing existing). Must draw: an object on a circle with the velocity arrow tangential at several points and the resultant force pointing to the centre; the straight-line path when the force is removed; TH: two circular orbits, the inner one faster.

### 6. Distance–Time Graphs
`distance-time-graphs` · physics / forces · AQA refs (8464 6.5.4.1.4; 8463 4.5.6.1.4) · true routes CF CH TF TH

- **Family:** QUANTITATIVE — kept: the gradient calculation (Δd ÷ Δt) carries the concept.
- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A journey that draws its own d–t line as it runs; pupil predicts the shape of each section, then measures the gradient.
- **Route layers:**
  - Tangent to a curve → instantaneous speed — 8464 6.5.4.1.4 (HT only) / 8463 4.5.6.1.4 (HT only).
- **Required practical:** none.
- **Calculations:**
  - Speed from a straight section: speed = Δd ÷ Δt (s = vt). Chains equations? **no**. Convert: minutes → s, km → m, cm → m. Base.
  - Average speed for a whole non-uniform journey: total distance ÷ total time. Chains equations? **no**. Convert: as above. Base.
  - Instantaneous speed from a tangent: draw tangent, read two points on it, Δd ÷ Δt. Chains equations? **no** (one equation, extra drawing step taught as its own line). Convert: as above. HT.
- **Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
  - s = v t — **On the sheet** (8464 and 8463, June 2026). Triangle: s top / v × t bottom.
  - speed = Δd ÷ Δt (gradient) — **Learn it** (a graph method, not printed). Triangle: Δd top / speed × Δt bottom.
- **Misconceptions to confront:**
  - "Flat line means steady speed." → flat = zero gradient = stationary (6.5.4.1.4).
  - "A higher line means faster." → speed is the slope, not the height.
  - "A d–t graph shows the route it took." → it plots distance against time, not a map.
  - "Downward line = slowing down." → downward = moving back towards the start (only on a distance-from-start axis); slowing down is a curve getting shallower.
  - "Gradient of a curve is just end distance ÷ end time." → that is average speed; instantaneous speed needs a tangent (HT).
- **"Start here" access note (rule 1):** walking to the shop then stopping to chat — is the line going up or flat while you chat?
- **Practice (rule 4):** frozen quiz items usable — CF 1, CH 1, TF 1, TH 1 (q2). Not usable as written: q1 (DTG-F1, wrong-answer explanations swapped). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.
- **Diagrams:** see `05-diagram-library/README.md` (#3, `motion_graphs()` d–t panel). Must draw: d–t graph with stationary / constant-speed / returning sections (axis "distance from start"), a curve with a tangent (HT), a gradient triangle.

### 7. Acceleration
`acceleration` · physics / forces · AQA refs (8464 6.5.4.1.5; 8463 4.5.6.1.5) · true routes CF CH TF TH

- **Family:** QUANTITATIVE — kept: a = Δv ÷ t and v² − u² = 2as carry the lesson; the v–t graph is their picture.
- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A v–t graph drawn live from a moving trolley; pupil reads acceleration from the slope (and, HT, distance from the area).
- **Route layers:**
  - Area under v–t = distance; counting squares — 8464 6.5.4.1.5 (HT only) / 8463 4.5.6.1.5 (HT only).
  - v–t graphs for objects reaching terminal velocity; interpret the forces — 8463 4.5.6.1.5 (physics only).
  - (Acceleration at constant speed in a circle — 6.5.4.1.3 (HT only) — belongs to motion-in-a-circle; not here.)
- **Required practical:** none on this page. The force/mass/acceleration practical (Combined RP19, 8464 6.5.4.2.2 / Physics RP7, 8463 4.5.6.2.2, all four routes) belongs to newtons-laws — verified.
- **Calculations:**
  - a = Δv ÷ t (incl. negative a for deceleration). Chains equations? **no**. Convert: km/h → m/s, minutes → s. Base.
  - v² − u² = 2as for v, u, a or s. Chains equations? **no** — but the square root (and ×2 / adding u²) is its own line. Convert: km/h → m/s, cm/km → m. **Base** (the source's `higher` field is wrong — ACC-F2).
  - Find a with a = Δv ÷ t, then s with v² − u² = 2as. Chains equations? **yes** — Step 1 a = Δv ÷ t; Step 2 s = (v² − u²) ÷ 2a. Convert: as above. Base.
  - a from the gradient of a v–t graph. Chains? **no**. Base.
  - Distance from the area under a v–t graph (triangle + rectangle / trapezium; counting squares). Chains? **no** (areas added, one idea). HT.
  - Free fall: a ≈ 9.8 m/s² used in v² − u² = 2as (e.g. speed after falling h). Chains? **no**. Base.
- **Equations that need formula triangles (rule 2):**
  - a = Δv ÷ t — **On the sheet** (8464 and 8463). Triangle: Δv top / a × t bottom. Δv = v − u is its own line.
  - v² − u² = 2as — **On the sheet** (8464 and 8463). Triangle: (v² − u²) top / 2 × a × s bottom. Square: "add u², then square-root" and "÷ 2" are each their own line. Show the sheet form, not v² = u² + 2as.
- **Misconceptions to confront:**
  - "Acceleration means going fast." → acceleration = rate of change of velocity (6.5.4.1.5).
  - "Flat line on a v–t graph means stopped." → flat = constant velocity; stopped is a line along v = 0.
  - "v–t gradient gives distance." → gradient = acceleration; distance is the area (HT).
  - "Slowing down isn't acceleration." → it is; it is deceleration, a negative a for forward motion.
  - "Heavier things fall faster." → near Earth, free fall a ≈ 9.8 m/s² for all objects (without air resistance).
- **"Start here" access note (rule 1):** a bus pulling away vs a sports car pulling away — which gets to 30 mph sooner, and does that mean more acceleration?
- **Practice (rule 4):** frozen quiz items usable — CF 0, CH 1, TF 0, TH 1 (q2, HT as written). Not usable as written: q1 (ACC-F1, distractor's working gives the key). Design tops the bank up to the batch's fixed size.
- **Diagrams:** see `05-diagram-library/README.md` (#4, `motion_graphs()` v–t panel). Must draw: v–t graph with constant / accelerating / decelerating sections; shaded area (HT); terminal-velocity v–t curve (TF TH).
- **Examiner tip:** none approved.

### 8. Newton's Laws of Motion
`newtons-laws` · physics / forces · AQA refs (8464 6.5.4.2.1–6.5.4.2.3; 8463 4.5.6.2.1–4.5.6.2.3) · true routes CF CH TF TH

- **Flagship (a suggestion, not a spec):** A trolley on a track: change force or mass, predict the acceleration, then see the a–F line.
- **Route layers:**
  - Inertia — 8464 6.5.4.2.1 (HT only) / 8463 4.5.6.2.1 (HT only).
