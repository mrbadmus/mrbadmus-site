**Which line was followed.** The Rainford scheme. It puts selective breeding (16b) in this unit with no page, so it is taught here, after natural selection, where the comparison does the most work. The NC KS3 programme has no selective breeding line; it is scheme-only.

**Gaps and mismatches.** Rainford 16b selective breeding had no page anywhere: now three cards and a reveal table comparing it with natural selection (who selects, which features win, how long, examples, effect on variation). The old page never named Darwin or Wallace in its main text; it now does. The five steps are drawn (new figure `b11-selection-steps`) and the moths on two barks are redrawn at phone width (new figure `b11-moth-barks`). Rainford 15b speciation is not in B11; see B10 `what-makes-a-species`.

**Quiz notes.** 
- Rung 1 (recall), all four options cut to 15 words or fewer, dash removed. Key B: "The proportions in the population — more of the moths born were dark, because dark parents survived to breed" → "The proportion of dark moths rose, because dark parents survived to breed" (KEY TEXT CHANGED).
- Rung 2 (apply), key D: "Because changes made during an animal's life do not alter the genetic information it passes on" → "Because stretching does not change its genes, so calves cannot inherit it" (KEY TEXT CHANGED; now the same "X, so Y" shape as A to C).
- Rungs 3 and 4 stems rewritten in short sentences (render only); seven ticks shown in shorter words, one now also checks step 2 (more are born than survive). Feedback for every wrong option of Rungs 1 and 2.

**Checked numbers.** Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859; Lamarck's giraffe idea, 1809. Darwin noticed the Galápagos mockingbirds differing by island; the finch beak story came from later scientists. Peppered moths changed in about 50 years. Bacteria can divide about every 20 minutes. Rung 4: 99% killed.
