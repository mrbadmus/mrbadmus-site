**Which line was followed.** NC C15/C16 and Rainford 17a agree. Row 17a is shared with the-ph-scale-and-indicators: this lesson places acids, alkalis and neutral on the scale; the indicator lesson teaches the colours, litmus and the advantages of universal indicator in full.

**Gaps and mismatches.** The old page never drew the pH scale (it was on the indicator page only); the same banded scale is now drawn here. The old stored feedback gave orange juice as pH about 3; the page says about 4 (orange juice is about pH 3.5 to 4), which sits in the weakly acidic band the key of Rung 2 relies on. Lemon juice is named as an acid with no pH. One set of universal indicator colours and bands is used across C6 lessons 1 to 3: pH 0 to 2 red (strongly acidic), 3 to 6 orange or yellow (weakly acidic), 7 green (neutral), 8 to 10 blue (weakly alkaline), 11 to 14 purple (strongly alkaline). These match the old pH bench bands and the C6 question bank.

**Checked numbers.** Dilute hydrochloric acid about pH 1, vinegar about pH 3, orange juice about pH 4, pure water and salt solution pH 7, baking soda solution about pH 8 (sodium hydrogencarbonate 8.3), bleach about pH 12, oven cleaner (sodium hydroxide) about pH 13.

**Left out because unsure.** Garden lime: the page calls it lime (calcium hydroxide), which is slaked lime; much garden lime sold is ground limestone (calcium carbonate). "Acids contain hydrogen ions" (the old vocabulary line "releases hydrogen into solution" is GCSE wording; left to ks4_becomes). Tasting acids is not mentioned as a test.
