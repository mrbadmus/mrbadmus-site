Internal energy

Turn the gas up under boiling water.

A pan of water is boiling on a hob. A thermometer in the water reads 100 °C. You turn the flame up to full and keep it there.

The particles that make up a substance store energy in two ways. They move, so they have kinetic energy. There are forces between them, so they also have potential energy, which depends on how far apart they are. Internal energy is the total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles that make up a system. Heating a system increases its internal energy, and that does one of two things: it raises the temperature, or it changes the state. Think again

“Keep heating it and it must keep getting hotter.”

Heating always raises the internal energy, but not always the temperature. Temperature follows the particles’ average kinetic energy. While a pure substance melts or boils, the energy supplied goes into the particles’ potential energy instead, so the thermometer holds still until the change of state is complete. Then the temperature rises again.

The internal energy rose at all four stages. The temperature rose at only two.

The temperature of a gas is related to the average kinetic energy of its particles: how energetic a typical particle is. Internal energy is the total over every particle, kinetic and potential. So a large object at a low temperature can hold far more internal energy than a small hot one. The two effects of heating each have an equation, both on the equation sheet, which the next lessons use: raising the temperature, ΔE = m c Δθ; changing state, E = m L.

Think again · hotter is not more

“The coffee is hotter, so it has more internal energy.”

Cooling runs the heating curve backwards. Energy leaves, so the internal energy falls the whole way down, and the flat sections are where the substance condenses or freezes.
