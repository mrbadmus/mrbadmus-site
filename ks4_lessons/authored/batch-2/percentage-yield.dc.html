Think again · weighing

“My yield came out at 108%. My technique must be better than perfect.”

Manufacturers want a high yield. A low yield wastes raw materials, which raises costs, and the wasted reactants become waste the factory has to deal with. Many raw materials are finite, so less waste is more sustainable. In the Haber process only about 15% of the nitrogen and hydrogen becomes ammonia on each pass, so the unreacted gases are recycled, raising the overall yield to about 98%.

Equation

Learn it · chemistry has no equation sheet

Percentage yield (%) = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100

Both yields in the same unit before you divide.

n = m ÷ Mr → mole ratio → m = n × Mr

How you get the theoretical yield from a mass of reactant.

Where the theoretical yield comes from

On the Higher paper you work out the theoretical yield yourself, from the mass of a reactant and the balanced equation. Find the moles of reactant (n = m ÷ Mr), use the ratio in the equation to get the moles of product, then turn that back into a mass (m = n × Mr). If one reactant is in excess, start from the limiting reactant: the one that is used up.
