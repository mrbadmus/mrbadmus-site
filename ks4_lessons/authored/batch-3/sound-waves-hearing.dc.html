Sound in air is a longitudinal wave: a pattern of compressions, where the air is squeezed together, and rarefactions, where it is spread out. It needs particles to travel through, so it cannot cross a vacuum. To be heard, that pattern has to be turned into something your body can sense. Think again

“The ear drum is the part that hears the sound.”

The ear drum only vibrates; on its own it senses nothing. Hearing needs the whole chain: sound waves make the ear drum and the parts behind it vibrate, and that causes the sensation of sound.

Turning sound waves into vibrations of a solid is not special to the ear. Any solid that sound reaches is pushed and pulled by the compressions and rarefactions, so it vibrates at the sound's frequency. It works the other way round too: a vibrating solid pushes and pulls on the air next to it and sends out sound waves.

When sound passes into a new material, its frequency stays the same: each compression still arrives once per cycle, which is why the ear drum vibrates at the sound's frequency. The speed changes, so the wavelength changes with it. Sound usually travels fastest in solids and slowest in gases, because the particles in a solid are closer together and more strongly linked, so each vibration is passed on more quickly: about 340 m/s in air and 1500 m/s in water.

Equations

On the sheet

v = f λ

wave speed (v, metres per second) = frequency (f, hertz) × wavelength (λ, metres)

λ = v ÷ f · f = v ÷ λ

Units first

Frequency must be in hertz. 1 kHz = 1000 Hz: multiply a kilohertz value by 1000 before you insert it.

Key fact

Hearing is a conversion: sound waves make the ear drum and the parts behind it vibrate. In a human ear, the conversion only works from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
