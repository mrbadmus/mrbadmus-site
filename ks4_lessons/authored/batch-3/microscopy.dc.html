Microscopy

A school microscope shows you a cell’s nucleus but never its ribosomes. What does a microscope need in order to show smaller things, and how do you work out how big a cell really is?

Start here

Every cell is full of ribosomes. For nearly 300 years, nobody saw one.

Robert Hooke looked at cork through a microscope in 1665 and named the tiny boxes he saw cells. Better and better light microscopes followed. Ribosomes were there in every cell the whole time, and none of those microscopes ever showed one.

A light microscope passes light through a thin specimen and magnifies it with glass lenses, up to about ×2000. It can show living cells, but it cannot separate two points closer together than about 200 nm. The electron microscope, invented in the 1930s, uses a beam of electrons instead. It magnifies up to about ×2 000 000 and separates points about 0.1 nm apart, but the specimen sits in a vacuum, so it must be dead.

Magnification is how many times bigger the image is than the real object. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two points that are close together as separate points.

Think again · empty magnification

“Zoom in further and the blur will split into two ribosomes.”

Enlarging an image adds no detail. The light microscope never recorded two separate points, so a bigger copy is just a bigger blur. Detail comes from resolution: to see two ribosomes 40 nm apart you need a microscope that can resolve them, and that means an electron microscope.

Required practical · RP1 Combined Science · RP1 Biology

Prepare a slide. Focus. Draw what you see.

{{ slideFig }}
  1. Peel a thin layer of epidermis from a piece of onion with forceps. Lay it flat in a drop of iodine solution on a microscope slide.
  2. Lower a coverslip at an angle with a mounted needle, so no air bubbles are trapped.
  3. Put the slide on the stage and start with the lowest-power objective.
  4. Watching from the side, raise the stage until the slide is just below the lens. Then, looking through the eyepiece, lower it with the coarse focus until the cells appear. Sharpen them with the fine focus.
  5. Switch to a higher-power objective and refocus with the fine focus only.
  6. Draw what you see with a sharp pencil: clear, continuous lines and no shading. Label with ruled lines, and add a title and the magnification or a scale bar.

Risks. Iodine solution irritates the eyes and stains skin: wear eye protection. Slides and coverslips are thin glass: hold them by the edges.

A drawing or a photograph also tells you a real size, if you know its magnification. Cells are measured in micrometres (µm), and the structures inside them in nanometres (nm). Very large and very small answers go in standard form: ×2 000 000 is ×2 × 10⁶, and 0.00015 mm is 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ mm.

mm ×1000 → µm

Image size and real size in the same unit before you divide. Magnification has no unit.

Key fact

More magnification without more resolution only makes a bigger blur. The electron microscope’s far higher resolution is what let biologists see ribosomes and the inside of mitochondria.
