Start here

“Do not burn, even after use.”

Every aerosol can carries that warning. An “empty” can is not empty: once the spray runs out, it is still sealed and still full of gas.

A gas is mostly empty space. Its molecules are in constant random motion: each one travels in a straight line, in any direction, until it hits another molecule or a wall of the container, then sets off in a new direction. Every hit on a wall gives that part of the wall a tiny push. Huge numbers of hits every second add up to a steady push on every part of the wall: the pressure of the gas. The bench models a sealed can with sixteen molecules. Heat it, and call what changes before it happens.

Think again

“Heat a gas and its molecules get bigger.”

The molecules on the bench stayed exactly the same size. Heating changes how fast molecules move, never how big they are. Faster molecules, not bigger ones, are why the hot gas pushes harder.

Think again

“The molecules bump into each other more, so the pressure goes up.”

Watch two molecules collide in the middle of the can: that bump pushes on no wall at all. Only hits on the walls push on the walls. The pressure goes up because the molecules hit the walls more often, and harder.

A gas can be compressed or expanded by changing the pressure on it. The pressure of a gas produces a net force at right angles to the wall of its container, and to any surface the gas touches. Here the right-hand wall is a piston, moved slowly enough for the gas to stay at the same temperature: its molecules keep the same average speed. Predict the gauge before the piston moves.

Equations

On the sheet

p V = constant

pressure (Pa) × volume (m³) = constant, for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature.

p₁ V₁ = p₂ V₂ · p₂ = p₁ V₁ ÷ V₂ · V₂ = p₁ V₁ ÷ p₂

Units first

The two pressures must be in the same unit, and so must the two volumes. Convert only when they arrive in different units.

1 kPa = 1000 Pa · 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³

Key fact

Gas pressure comes from molecules hitting the walls. Heat a gas at constant volume and its molecules move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder: the pressure rises.
