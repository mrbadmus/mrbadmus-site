Start here

Same temperature, worse burn.

A splash of boiling water on your hand hurts. A puff of steam from a kettle spout, at the same 100 °C, can leave a much worse burn.

When a substance changes state, the energy supplied changes its internal energy but not its temperature. The energy needed to change state is called latent heat. The specific latent heat of a substance is the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of it with no change in temperature. Melting, solid to liquid, takes the specific latent heat of fusion. Boiling, liquid to vapour, takes the specific latent heat of vaporisation. Freezing and condensing give the same energy back. For water, fusion takes 334 000 J/kg and vaporisation 2 260 000 J/kg.

Think again

“It stays at 100 °C, so no energy is transferred.”

A steady temperature means the particles’ average kinetic energy is not changing. It does not mean no energy is moving. As steam condenses, its particles come together and the bonds between them form, which releases energy while the temperature holds at 100 °C. That energy is E = m L. With Δθ = 0, m c Δθ cannot see it.

Equations · choose by what is changing

On the sheet

E = m L

State changing, temperature constant. Energy for a change of state (J) = mass (kg) × specific latent heat (J/kg).

m = E ÷ L · L = E ÷ m

On the sheet

ΔE = m c Δθ

Temperature changing, state constant. c is the specific heat capacity (J/kg °C).

On the sheet

E = P t

Energy transferred at a steady rate: power (W) × time (s).

On a temperature–time graph, a sloping section means the temperature is changing: ΔE = m c Δθ. A flat section means the state is changing: E = m L. Run it backwards on a cooling graph, and the flat sections are where the substance condenses or freezes, giving energy out. When energy goes in or out at a steady rate, the length of a flat section measures the energy, through E = P t.
