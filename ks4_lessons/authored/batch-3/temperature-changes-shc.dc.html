Temperature changes and specific heat capacity

Heat two things with the same energy and one ends up far hotter. What decides how far a temperature rises, and how do you measure the property that decides it?

Start here

Scorching sand, cool sea.

On a sunny afternoon, dry sand on a beach gets too hot to walk on, while the sea next to it hardly warms up. Both have had the same sunshine all day.

Heat a material and its temperature rises. How far it rises depends on three things: the mass being heated, the material, and the energy put in. The material’s part is its specific heat capacity, c: the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of the substance by 1 °C. Water’s is 4200 J/kg °C, one of the highest of any common substance. One equation links all three to the temperature change, Δθ.

Equations

On the sheet

ΔE = m c Δθ

change in thermal energy (J) = mass (kg) × specific heat capacity (J/kg °C) × temperature change (°C)

Δθ = ΔE ÷ (m c) · c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ) · m = ΔE ÷ (c Δθ)

On the sheet

E = P t

energy transferred (J) = power (W) × time (s). How an electric heater’s energy is found.

P = E ÷ t · t = E ÷ P

Mass, material, energy

Rank the temperature rises.

Identical heaters give each sample the energy on its card. Water: c = 4200 J/kg °C. Aluminium: c = 900 J/kg °C. Tap the samples in order, biggest rise first.

Required practical {{ rpNum }} · the AQA method

Heat a block with a known energy. Measure its temperature rise.

  1. Measure the mass of the metal block on a balance.
  2. Put the heater and the thermometer in the block’s two holes, with a few drops of water in the thermometer hole for good contact. Wrap the block in insulation.
  3. Connect the heater to a low-voltage supply through a joulemeter, or with an ammeter and a voltmeter. Record the starting temperature.
  4. Switch on and start the stopclock. Record the temperature every minute for 10 minutes.
  5. Switch off. Record the energy supplied (the joulemeter reading, or E = V × I × t) and the highest temperature the block reaches.
  6. Work out Δθ, then c = ΔE ÷ (m × Δθ).
Independent
Time of heating, which sets the energy supplied.
Dependent
The temperature of the block.
Control
The block’s mass and material, the heater’s power, the insulation.
Risks
The heater and block get hot enough to burn: do not touch them, and let them cool on a heat-proof mat. Clamp the thermometer so it cannot roll off and break.

Think again

“Δθ is the temperature it ended at.”

Δθ is a change: how far the temperature moved, not where it finished. The block started well above 0 °C, so the finishing temperature overstates the rise and c comes out too small. Δθ = highest − starting temperature.

Key fact

ΔE = m c Δθ, with Δθ = final − initial. Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 °C.
