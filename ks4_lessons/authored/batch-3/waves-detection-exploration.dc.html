Earthquakes produce seismic waves that travel right through the Earth. There are two kinds. P-waves are longitudinal: they travel through solids and through liquids, at different speeds in each, and arrive first. S-waves are transverse: they travel through solids but cannot travel through a liquid. Seismometers all round the world record which waves arrive, and when. Use those records to work out what is inside.

Think again

“S-waves can't pass through the core.”

Only half right, and it costs marks. P-waves do pass through the core: that is how they reach 180°. What stops S-waves is the liquid outer core. Write it as: S-waves cannot travel through the liquid outer core; P-waves can.

No one can drill to the core or look at it. The study of seismic waves gave scientists new evidence about parts of the Earth that cannot be observed directly: the liquid outer core and the size of the core were both worked out from records like these.

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above the upper limit of human hearing, 20 kHz. To scan an unborn baby, a probe held on the skin sends short pulses of ultrasound into the body and listens for echoes. On the way to the baby, the pulse meets several boundaries where one kind of tissue meets another.

Think again

“The ultrasound bounces straight back off the first thing it meets.”

If it did, nothing deeper could ever be seen. Ultrasound is only partially reflected at a boundary; the rest is transmitted and reaches the deeper boundaries, which is how a scan shows layer after layer.

Ultrasound is not ionising, so unlike X-rays it can safely scan an unborn baby. The same echo timing finds hidden cracks inside metal parts: industrial imaging. At sea, echo sounding uses pulses of high-frequency sound to measure the depth of water and to detect objects in deep water, such as shoals of fish or a wreck. Every time, the pulse goes there and back, so the boundary is half the distance travelled away.

Equations

On the sheet

s = v t

distance travelled (s, metres) = speed (v, metres per second) × time (t, seconds)

v = s ÷ t · t = s ÷ v

For an echo

The time is for the trip there and back. Depth of the boundary = distance travelled ÷ 2. This is a method step, not a separate equation.

Units first

Echo times are often given in milliseconds. 1 ms = 0.001 s: divide by 1000 before you insert it.

Key fact

S-waves cannot cross the liquid outer core; P-waves can, but refract. An echo's time gives a boundary's depth: depth = speed × time ÷ 2.
