Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation

Unstable nuclei give out radiation at random. Sort alpha, beta and gamma by what they are, how far they go and how strongly they ionise, then pick the right one for a job.

Unstable nuclei

Decay is random.

Some atomic nuclei are unstable. The nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable. This is radioactive decay, and it is random: you cannot predict when any one nucleus will decay.

Activity

The rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays, measured in becquerel (Bq). 1 Bq is one decay per second.

Count-rate

The number of decays recorded each second by a detector, such as a Geiger–Müller tube. A detector records only some of the decays, so count-rate is not the activity.

What a nucleus can emit

Alpha particle (α)

Two neutrons and two protons: the same as a helium nucleus.

Beta particle (β)

A high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton.

Gamma ray (γ)

Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus.

Neutron (n)

Some nuclei emit a neutron.

Uses and hazards

The properties pick the job.

Smoke alarm · alpha

Alpha ionises the air in the alarm so a small current flows. Smoke absorbs the alpha, the current drops, and the alarm sounds. Its short range keeps it inside the alarm.

Paper thickness · beta

Beta partly passes through paper. If the count-rate falls, the paper is getting thicker, and the rollers are adjusted.

Sterilising · gamma

Gamma passes through sealed packaging and kills microbes on medical equipment. Irradiating an object does not make it radioactive.

Ionising radiation damages living cells. Alpha is most dangerous inside the body, where all its ionisation happens in nearby cells; outside, skin stops it. Gamma is the hazard from outside, because it reaches the organs. Keep your distance: alpha and beta only travel a short way in air, and gamma gets weaker further away. Use shielding, and keep exposure time short.

Key fact

Radioactive decay is random. Alpha is the most ionising and least penetrating; gamma is the least ionising and most penetrating; beta is between.

Key note · AQA 6.4.2.1

Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation

  1. 01Some nuclei are unstable and emit radiation as they change to become more stable. Decay is random.
  2. 02Activity: the rate of decay, in becquerel (Bq). Count-rate: decays recorded each second by a detector.
  3. 03Emissions: alpha (2 protons, 2 neutrons), beta (high-speed electron from the nucleus, as a neutron becomes a proton), gamma (EM radiation), neutron.
  4. 04Alpha: a few cm in air, stopped by paper, most ionising. Beta: up to about a metre, stopped by a few mm of aluminium. Gamma: very long range, reduced by thick lead, least ionising.
  5. 05Uses follow the properties: smoke alarms (alpha), paper thickness (beta), sterilising (gamma).
  6. 06Alpha is most dangerous inside the body; gamma from outside. Distance, shielding and short exposure reduce the risk.
