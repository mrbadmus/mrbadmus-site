Required practical · pH and amylase

Start here

Chew a plain cracker for a minute. It starts to taste sweet.

A cracker is mostly starch, and starch does not taste sweet. Keep a piece in your mouth without swallowing, and after a minute or so it tastes sugary.

If you had to guess, is the sugar made by the chewing, or by something in your saliva?

Enzymes are biological catalysts: proteins that speed up reactions in living things without being used up. Each enzyme has an active site, a region with a specific shape. Only a substrate whose shape fits the active site can bind to it, which is why amylase digests starch but not protein or fat. The lock and key model is a simplified picture of this: the active site is the lock, the substrate is the key.

pH changes the shape of the active site too. Each enzyme has an optimum pH, matched to where it works. The protease made in the stomach works best in strong acid; enzymes in the small intestine work best near neutral, because bile, which is alkaline, neutralises the stomach acid. Far from its optimum pH, an enzyme is denatured. AQA's required practical measures this for amylase.

Required practical · RP4 Combined Science · RP5 Biology

Change the pH. Time how long the starch lasts.

  1. Put one drop of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile.
  2. Put 2 cm³ of amylase solution and 1 cm³ of a pH buffer in one test tube, and 2 cm³ of starch solution in another. Stand both in a water bath at 35 °C for 5 minutes.
  3. Mix the two and start the stopwatch.
  4. Every 30 seconds, put one drop of the mixture into a fresh well of iodine.
  5. Stop when a drop leaves the iodine orange-brown: the starch is all digested. Record the time.
  6. Repeat with buffers of other pH values, and repeat each pH to calculate a mean.

Risks. Iodine solution irritates the eyes and stains: wear eye protection. Amylase can irritate skin and eyes, so avoid contact. Keep the water bath below 40 °C so it cannot scald.

Equation · processing the data

Learn it

rate = 1 ÷ time

time in seconds (s); rate in per second (s⁻¹).

A shorter time is a faster rate. Plotted as rate against pH, the optimum shows as a peak; plotted as time, it would be a dip.

Key fact

An enzyme works fastest at its optimum temperature and optimum pH. Too hot, or too far from its optimum pH, and the active site changes shape: the enzyme is denatured, and the substrate no longer fits.

    Not sure why a denatured enzyme stops working?

    The lock and key bench is a simplified model: real active sites are not rigid outlines, and the molecules are not drawn to scale. The practical is simulated. End-point times come from a model amylase with its optimum near pH 6 at 35 °C, with a small random scatter, and sampling every 30 seconds rounds each time up to the next sample. Groups B and C are invented results, and one of them is anomalous on purpose.
