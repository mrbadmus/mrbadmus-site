Fuels and energy resources

Wood is renewable. Nuclear is not. Which one puts more carbon into the air?

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

Renewable and dirty. Non-renewable and clean.

Burning wood releases carbon dioxide, soot and particulates every time. A nuclear power station releases no carbon dioxide at all while generating. Wood grows back; uranium does not.

Commit. What does that tell you about the word "renewable"?

    •

It answers one question and one only: will it run out? It says nothing about carbon, nothing about pollution, nothing about safety, and nothing about cost. Two independent questions get collapsed into one word, and that is the mistake this whole lesson is built to take apart.

Almost every resource below traces back to the Sun, and almost every one is a chemical or nuclear store being emptied into a pathway. What differs is whether the store refills on a human timescale, and what else comes out while you use it. Those are two axes, not one.

Key fact

Renewable means the store refills on a human timescale. It is a separate question from how much carbon, how much pollution, or how reliable — and the answers do not line up.

!

Think again

“Renewable means clean, and non-renewable means polluting.”

Commit. Your own grid is the evidence.

    •

Look at where the corners are occupied. Nuclear is non-renewable and emits almost no carbon. Wood is renewable and emits a great deal, along with particulates that damage lungs. Both of those cells would have to be empty if the two words meant the same thing.

There are more axes than two, and honest comparison needs all of them: carbon while generating, other pollution, land taken, waste produced and for how long, reliability, cost, and where the fuel comes from. Hydroelectric floods valleys. Solar panels need mining and manufacturing. Nuclear produces waste that stays dangerous for millennia. Gas is reliable and emits carbon. None of these is a clean win, and anyone telling you one of them is has stopped counting axes early.

Being able to hold two independent questions apart is not a debating trick. It is the difference between an opinion and an argument.

“Electricity is a clean energy resource.”

Electricity is not a resource at all. It is a pathway — a way of moving energy from wherever it was generated to wherever it is wanted — and it is exactly as clean as whatever filled the store at the far end. The same electric car is close to zero-carbon on Norwegian hydro and considerably worse than a small diesel on Polish coal. Asking whether electricity is clean is asking about the wrong end of the wire.

Key fact

Renewable means the resource is replaced as fast as it is used. It is a claim about supply, not about cleanliness — and every resource, renewable or not, carries a cost somewhere.

Key note

Renewable answers one question: does the store refill? Carbon, pollution, reliability, land and waste are separate questions with different answers. Nuclear is non-renewable and low-carbon; wood is renewable and smoky. Both corners are occupied.

Going further

Almost everything on the grid is the Sun, wearing a disguise. Coal is a Carboniferous forest that photosynthesised three hundred million years ago and never fully rotted. Oil is ancient plankton. Wind exists because sunlight heats some parts of the atmosphere more than others. Hydroelectric power runs on water the Sun lifted into the sky as vapour. Even the food in your own chemical store traces back to a plant catching light. Only three things on the list break the pattern: geothermal, which is heat left over from the Earth's formation plus radioactive decay in the mantle; tidal, which is the Moon's gravity; and nuclear, which is energy locked into heavy atoms by exploding stars long before the Sun existed. Everything else is sunlight with a delay — and the length of that delay is exactly what "renewable" is measuring.

Ask about this lesson

The grid is a teaching model. Each resource is scored on carbon, availability on demand and land use on a simple scale so that the three axes can be compared at a glance; the scores are relative rankings rather than measured figures, and every one of them varies with the technology, the site and the country. Carbon is counted while generating, so the emissions of building a wind turbine or mining uranium are not in the column. The point of the grid is that renewable and clean are different questions, not that any cell is a precise number.
