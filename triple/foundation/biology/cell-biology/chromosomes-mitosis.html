Chromosomes, the cell cycle and mitosis

You began as one cell. How does one cell become two that carry exactly the same chromosomes, again and again, without losing a single one?

One fertilised egg. Trillions of cells.

Every cell in your skin, muscles and gut came from one fertilised egg dividing again and again, and nearly every body cell still carries the same 46 chromosomes it had. Here is a cell cut down to 4 chromosomes so you can follow every one.

Commit first

This cell will divide into two cells, and each must end up with all 4 chromosomes. How?

The nucleus of a cell contains chromosomes, each one a long molecule of DNA. Each chromosome carries a large number of genes: a gene is a small section of DNA that codes for a particular protein. In body cells the chromosomes are found in pairs, one of each pair from each parent. A human body cell has 46, in 23 pairs. Sperm and egg cells carry one of each pair, 23 in all, so fertilisation restores 46. In the model, red chromosomes came from one parent and blue from the other.

Mitosis makes cells identical to the parent, so it is used wherever an organism needs more of the same cells: growth and development from a fertilised egg, repair of damaged tissue, and replacement of worn-out cells. In mature animals, cell division is mainly restricted to repair and replacement. Some organisms reproduce asexually this way: one parent, no gametes, only mitosis, so every offspring is a clone. In animals, mitosis never makes gametes.

Every stage of the cycle is controlled. Cancer is the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division, forming a mass of cells called a tumour. A benign tumour stays in one area, usually within a membrane, and does not invade other parts of the body. Malignant tumour cells are cancers: they invade neighbouring tissues and spread in the blood to other parts of the body, where they form secondary tumours.

Key fact

The cell grows and its DNA replicates; in mitosis one set of chromosomes is pulled to each end and the nucleus divides; then the cytoplasm and membrane divide into two identical cells.
