States of matter

You keep heating a solid, but the thermometer stops rising. Where is the energy going, and how do you find a melting point from messy readings?

Combined · TripleFoundation · Higher

Start here · an exam scenario

A tube of white wax in a hot-water bath.

A student puts a boiling tube of solid stearic acid, a waxy substance, into a beaker of water at 90 °C. She stirs the wax with the thermometer and reads the temperature every 30 seconds for ten minutes. The water bath never stops heating the tube.

Commit first

While the wax is melting, what does the thermometer do?

In a solid, particles are packed in a regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions. In a liquid, they are still close together but randomly arranged, and they move past each other. In a gas, they are far apart and move quickly in all directions. Melting and boiling need energy, because the forces between the particles have to be overcome. The stronger those forces, the more energy it takes, and the higher the melting or boiling point. Run the investigation and watch the energy go.

State symbols · AQA 5.2.2.2

(s), (l), (g) or (aq)?

In an equation each substance carries its state. (aq) means dissolved in water, and nothing else.

Key fact

While a pure substance melts or boils its temperature stays constant: the energy goes into overcoming the forces between particles. It is a physical change; the substance is unchanged.

Examiner tip

Melting and boiling are physical changes — the particles rearrange but the substance is unchanged (ice, water and steam are all H₂O), so don't call them chemical changes. And use the state symbol (aq) only for something dissolved in water, not for any liquid.

The investigation is simulated. Readings include small random scatter of about half a degree and one deliberately misread value, so that anomalies can be identified. Stearic acid's melting point is taken as 69 °C; the water bath is modelled at 90 °C, so the liquid levels off below it. Particle pictures are the simple sphere model.
