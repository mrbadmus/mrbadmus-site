AQA Chemistry (8462) 4.8.3.2 · Classify

Metal hydroxides

A few drops of sodium hydroxide, and a solid appears in the tube. Can its colour name the metal ion, and what do you do when the solid is white?

Triple Science · Foundation tier
  • Triple Science · Higher tier
Required practical · identifying ions

Start here

Blue solution in. Blue solid out.

Copper(II) sulfate solution is clear and blue. Add a few drops of colourless sodium hydroxide solution and a pale blue solid appears, swirls, and slowly sinks to the bottom of the tube.

Commit first

What is the blue solid?

Sodium hydroxide solution tests for some metal ions (cations). Its hydroxide ions join the metal ions to make metal hydroxides that are insoluble, so they come out of solution as a precipitate. Some precipitates have a colour of their own; a white one needs a second observation before it names anything.

Report the colour of the precipitate, never the solution's: copper(II) sulfate solution is blue before you add a drop.

Required practical 7 · chemistry only · the sodium hydroxide part

A few drops first. Then excess.

  1. Put about 1 cm³ of the solution in a clean test tube.
  2. Add dilute sodium hydroxide solution a few drops at a time. Record the colour of any precipitate.
  3. Keep adding sodium hydroxide, shaking, until it is in excess. Record whether a white precipitate dissolves.

Risks. Sodium hydroxide is an irritant and especially harmful to eyes: wear eye protection. Copper compounds are harmful: avoid skin contact and wash your hands.

Key fact

Three coloured precipitates and three white ones. Of the white ones, only aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide.

Key note · AQA 4.8.3.2 · RP7

Metal hydroxides

  1. 01NaOH test for metal ions: Cu²⁺ = blue, Fe²⁺ = green, Fe³⁺ = brown, Al³⁺ = white (dissolves in excess), Ca²⁺ = white (stays), Mg²⁺ = white (stays).
  2. 02Only aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide solution.
  3. 03Add sodium hydroxide a few drops at a time first, then in excess.
  4. 04Calcium or magnesium? A flame test decides: calcium gives an orange-red flame.
  5. 05CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂ + Na₂SO₄: one OH for each positive charge on the metal ion.

    Two white precipitates and not sure which is which?

    The bench draws each precipitate in one idealised shade and does not model volumes or concentrations. Real colours vary: iron(II) hydroxide turns brown at the surface as air oxidises it, and calcium's precipitate can be faint.
