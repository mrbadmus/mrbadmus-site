Key knowledge

When a current flows through a conducting wire, a magnetic field is produced around the wire.

The strength of the magnetic field depends on the current through the wire and the distance from the wire. A bigger current gives a stronger field. The field is weaker further from the wire.

  • Put a plotting compass next to a wire in a circuit.
  • Switch on the current. The compass needle moves to line up with the field of the wire.
  • Switch off the current. The needle goes back to lining up with the Earth's field.
  • Reverse the current. The needle turns the other way.

Look at the arrows on the circles.

  • The field lines are circles around the wire, with the wire at the centre.
  • The circles get further apart further from the wire, where the field is weaker.
  • Reverse the current and the direction of the field reverses.

To find the direction, grip the wire in your right hand with your thumb pointing the way the current flows. Your fingers curl the way the field goes.

This is the right-hand grip rule.

A solenoid is a wire wound into a long coil.

Shaping a wire to form a solenoid increases the strength of the magnetic field created by a current through the wire.

The magnetic field inside a solenoid is strong and uniform. Uniform means the same strength and direction everywhere: the lines inside are straight, parallel and equally spaced.

The magnetic field around a solenoid has a similar shape to that of a bar magnet.

  • Each turn of the coil has its own magnetic field.
  • Inside the coil, the fields of all the turns point the same way, so they add together.
  • This makes the field inside the solenoid strong and uniform.

Curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current goes round the turns. Your thumb points to the north pole end.

Outside the solenoid, the field lines go from the north pole end to the south pole end, as they do for a bar magnet.

Reverse the current and the two poles swap ends.

Adding an iron core increases the strength of the magnetic field of a solenoid.

An electromagnet is a solenoid with an iron core.

The iron core is in the field of the solenoid, so it becomes an induced magnet. Its field adds to the field of the solenoid.

When the current is switched off, the iron loses most or all of its magnetism quickly, so the electromagnet switches off.

Iron is used for the core, not steel. Steel keeps more of its magnetism than iron when the current stops, so the electromagnet would not switch off properly.

  • Increase the current.
  • Add an iron core.
  • Wind more turns of wire into the same length of coil.

Find the electromagnet first. Then ask what it attracts when the current is on, and what happens when the current stops.

  • 1. Press the switch. A current flows through the coil of the electromagnet.
  • 2. The electromagnet attracts the iron armature. The hammer on the armature hits the gong.
  • 3. As the armature moves, it pulls away from the contact screw. The circuit is broken and the current stops.
  • 4. The electromagnet loses its magnetism, so the spring pulls the armature back.
  • 5. The armature touches the contact screw again. The current flows again and the cycle repeats while the switch is pressed.

  • Wrong: The field lines around a wire run along the wire.

    Right: They are circles around the wire.

  • Wrong: The field around a wire is the same strength everywhere.

    Right: It is strongest close to the wire and weaker further away.

  • Wrong: An electromagnet is a coil of wire.

    Right: An electromagnet is a solenoid with an iron core.

Key fact

A current in a wire produces a magnetic field around it, which is stronger with a bigger current and weaker further from the wire. Shaping the wire into a solenoid makes the field inside strong and uniform, and adding an iron core makes an electromagnet with a stronger field still.

An electromagnet can be switched on and off.

Its strength can be changed by changing the current.

A scrapyard crane uses an electromagnet to lift iron and steel, then drops it when the current is switched off.

A dot in a circle means the current comes out of the page, towards you. The field goes anticlockwise.

A cross in a circle means the current goes into the page, away from you. The field goes clockwise.

  • Draw: field lines with arrows on every line.
  • Describe how to demonstrate: say what you set up, what you do and what you would see.
  • Explain: say what happens and why.
