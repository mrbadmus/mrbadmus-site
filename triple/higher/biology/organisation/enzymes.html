Key knowledge

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up reactions in living organisms and are not used up.

Enzymes are large protein molecules. Each one has an active site with a particular shape.

Enzymes catalyse specific reactions in living organisms due to the shape of their active site.

The substrate fits into the active site like a key in a lock.

Only P has the right shape to fit this active site, so only P is broken down.

  • The substrate binds to the active site.
  • The enzyme breaks it down into products.
  • The products are released. The enzyme is unchanged, so it can be used again.

The lock and key theory is a simplified model of how enzymes work.

  • Up to the optimum, a higher temperature makes the molecules move faster, so the substrate and the active site collide more often. The rate increases.
  • Above the optimum, the shape of the active site changes, so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured and the rate falls quickly.

Many enzymes in the human body work best at about 37 °C.

Each enzyme works best at its optimum pH. At a pH far above or below it, the shape of the active site changes and the enzyme is denatured.

Different enzymes have different optimum pH values. The protease made in the stomach works best in acid conditions.

  • Put amylase solution with a pH buffer (a solution that keeps the pH steady) in one tube, and starch solution in another. Stand both in a water bath, for example at 35 °C, so they reach the same temperature.
  • Put a drop of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile.
  • Mix the amylase and the starch, and start a stopwatch.
  • Every 30 seconds, put a drop of the mixture into a new well of iodine.
  • When the iodine stays orange-brown, all the starch has been digested. Record the time.
  • Repeat with buffers at different pH values. Keep the temperature, and the volumes and concentrations of amylase and starch, the same.

To process the results, rate = 1 ÷ time, with the time in seconds. The unit is s⁻¹, which means per second.

The shortest time gives the fastest rate. Of the pH values tested, that one is closest to the optimum.

  • Wrong: A high temperature kills the enzyme.

    Right: Enzymes are not alive. A high temperature denatures them.

  • Wrong: The enzyme is used up in the reaction.

    Right: The enzyme is unchanged, so it can be used again.

  • Wrong: Every enzyme works best at pH 7.

    Right: Different enzymes have different optimum pH values.

Key fact

Enzymes are biological catalysts. Each one catalyses a specific reaction, because only a substrate with the right shape fits its active site. Above the optimum temperature, or far from the optimum pH, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured, so the rate falls.

Metabolism is the sum of all the reactions in a cell or the body. Enzymes control these reactions. Metabolism has the details.

In a real enzyme, the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. The lock and key model leaves this out.

  • Explain a fall in rate: name the change in the active site, then say the substrate no longer fits.
  • Describe the practical: buffer, water bath, iodine every 30 seconds, record the time.
  • Calculate the rate: time in seconds first, then 1 ÷ time.
