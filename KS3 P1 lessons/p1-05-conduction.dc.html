Conduction

Put your hand on the metal leg of your desk, then on the wooden top. The metal feels colder. Both have been in the same room all night. Which one is actually colder?

Draft — not yet science-reviewed.

Start here

Two things in the same room, and one feels colder.

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon in the same drawer, overnight, in the same kitchen. Pick up both. The metal one feels distinctly colder against your fingers and everyone agrees about it.

Commit before you read on.

    •

Neither. Put a thermometer on both and they read the same, to within a fraction of a degree — they have had all night to reach the room's temperature. What differs is how fast each one takes energy out of your hand. Your fingers are not thermometers; they are rate detectors, and the metal is winning a race, not reporting a temperature.

Conduction is energy moving through a material by particles knocking into their neighbours — no substance travels, only the movement is passed along. In metals there is a second, much faster route as well, and that difference is why this lesson has a misconception attached to it that survives into adulthood.

Every one of them is at 20 °C. All four thermometer readings are identical and your hand ranked them confidently from freezing to fine. Your skin has no way to measure temperature — it detects how fast energy is leaving it, and a good conductor drains it faster. That is the entire illusion.

The same works upwards. Metal at 45 °C feels scalding and wood at 45 °C feels pleasant, because the metal delivers energy into you as fast as it took it away. A sauna at 90 °C is survivable; a metal bench in that sauna at 90 °C is not.

Think again

“Metal is a colder material than wood.”

Commit to the correction.

    •

A material does not have a temperature of its own. Leave anything in a room long enough and it ends up at the room's temperature — that is thermal equilibrium, from last lesson, and it applies to every object in the room without exception.

What metal has is a second route for conduction that non-metals do not. Alongside the particle-to-particle wobble, metals contain electrons that are free to move through the whole structure, and they carry energy across the material far faster than vibration alone. That is why metals conduct roughly a thousand times better than wood, why they are used for saucepans and heat sinks, and why they feel cold — one property, doing every job on that list.

“In conduction the hot particles travel along the bar to the cold end.”

They do not go anywhere. Each particle vibrates about a fixed position and hands energy on to the next by colliding with it — the energy travels, the matter does not. That is exactly what makes conduction different from convection, where the heated material really does move and carry its energy with it. A steel bar does not get shorter at the hot end.

Key fact

Conduction is energy passed on by particles that stay where they are: they vibrate harder and jostle their neighbours, and in metals free electrons carry it much further and faster. It needs particles in contact, so solids conduct well and gases hardly at all.

Key note

Conduction passes vibration from particle to particle; nothing moves along. Metals also have free electrons, which is why they conduct far better — and why they feel cold without being cold.

Going further

There is a party trick built entirely on this lesson. A wooden block and a metal block, both at room temperature, each with an ice cube on top: the ice on the metal melts far faster, which looks like the metal is warmer even though a thermometer says otherwise. The metal is simply better at delivering the room's energy to the ice. Firewalkers use the same physics — glowing embers are at 500 °C but wood ash is a dreadful conductor, so very little energy reaches a foot in the fraction of a second it is in contact. Nobody firewalks on a metal plate at 500 °C.

Ask about this lesson

The bench is a teaching model. The rods are compared by the time a blob of wax takes to melt at a fixed distance from the flame, with copper as the fastest of the four; the figures are typical of a school demonstration rather than measurements of a particular apparatus. Real conduction rates depend on the thickness and length of the rod, how well the flame is applied and how much energy is lost to the air along the way. The free-electron layer is a visual aid: electrons in a metal drift, they do not travel in the neat lines drawn here.
