Making a pure dry salt

Start here

You are making pure copper sulfate crystals from sulfuric acid and black copper oxide powder. Copper oxide does not dissolve in water.

If you had to guess, how much black powder should you add to the acid: only just enough, or slightly too much?

You cannot see when the acid runs out. Any acid left over would end up in your crystals.

Right. The extra powder does not dissolve, so you filter it off. No acid is left.

Key words

  • The compound made when a metal takes the place of the hydrogen in an acid.

  • More than is needed, so some is left over at the end.

  • The liquid that passes through the filter paper.

  • To grow crystals by letting a concentrated solution cool slowly.

Key knowledge

A salt's name has two parts.

  • The first word is the metal, such as copper or sodium.
  • The second word comes from the acid.

So copper oxide and sulfuric acid make copper sulfate.

The metal can come from the metal itself, or from its oxide, hydroxide or carbonate.

The metal's name still starts the salt's name.

  • Magnesium oxide and nitric acid make magnesium nitrate.
  • Sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid make sodium chloride.
  • Calcium carbonate and sulfuric acid make calcium sulfate.

  • Sodium chloride flavours food and grits icy roads.
  • Potassium nitrate is a fertiliser. It helps crops grow.
  • Copper sulfate kills fungi that attack crops.
  • Calcium sulfate is used as plaster for walls.

Copper oxide is a base. A base neutralises an acid.

  • Warm some dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker.
  • Add copper oxide a spatula at a time, stirring.
  • Stop when black powder is left over. This is excess.
  • Filter to remove the extra copper oxide.
  • The blue filtrate is copper sulfate solution.
  • Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until about half the water has gone.
  • Leave it to cool slowly. Crystals form.
  • Pat the crystals dry with filter paper.

You cannot see when the acid has all been used up.

So you add copper oxide until some is left over.

Then you know every drop of acid has reacted.

The extra copper oxide does not dissolve, so filtering removes it.

Leftover acid could not be filtered out. It would stay in your salt.

Heating boils off some of the water, so the solution becomes concentrated.

As it cools slowly, the salt comes out as large crystals.

Boiling it until it is dry gives a powder of tiny crystals instead.

Sodium hydroxide is an alkali. It dissolves in water.

So extra sodium hydroxide cannot be filtered off.

You must add just enough to neutralise the acid.

But you cannot see when enough alkali has gone in.

  • Use an indicator to find the volume of alkali that neutralises the acid.
  • Mix the same volumes again, with no indicator.
  • Evaporate some water and leave it to cool slowly, as before.

  • Wrong: Boiling the solution dry gives the best crystals.

    Right: Boiling dry gives a powder. Cooling slowly grows large crystals.

  • Wrong: Sulfuric acid makes chlorides.

    Right: Sulfuric acid makes sulfates. Hydrochloric acid makes chlorides.

  • Wrong: The acid's name comes first in a salt's name.

    Right: The metal comes first, as in copper sulfate.

  • Wrong: Copper oxide dissolves in water.

    Right: Copper oxide does not dissolve. That is why the extra can be filtered off.

Key fact

A salt is named from its metal and its acid. Hydrochloric acid makes chlorides, sulfuric acid makes sulfates and nitric acid makes nitrates. For a pure, dry salt, add an insoluble base, such as copper oxide, in excess. Filter it, then evaporate some water and let the solution cool slowly.

If small crystals form on the rod, stop heating.

The solution is now concentrated enough to crystallise as it cools.

Calcium carbonate and sulfuric acid make calcium sulfate.

Calcium sulfate dissolves only a little. It coats the chips and stops the reaction.

So this method does not work for calcium sulfate.
