Conductors and insulators

Start here

A lamp cable has a copper wire inside a plastic coating.

If you had to guess, how much harder is it to push electricity through the plastic than through the copper?

That is about right for tap water. Plastic resists over a million million times more than copper.

Yes. Copper lets charge through easily. Plastic hardly lets any through at all.

Key words

  • A material that lets a current flow through it easily. It has a low resistance.

  • A material that hardly lets any current through. It has a very high resistance.

  • Electrons in a metal that are not held by one atom, so they can move.

Key knowledge

A conductor lets a current flow through it easily.

An insulator hardly lets any current through.

Conductors have a low resistance. Insulators have a very high resistance.

A cable uses both.

The copper wire carries the current.

The plastic coating keeps the current in the copper, and keeps you safe.

  • Make a loop with a battery, a lamp and two crocodile clips.
  • Clip the material into the gap between the clips.
  • If the lamp lights, the material is a conductor.
  • If the lamp stays off, it is an insulator or a poor conductor.
  • An ammeter in the loop shows currents too small to light the lamp.

A current is a flow of charge.

In a metal, some electrons are not held by any one atom.

These free electrons can move through the whole metal.

They carry the charge, so metals are good conductors.

In plastic, almost every electron is held in place by its atoms.

There are almost no free charges to carry the current, so plastic is an insulator.

Salt water conducts too. It has charged particles called ions that can move.

Plastic resists more than a million million times as much as copper.

Materials fill the whole range in between.

Tap water and salt water conduct, but poorly.

So there is no sharp line between conductors and insulators.

In this lesson, below 100 Ω counts as a conductor.

Above 100 000 Ω counts as an insulator.

Even plastic lets a tiny current through. It is far too small to matter.

These are typical values. Real samples vary.

resistance (Ω) = potential difference (V) ÷ current (A)

R = V ÷ I

Put a known p.d. across the material. Measure the current. Then divide.

There is 6.0 V across 10 cm of pencil lead. The ammeter reads 0.20 A.

Find the resistance of the pencil lead.

  • Convert: nothing. The p.d. is in V and the current is in A.
  • Formula: R = V ÷ I
  • Insert: R = 6.0 V ÷ 0.20 A
  • Fine-tune: 6.0 ÷ 0.20 = 30
  • Answer: R = 30 Ω

30 Ω is a low resistance, so pencil lead is a conductor.

Tap water conducts, because it has dissolved salts in it.

Wet skin lets a much bigger current pass through the body.

So never touch switches, plugs or sockets with wet hands.

  • Wrong: An insulator lets absolutely no current through.

    Right: A tiny current flows, far too small for a school meter to show.

  • Wrong: Every material is either a conductor or an insulator.

    Right: There is no sharp line. Tap water conducts, but poorly.

  • Wrong: Plastic insulates because it has no charged particles at all.

    Right: Plastic is full of electrons. Almost all are held in place, so cannot move.

  • Wrong: A very short piece of plastic would conduct well.

    Right: A shorter piece resists less, but its resistance is still huge.

  • Wrong: Only metals conduct.

    Right: Graphite in pencil lead conducts too. So does salt water.

Key fact

Conductors let current flow easily, because they have charged particles free to move. Metals conduct because they have free electrons. Insulators, like plastic, have almost no free charges, so their resistance is huge.

Silicon is neither a good conductor nor a good insulator.

Its resistance can be changed on purpose.

That makes it useful for the chips inside computers and phones.

Some metals lose all their resistance when they are cooled enough.

Mercury does this at about minus 269 °C.

These materials are called superconductors.

Hospital MRI scanners use them to make very strong magnets.

At a very high p.d., a current can be forced through an insulator.

Lightning is a current through air, which is normally an insulator.

That is why power lines hang from long glass or ceramic insulators.
