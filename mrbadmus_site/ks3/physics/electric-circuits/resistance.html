Electric circuits · Quantitative

Resistance

Start here

A bulb is lit by two cells. A metre of thin nichrome wire is added into the loop. The bulb gets dimmer.

If you had to guess, is less electricity flowing only through the bulb, or all the way round?

In one loop, the current is the same at every point. So less flows all the way round.

Yes. The nichrome wire makes the whole loop harder to get through. Less current flows everywhere.

Key words

  • How hard it is for a current to flow through a component.

  • The unit of resistance, written Ω. One ohm means 1 V is needed for each 1 A.

  • A lamp with a thin metal wire inside that glows when it gets very hot.

Key knowledge

Resistance is how hard it is for a current to flow through a component.

It is measured in ohms. The symbol for ohms is Ω.

For the same p.d., a bigger resistance gives a smaller current.

In a metal wire, free electrons flow between the metal atoms.

The electrons keep bumping into the atoms.

Each bump makes it harder for them to flow, and heats the wire.

Adding a thin wire to a loop adds resistance.

The current gets smaller all the way round the loop.

That is why the bulb gets dimmer.

The current is still the same at every point in the loop.

No current is used up by the wire.

  • A longer wire has more resistance. The electrons bump into more atoms.
  • A thinner wire has more resistance. There is less room for the electrons to flow.
  • The material matters. Nichrome has more resistance than copper.
  • A hotter metal wire has more resistance. Its atoms vibrate more, so there are more bumps.

resistance (Ω) = potential difference (V) ÷ current (A)

R = V ÷ I

Measure the p.d. across the component with a voltmeter.

Measure the current through it with an ammeter.

Then divide the p.d. by the current.

The voltmeter across the resistor reads 6.0 V. The ammeter reads 0.40 A.

Find the resistance of the resistor.

  • Convert: nothing. The p.d. is in V and the current is in A.
  • Formula: R = V ÷ I
  • Insert: R = 6.0 V ÷ 0.40 A
  • Fine-tune: 6.0 ÷ 0.40 = 15
  • Answer: R = 15 Ω

The voltmeter across the lamp reads 3.0 V. The ammeter reads 250 mA.

Find the resistance of the lamp.

  • Convert: 250 mA ÷ 1000 = 0.25 A
  • Formula: R = V ÷ I
  • Insert: R = 3.0 V ÷ 0.25 A
  • Fine-tune: 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12
  • Answer: R = 12 Ω

Put in 250 instead of 0.25, and the answer is a thousand times too small.

A resistor keeps the same resistance at every p.d., if it stays cool.

That is why its value can be printed on it.

A filament lamp gets hotter as you turn the p.d. up.

Hot metal has more resistance, so the lamp's resistance goes up.

A small lamp might be about 6 Ω at 1.5 V.

At 12 V, the same lamp might be about 18 Ω.

Both values are right. The lamp really does resist more when it is hot.

  • Wrong: Resistance is a force that pushes back on the current.

    Right: Resistance is how hard it is for current to flow. It is not a force.

  • Wrong: A component has one resistance, whatever supply you test it on.

    Right: A resistor does. A filament lamp's resistance goes up as it gets hotter.

  • Wrong: Find resistance by multiplying the voltmeter and ammeter readings.

    Right: Divide the p.d. by the current. R = V ÷ I.

  • Wrong: A lamp gives two different resistances, so one meter must be faulty.

    Right: Both readings can be right. The lamp is hotter at the bigger p.d.

  • Wrong: A thicker wire has more resistance.

    Right: A thicker wire has less resistance. A thinner wire has more.

  • Wrong: Put 250 mA straight into R = V ÷ I.

    Right: Change milliamps to amps first. 250 mA is 0.25 A.

Key fact

Resistance is how hard it is for current to flow, measured in ohms (Ω). Resistance = potential difference ÷ current, so R = V ÷ I. A longer, thinner or hotter metal wire has a higher resistance.

A variable resistor lets you change the resistance in a circuit.

Turn the resistance up and the current goes down.

A lamp in the same loop then gets dimmer.

Some dimmer switches and volume controls work like this.

A wire with a high resistance gets hot when a current flows.

Toasters and heaters use this on purpose.

Cables are made thick and from copper, so they have a low resistance.

Then they stay cool.

A thermistor's resistance falls as it gets warmer.

An oven can use one to sense its own temperature.

A light-dependent resistor, or LDR, has a lower resistance in brighter light.

A street light can use one to switch on when it gets dark.

Georg Ohm was a German scientist.

In 1827 he published how current depends on the p.d. across a wire.

The unit of resistance, the ohm, is named after him.
