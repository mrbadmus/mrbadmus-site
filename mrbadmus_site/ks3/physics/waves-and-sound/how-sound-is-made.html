How sound is made

Start here

Put two fingers on the front of your throat and hum. Then stop humming and just breathe out.

If you had to guess, what makes the humming sound: air rushing past, or something shaking?

Breathing out moves even more air, and it makes no note. Moving air is not enough.

Yes. Two folds in your throat vibrate. You can feel it under your fingers.

Key words

  • Moving quickly to and fro about one place.

  • A device with a cone that vibrates to turn an electrical signal into sound.

  • The thin sheet in a microphone that vibrates when sound reaches it.

  • The thin sheet in your ear that vibrates when sound reaches it.

Key knowledge

Every sound is made by something vibrating.

Vibrating means moving quickly to and fro about one place.

A guitar string vibrates when you pluck it.

A loudspeaker cone vibrates when an electrical signal drives it.

Your vocal folds vibrate when you speak or hum.

When the vibration stops, the sound stops.

The vibrating object pushes and pulls on the air next to it.

Each layer of air pushes on the next, so the vibration travels.

The air does not travel to you. Only the vibration does.

When sound reaches a thin sheet, it makes the sheet vibrate too.

In your ear, the thin sheet is the eardrum.

Your ear turns these vibrations into nerve signals, which go to your brain.

In a microphone, the thin sheet is the diaphragm.

The microphone turns its vibrations into an electrical signal.

  • Wrong: Sound is made by the air.

    Right: Sound is made by a vibrating object. The air carries it.

  • Wrong: An object you cannot see moving is not vibrating.

    Right: Many vibrations are too small or too fast to see. You can often feel them.

  • Wrong: A microphone is a quiet loudspeaker.

    Right: A microphone turns sound into an electrical signal. A loudspeaker does the reverse.

  • Wrong: Sound is stored inside an object until it gets out.

    Right: Sound only exists while something vibrates. Stop the vibration and the sound stops.

Key fact

Sound is produced by vibrating objects. The vibration travels through the air and makes your eardrum, or a microphone's diaphragm, vibrate.

A tuning fork held in the air sounds quiet.

Its thin prongs push very little air.

Stand its base on a table and it sounds much louder.

Now the whole table top vibrates and pushes on the air.

A loudspeaker can work as a microphone.

Talk into it and it makes a small electrical signal.

Some intercoms use one speaker for both jobs.
