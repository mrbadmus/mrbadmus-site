Waves and sound · Investigation

Sound needs a medium

Start here

A small buzzer hangs on a thread inside a thick glass jar, ringing away. A pump starts pulling the air out of the jar.

If you had to guess, once the air is out, does the little hammer keep beating against the bell?

Yes. The buzzer runs on electricity, so it keeps beating. But the sound fades to nothing.

It keeps beating, because the buzzer runs on electricity. What stops is the sound.

Key words

  • The material a wave travels through. Sound needs a solid, a liquid or a gas.

  • A space with no particles in it at all. Sound cannot travel through it.

  • How fast sound travels through a material. It is different in each material.

Key knowledge

Sound is a vibration passed from particle to particle.

Each particle pushes the next one along, so the vibration travels.

The material sound travels through is called the medium.

Sound can travel through solids, liquids and gases.

A vacuum has no particles, so sound cannot travel through it.

A ringing buzzer hangs inside a glass jar.

A vacuum pump slowly takes the air out of the jar.

The sound gets quieter and quieter, until you hear nothing.

You can still see the hammer beating against the bell.

Light can travel through a vacuum, so you still see it.

Let the air back in, and you hear the sound again.

So the buzzer never stopped. Sound needs particles to travel.

Sound in different materials

RowThe particlesSpeed of sound
A vacuum
Air, a gas
Water, a liquid
Steel, a solid

Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases.

In a solid, the particles are close together and strongly held.

Each one passes the vibration to the next very quickly.

In a gas, the particles are far apart.

The vibration takes longer to pass from one particle to the next.

Sound travels about 15 times faster in steel than in air.

distance (m) = speed (m/s) × time (s)

speed = distance ÷ time

time = distance ÷ speed

Use the speed of sound for the material the sound travels through.

A hammer hits one end of a steel rail 1000 m long.

Find the time the sound takes to travel along the steel.

  • Convert: nothing. The distance is in m and the speed is in m/s.
  • Formula: time = distance ÷ speed
  • Insert: time = 1000 m ÷ 5000 m/s
  • Fine-tune: 1000 ÷ 5000 = 0.2
  • Answer: time = 0.2 s

Through the air, the same sound takes 1000 ÷ 340 = 2.9 s.

So the listener hears the blow through the rail first.

A tap travels 2.4 km along a steel pipe in 0.48 s. Find the speed of sound in the pipe.

  • Convert: 2.4 km × 1000 = 2400 m
  • Formula: speed = distance ÷ time
  • Insert: speed = 2400 m ÷ 0.48 s
  • Fine-tune: 2400 ÷ 0.48 = 5000
  • Answer: speed = 5000 m/s

Put in 2.4 instead of 2400, and the answer is a thousand times too small.

  • Wrong: Sound can cross a vacuum, just very faintly.

    Right: Sound cannot cross a vacuum at all. There are no particles to carry it.

  • Wrong: Sound is fastest in air, because air is easy to move through.

    Right: Sound is slowest in gases like air. It is fastest in solids.

  • Wrong: The buzzer cannot vibrate in a vacuum, because there is no air to push against.

    Right: It vibrates just the same. There are no particles to carry the vibration away.

  • Wrong: Sound can only travel through air.

    Right: Sound travels through any solid, liquid or gas.

  • Wrong: Put 2.4 km straight into the equation.

    Right: Change kilometres to metres first. 2.4 km is 2400 m.

Key fact

Sound is a vibration passed from particle to particle, so it needs a medium. It cannot travel through a vacuum. It is fastest in solids and slowest in gases. Speeds: about 340 m/s in air, 1500 m/s in water, 5000 m/s in steel.

Rung 4

Put your ear to a long metal fence while someone taps the far end.

You hear the tap through the metal first.

A moment later, you hear it again through the air.

Sound travels a little faster in warm air than in cold air.

The speed goes up by about 0.6 m/s for each degree Celsius.

So 340 m/s is a typical value for air, not an exact one.

Space is a vacuum, so astronauts cannot talk to each other through it.

They use radios instead.

Radio waves, like light, can travel through a vacuum.
