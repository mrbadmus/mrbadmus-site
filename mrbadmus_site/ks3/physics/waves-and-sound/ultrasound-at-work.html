Waves and sound · System

Ultrasound at work

Start here

A steel bridge girder looks perfect. A tiny crack may be hidden inside it.

If you had to guess, would a short pulse of sound sent into the steel bounce back off a hidden crack?

Right. Sound reflects where one material meets another. A crack is steel meeting air.

Much of it bounces back. Sound reflects wherever steel meets the air in a crack.

Key words

  • Sound above about 20 000 Hz. It is too high for humans to hear.

  • Sound below about 20 Hz. It is too low for humans to hear.

  • A very short burst of sound, sent so that its echo can be timed.

  • The place where one material meets another. Some sound reflects there.

Ultrasound and infrasound

What people use it for

Ultrasound is made by vibrations, like any other sound.

It needs a medium, and it cannot cross a vacuum.

It travels at the same speed as other sound in that material.

It reflects at a boundary, where one material meets another.

Sound is a pressure wave, and it transfers energy.

In an ultrasonic cleaning bath, the waves shake dirt off jewellery and tools.

Physiotherapists use ultrasound to warm and treat injured muscles under the skin.

A probe sends a short pulse of ultrasound into a material.

Part of the pulse reflects at each boundary inside.

The probe detects the echo, and a timer measures how long it took.

The pulse goes down and back, so depth = (speed × time) ÷ 2.

A probe sends a pulse into a steel block. The echo returns after 0.040 ms.

Sound travels at 5000 m/s in steel. Find the depth of the crack in mm.

  • Convert: 0.040 ms ÷ 1000 = 0.00004 s
  • Formula: distance travelled = speed × time
  • Insert: distance travelled = 5000 m/s × 0.00004 s
  • Fine-tune: 5000 × 0.00004 = 0.2 m down and back. 0.2 ÷ 2 = 0.1 m
  • Answer: depth = 0.1 m = 100 mm

1 ms is a thousandth of a second, so divide by 1000.

1 m is 1000 mm, so multiply by 1000 at the end.

Ordinary sound travels well through solids and through the body.

Ultrasound has a much shorter wavelength than sound we hear.

Short wavelengths reflect off small things, so the scan shows fine detail.

Unlike X-rays, ultrasound is not known to harm an unborn baby.

Before a scan, gel is spread on the skin.

Without it, a thin layer of air sits between the probe and the skin.

Almost all of the pulse would reflect off that air.

The gel removes the air, so the pulse can get into the body.

A microphone turns sound into an electrical signal.

Sound waves make a thin diaphragm inside it vibrate.

The diaphragm's movement makes an electrical signal with the same pattern.

The signal can then be recorded, sent or made louder.

  • Wrong: Ultrasound is a special wave that gets through solids where sound cannot.

    Right: Ordinary sound travels well through solids. Ultrasound is sound of higher frequency.

  • Wrong: A scan works by sending ultrasound through you to the other side.

    Right: The probe detects echoes that reflect back from boundaries inside the body.

  • Wrong: Ultrasound is used because it travels faster than other sound.

    Right: All sound travels at the same speed in one material. Ultrasound gives more detail.

  • Wrong: The gel is only there to help the probe slide about.

    Right: Its main job is removing the air, so the pulse gets into the skin.

  • Wrong: Depth = speed × time.

    Right: Speed × time is down and back. Halve it to get the depth.

Key fact

Ultrasound is sound above 20 000 Hz, and infrasound is sound below 20 Hz. Ultrasound is used for the energy it carries, in cleaning and physiotherapy. It is also used for its echoes, in scans, sonar and finding cracks.

Before a scan the operator squeezes a cold gel onto the skin and presses the probe into it. Explain why the scan would not work with a thin layer of air between the probe and the skin.

Higher frequencies show finer detail, but they are absorbed faster.

So a scan deep inside the body uses a lower frequency.

A scan just under the skin can use a higher one.

Erupting volcanoes and big explosions make infrasound.

Infrasound travels a very long way through the air.

Detectors around the world pick it up, thousands of kilometres away.

A bat hunting in the open uses calls that reach far.

As it closes in on a moth, it calls much faster.

That tells it where the moth is, moment by moment.
