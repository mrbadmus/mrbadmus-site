Nutrition and digestion · Contrast

When diet goes wrong

Start here

A patient takes in 13 000 kJ a day. That is more energy than most adults need.

If you had to guess, could this patient be malnourished?

Yes. Malnourished means the diet is out of balance. Too much energy counts.

They could be. Too much energy is malnutrition. A big diet can also lack iron.

Key words

  • A diet that is not balanced: too much energy, too little, or a missing nutrient.

  • An illness caused by too little of one nutrient in the diet.

  • Having so much stored body fat that it harms health.

  • Going without enough food for a long time, so the body breaks itself down.

Key knowledge

Malnutrition means a diet that is not balanced.

It can mean too much energy, too little energy, or a missing nutrient.

Someone who eats plenty can still be malnourished.

Three kinds of imbalance

Energy shortfall
Missing nutrient

Obesity comes from taking in more energy than you need, over a long time.

The extra energy is stored as fat under the skin and around organs.

Obesity raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and high blood pressure.

It also puts strain on the joints, such as the knees.

A raised risk means these are more likely. They are not certain.

In starvation, the body takes in far less energy than it needs.

It breaks down its fat stores first. Then it breaks down muscle.

  • The person loses mass and feels weak and cold.
  • They catch infections more easily.
  • In children, growth slows or stops.

A deficiency disease is cured by the nutrient that is missing.

More of the same food does not help. It still lacks that nutrient.

  • Vitamin C: oranges, other citrus fruit, peppers.
  • Vitamin D: oily fish, eggs, and sunlight on the skin.
  • Calcium: milk, cheese and yoghurt.
  • Iron: red meat, beans and leafy green vegetables.
  • Protein: meat, fish, eggs, beans and lentils.

  • Not enough food, because of poverty, war or crops failing.
  • Eating only a few kinds of food, so a nutrient is missing.
  • Taking in more energy than you need, with little exercise.
  • Some illnesses stop the gut absorbing food properly.
  • Some illnesses and medicines change how hungry you feel.

  • Wrong: Malnourished only means not having enough to eat.

    Right: It means an unbalanced diet. Too much food can cause it too.

  • Wrong: Deficiency diseases only happened in the past.

    Right: Scurvy, rickets and anaemia still happen in the UK today.

  • Wrong: You can tell what someone eats by looking at them.

    Right: Body size also depends on genes, illness and activity. Blood tests can show deficiencies.

  • Wrong: More of a food that lacks the nutrient cures a deficiency disease.

    Right: Only the missing nutrient cures it, such as vitamin C for scurvy.

  • Wrong: Only eating too much fat causes obesity.

    Right: Extra energy from fat, carbohydrate or protein can be stored as fat.

Key fact

Malnutrition means an unbalanced diet: too much energy, too little, or a missing nutrient. A deficiency disease, such as scurvy or rickets, is caused by one missing nutrient. It can happen at any energy intake.

In 1747, James Lind treated twelve sailors who had scurvy.

He split them into pairs and gave each pair a different treatment.

The pair given oranges and lemons got better fastest.

It was one of the first controlled trials in medicine.

Your skin makes vitamin D in sunlight.

In the UK, from October to March, sunlight is too weak to make enough.

So the NHS advises a vitamin D supplement in autumn and winter.
