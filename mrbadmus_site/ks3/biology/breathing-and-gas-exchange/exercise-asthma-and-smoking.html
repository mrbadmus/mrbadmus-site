Key words

  • A condition in which the bronchioles narrow. The muscle contracts, the lining swells and extra mucus is made.

  • bronchioles

  • Tiny moving hairs on the airway lining that sweep mucus up and out of the lungs.

  • A gas that binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does and does not readily let go.

Key knowledge

When you exercise, your muscles respire faster.

They need more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide.

Your brain detects the rising carbon dioxide in your blood.

Your breathing gets faster and deeper. Your heart rate rises too.

This delivers more oxygen and removes more carbon dioxide.

These changes reverse after you stop.

In an asthma attack, the bronchioles get narrower.

The muscle in their walls contracts.

The lining swells and makes more mucus.

Less air can get through to the alveoli.

A reliever inhaler relaxes the muscle, so the airways open again.

Key fact

Exercise raises breathing rate, and this reverses after you stop. In an asthma attack the airways narrow, and treatment can open them again. Smoking destroys the walls of the alveoli, which permanently reduces gas exchange.

Your blood still holds extra carbon dioxide when you stop.

After hard exercise, your body also needs oxygen to break down lactic acid.

You keep breathing hard until this is done.

The air already holds plenty of oxygen.

The problem is the narrow airways, so less air can get through.

A reliever inhaler treats that by opening the airways.

In emphysema, the walls between alveoli break down.

The lungs still hold about as much air.

But the surface area is much smaller, so gas exchange is poor.
