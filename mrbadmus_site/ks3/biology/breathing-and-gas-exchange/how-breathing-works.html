Why air moves

Your lungs cannot move themselves.

The diaphragm and intercostal muscles do the work.

The lungs stretch when the chest gets bigger.

Air particles hit the walls of the chest. This causes pressure.

In a bigger space, the particles hit the walls less often. So the pressure falls.

In a smaller space, they hit the walls more often. So the pressure rises.

A bell jar is a model of the chest.

  • The jar is the chest.
  • The balloons are the lungs.
  • The rubber sheet is the diaphragm.

Pull the sheet down. The volume in the jar increases and the pressure falls.

Air moves in and the balloons inflate.

Push the sheet up. The pressure rises, air moves out and the balloons deflate.

Fill a large bottle with water. Turn it upside down in a tub of water.

Breathe out fully through a tube into the bottle.

Your breath pushes water out of the bottle.

The volume of water pushed out equals the volume of air you breathed out.

  • Wrong: The lungs pull air in.

    Right: Muscles make the chest bigger, then air moves in.

  • Wrong: Air coming in makes the chest bigger.

    Right: The chest gets bigger first. Then air moves in.

  • Wrong: Air gets sucked in.

    Right: Air moves in because the pressure outside is higher.

Key fact

Breathing works by changing pressure. Muscles change the volume of the chest, which changes the pressure inside it. Air always moves from high pressure to low pressure.

Want to check you have the order of events the right way round?

Pressure values on the model are illustrative, in kilopascals relative to an atmosphere of about 101 kPa. Real intrapulmonary pressure swings during quiet breathing are under 1 kPa.
