Evolution, extinction and biodiversity · Process

Natural selection

Start here

A giraffe's neck is about two metres long. Its ancestors had much shorter necks.

If you had to guess, how did giraffes end up with such long necks?

Yes. Giraffes with longer necks reached more food, so more survived to breed.

Long-necked giraffes had more babies. Stretching does not change the genes a giraffe passes on.

Key words

  • The change in a species over many generations.

  • The best adapted individuals survive and breed more. They pass on their genes.

  • Passed from parents to their young in their genes.

  • People choose parents with a feature they want, and breed them.

Key knowledge

Evolution is the change in a species over many generations.

Charles Darwin came up with the theory of evolution by natural selection.

He published it in 1859, in his book On the Origin of Species.

Alfred Russel Wallace came up with the same idea at the same time.

Darwin sailed round the world on a ship called HMS Beagle.

On the Galápagos Islands, he saw mockingbirds that differed from island to island.

Later, scientists found the finches' beaks suited the food on each island.

He also bred pigeons, and saw how much breeding could change them.

  • Variation: individuals in a species are different from each other.
  • Competition: more are born than can survive. They compete for food, space and mates.
  • The best adapted survive: individuals with useful features are more likely to survive.
  • They reproduce: the survivors breed and pass on their genes.
  • Over many generations: the useful feature becomes common. The species has changed.

Peppered moths rest on tree bark in the day. Birds hunt them by sight.

Most were pale. A few dark ones were born too.

In the 1800s, soot from factories blackened the bark in industrial areas.

Birds saw the pale moths more easily, so they ate more of them.

More dark moths survived and bred. Their young inherited dark wings.

Over many generations, most moths in those areas became dark.

When the air got cleaner, pale moths became common again.

Changes during an animal's life do not change its genes.

So they are not inherited.

A giraffe that stretches its neck does not have longer-necked young.

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck suggested this stretching idea in 1809. Evidence showed it was wrong.

Long necks spread because giraffes born with longer necks survived and bred more.

In selective breeding, people choose which animals or plants breed.

They choose parents with a feature they want. Examples:

  • cows that give a lot of milk (a high milk yield)
  • chickens that lay many eggs
  • wheat with large grains, and crops that resist disease
  • dogs with a gentle nature
  • large, tasty fruit and vegetables

  • Choose parents that show the wanted feature most.
  • Breed them together.
  • From their young, choose those that show the feature most.
  • Breed these together.
  • Repeat for many generations.

Over time, more and more of the young show the feature.

It gives us more milk, more food and crops that resist disease.

But the animals in a breed are closely related, so they have little variation.

A new disease could kill many of them, as none may resist it.

Some breeds have health problems. Some flat-faced dogs struggle to breathe.

Examples
Effect on variation

  • Wrong: Animals change themselves to suit their home, and pass the change on.

    Right: Changes during an animal's life are not inherited. Only genes are passed on.

  • Wrong: The population needed to change, so it did.

    Right: Nothing plans the change. The useful variation was already there.

  • Wrong: The soot turned the moths dark.

    Right: Each dark moth was born dark. Soot changed which moths birds could see.

  • Wrong: One animal can evolve during its life.

    Right: Species evolve over many generations. One animal keeps the genes it was born with.

  • Wrong: Selective breeding happens in the wild.

    Right: People do selective breeding. Natural selection happens in the wild.

Key fact

Individuals vary, and the best adapted survive and reproduce. They pass on their genes, so over many generations the species changes. This is natural selection, explained by Charles Darwin.

A few bacteria may happen to resist an antibiotic.

The antibiotic kills the rest. The resistant ones survive and divide.

Bacteria can divide every 20 minutes, so resistance spreads in days.

Insects become resistant to pesticides in the same way.

In the 1950s, Bernard Kettlewell released pale and dark moths in two woods.

In each wood, more of the better hidden moths were caught again later.

Other scientists said his method was unrealistic.

Michael Majerus repeated it with a better method. He got the same result.
