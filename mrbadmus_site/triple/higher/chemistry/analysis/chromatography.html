Key knowledge

Chromatography can be used to separate mixtures and can give information to help identify substances.

Paper chromatography separates the coloured substances in inks and food colourings.

Chromatography involves a stationary phase and a mobile phase.

  • The stationary phase does not move. In paper chromatography it is the paper.
  • The mobile phase moves. In paper chromatography it is the solvent, which soaks up the paper.

Separation depends on the distribution of substances between the phases.

  • The solvent moves up the paper and dissolves the substances in the spot.
  • Each substance is distributed differently between the paper and the solvent.
  • A substance that spends more time dissolved in the solvent moves further up the paper.
  • A substance that is more attracted to the paper moves a shorter distance.
  • So the substances move different distances and separate into different spots.

The paper after the solvent has moved is called a chromatogram.

  • The start line (origin) is where the spots were put.
  • The solvent front is how far the solvent moved.
  • Each spot in one lane is a different substance.

The ratio of the distance moved by a compound (centre of spot from origin) to the distance moved by the solvent is its Rf value.

  • Measure both distances from the start line, in the same unit.
  • Measure to the centre of the spot.
  • An Rf value has no unit. It is always less than 1, because a substance cannot move further than the solvent.
  • Give the answer to a sensible number of significant figures: usually two, like the distances you measured.

Different compounds have different Rf values in different solvents, which can be used to help identify the compounds.

Run the unknown next to known substances on the same paper, in the same solvent. A spot with the same Rf value and colour as a known substance is probably that substance.

The compounds in a mixture may separate into different spots depending on the solvent, but a pure compound will produce a single spot in all solvents.

One spot in one solvent does not prove a substance is pure. Run it in two or three different solvents. A single spot in every solvent suggests it is pure.

Investigate how paper chromatography can be used to separate and tell the difference between coloured substances. Calculate Rf values.

  • 1. Draw a start line in pencil about 2 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper.
  • 2. Put a small spot of each ink or food colouring on the start line.
  • 3. Stand the paper in a beaker with a little solvent, such as water. The solvent must be below the start line.
  • 4. Put a lid on the beaker. Leave it until the solvent has moved most of the way up the paper.
  • 5. Take the paper out and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away. Let it dry.
  • 6. Measure the distance from the start line to the solvent front, and to the centre of each spot. Calculate each Rf value.

Key fact

Chromatography separates a mixture because its substances are distributed differently between the stationary phase and the mobile phase, so they move different distances. Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent, and a pure compound produces a single spot in all solvents.

Pure substances shows another test for purity: melting and boiling points.

Formulations are mixtures, and chromatography can separate their coloured components.

  • Calculate: write Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent, then the numbers, then the answer with no unit.
  • Explain how it separates: the phases, the different distances, the different spots.
  • Suggest how to show a substance is pure: run it in several solvents and look for one spot in each.
