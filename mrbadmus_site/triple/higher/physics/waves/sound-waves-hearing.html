Sound waves and hearing

A sound is only squeezes and stretches travelling through the air. How does your ear turn that into something you hear, and why are some sounds silent to you but not to a dog?

    A whistle that only the dog can hear.

    A trainer blows a dog whistle. Across the park, the dog's head snaps round. The trainer, with the whistle at her lips, hears nothing at all.

    Commit first

    Why does the trainer hear nothing?

    Sound in air is a longitudinal wave: a pattern of compressions, where the air is squeezed together, and rarefactions, where it is spread out. It needs particles to travel through, so it cannot cross a vacuum. To be heard, that pattern has to be turned into something your body can sense.     Turning sound waves into vibrations of a solid is not special to the ear. Any solid that sound reaches is pushed and pulled by the compressions and rarefactions, so it vibrates at the sound's frequency. It works the other way round too: a vibrating solid pushes and pulls on the air next to it and sends out sound waves.

    Which way is the conversion?

    Each card is a solid vibrating. Did sound waves make it vibrate, or is its vibration making sound waves?

    When sound passes into a new material, its frequency stays the same: each compression still arrives once per cycle, which is why the ear drum vibrates at the sound's frequency. The speed changes, so the wavelength changes with it. Sound usually travels fastest in solids and slowest in gases, because the particles in a solid are closer together and more strongly linked, so each vibration is passed on more quickly: about 340 m/s in air and 1500 m/s in water.

    Equations

    On the sheet

    v = f λ

    wave speed (v, metres per second) = frequency (f, hertz) × wavelength (λ, metres)

    λ = v ÷ f · f = v ÷ λ

    Units first

    Frequency must be in hertz. 1 kHz = 1000 Hz: multiply a kilohertz value by 1000 before you insert it.

    Key fact

    Hearing is a conversion: sound waves make the ear drum and the parts behind it vibrate. In a human ear, the conversion only works from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz.

    Key note · AQA 4.6.1.4 (8463)

    Sound waves and hearing

    1. 01Sound: longitudinal, mechanical, needs medium.
    2. 02Sound waves travelling through a solid make the solid vibrate.
    3. 03In the ear, sound waves make the ear drum and the parts behind it vibrate, causing the sensation of sound.
    4. 04The conversion only works over a limited range of frequencies. Human range: 20 Hz–20 kHz.
    5. 05Speed: usually solid > liquid > gas.
    6. 06Entering a new material, the frequency stays the same; the speed and wavelength change (v = f λ).
