Internal energy

Commit first

What does the thermometer do while the water keeps boiling?

The particles that make up a substance store energy in two ways. They move, so they have kinetic energy. There are forces between them, so they also have potential energy, which depends on how far apart they are. Internal energy is the total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles that make up a system. Heating a system increases its internal energy, and that does one of two things: it raises the temperature, or it changes the state. You keep heating. What happens next?

The temperature of a gas is related to the average kinetic energy of its particles: how energetic a typical particle is. Internal energy is the total over every particle, kinetic and potential. So a large object at a low temperature can hold far more internal energy than a small hot one. The two effects of heating each have an equation, both on the equation sheet, which the next lessons use: raising the temperature, ΔE = m c Δθ; changing state, E = m L.

Key note · AQA 4.3.2.1 (8463)

Internal energy

  1. 01Internal energy = KE + PE of all particles.
  2. 02Heating → increases internal energy → either raises temperature (KE) or causes change of state (PE).
  3. 03Temperature = average KE per particle.
  4. 04During change of state: temperature constant, PE increasing.
  5. 05Heating curve: slopes = temperature rise; flat sections = changes of state.

Mixing up temperature and internal energy?

The bench follows the GCSE model: warming raises only the particles' kinetic energy, and a change of state raises only their potential energy. Bar lengths and the time axis are not to scale, particles are drawn as spheres, and the water is at normal atmospheric pressure, so it boils at 100 °C. Both graphs are sketches, not measured data.
